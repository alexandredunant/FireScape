\noindent
European wildfire science relies heavily on tools, satellite products and management doctrines built for large summer fires in Mediterranean landscapes, and has consequently left the European Alps at its margin. That position is no longer tenable. Large fires in European mountains have shifted upslope by roughly 72\,m per decade since 2000, fire danger is projected to rise across the Alpine arc, and land abandonment has created continuous fuel on steep former pasture. In this review we synthesise the Alpine evidence across hazard, climate, water, forest, risk and governance, and set imported fire tools and doctrines against Alpine reality. Roughly 80\% of Alpine fires burn less than a hectare, below continental satellite thresholds, yet they act on fire-intolerant stands managed to hold rock, snow and sediment above settlements and transport corridors. Consequence is therefore decoupled from burnt area. Loss instead runs simultaneously through degraded protective structure, through ecological shifts of opposite sign across guilds and through a hydrogeomorphic cascade that lowers rainfall thresholds for debris flow, landslide and rockfall from several years to many decades. Because the primary asset at risk is a downslope protective service rather than timber, forest and agricultural policy rival climate in shaping outcomes. We show why valley-interpolated weather indices, separable spread formulations, linear fuel breaks, windthrow salvage conventions and hazard maps without exposure or vulnerability terms misfire on fragmented Alpine slopes. We then outline an interdisciplinary research agenda scaled to what the sparse Alpine observational record can realistically support.

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:}
European Alps; mountain wildfire; protection forest; post-fire cascades; model transferability; fuel management; drought and water availability; Earth observation; wildfire governance and economics; multi-hazard risk assessment

\noindent\textbf{Key Points}
\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}
\item Tools, satellite products and doctrines built for large summer wildfires misread risk in the small-fire, bimodal European Alps.
\item Consequence decouples from burnt area as fires remove protective forest and lower thresholds for slope hazards over decades.
\item Turning Alpine hazard maps into risk requires local calibration, downslope exposure data and post-fire institutional handovers.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent\textbf{Plain Language Summary}\\
Wildfires are becoming a growing concern in the European Alps as temperatures rise, snow cover shortens and forests expand across abandoned mountain pastures. However, most European wildfire monitoring systems, weather indices and prevention strategies were designed for large summer fires in Mediterranean regions. This interdisciplinary review brings together Alpine research across natural hazards, climate, hydrology, forest ecology, risk assessment and governance to test how well those imported tools fit Alpine conditions. Most Alpine fires burn less than one hectare and go undetected by continental satellites, yet even a small fire can destroy protective forests that shield valley towns, roads and railways from rockfall, avalanches and landslides. Fire also weakens mountain soils so that ordinary rainstorms can trigger debris flows, with recovery taking decades. Managing Alpine wildfire therefore requires locally adapted warning systems, agricultural and forest stewardship on steep slopes, and coordinated post-fire handovers across hazard agencies.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{An emerging fire frontier in the European Alps}

European fire science has largely been shaped by where burnt area is concentrated. Because the Mediterranean basin accounts for most of the continent's burning, it has also supplied most of its fire statistics, its operational danger rating and much of its prevention doctrine. This geographical bias matters because the distribution of burning is now changing. Globally, \citet{jones_global_2022} report that total burnt area declined by roughly a quarter between 2001 and 2019 as savanna burning contracted, while fire-weather extremity rose across most burnable regions and forest burnt area increased substantially in several extratropical ecoregions. A similar divergence is apparent in Europe, where burnt area has declined in the Mediterranean but increased in mid- and high-latitude regions in which fuel is abundant and plant communities have little adaptation to fire \citep{miller_compounding_2025}. The shift is, however, vertical as well as latitudinal. Across eight European mountain regions, \citet{beloiu_climatedriven_2026} found that the median elevation of fires burning at least 30\,ha above 800\,m rose from 910\,m in 2000--2014 to 1004\,m in 2015--2025, with the ninetieth percentile rising from 1353 to 1460\,m. This upslope shift of approximately 72\,m per decade appears to reflect a change after 2015 rather than a gradual trend, and burnt area in these regions was most strongly predicted by vapour pressure deficit.

Projections for the European Alps point in the same direction, and the projected increases are large relative to the historical baseline. Across the Alpine Space cooperation area (which spans the Alps and their surrounding foothills), \citet{gerberding_mapping_2025} project mean hazard probability to increase by 15.5\% under SSP1-2.6 and by 35.4\% under SSP5-8.5 by 2070--2100, with the largest relative increases in the northern and eastern sectors rather than in the already fire-affected south-west. For adjacent central Europe, the forced fire-danger signal is similarly projected to emerge before 2050 \citep{miller_climate_2024}. Taken together, these projections suggest that fire may become a routine hazard within the planning horizon of the Alpine institutions that would have to absorb it (Section~\ref{sec:climate}).

Climate alone, however, does not determine how much of this increase is realised. \citet{billing_future_2026} coupled two fire models, SPITFIRE and the Burnt Area Simulator for Europe, to the fire-enabled dynamic global vegetation model LPJmL. In their simulations, intensified fire weather alone is projected to raise annual burned area by around 39\% under SSP1-2.6 and by nearly 192\% under SSP3-7.0 by the end of the century, whereas continued improvement in management capacity would reduce burned area by 72\% to 92\% relative to scenarios without it. Even so, net increases would remain in roughly 55\% of Europe's fire-prone regions, and under the higher pathway the hotspots are concentrated in medium-elevation terrain such as the Alps, Pyrenees, Carpathians and Massif Central. We therefore treat management capacity as a primary determinant of Alpine outcomes rather than as a residual to be considered once the climate signal has been counted.

Emissions mitigation also shapes the outcome. According to the accounting of the European Forest Fire Information System (EFFIS), burnt area across Europe in 2025 was the largest since its records began in 2006, with the largest contributions coming from the Iberian Peninsula \citep{burton_state_2026}. The same assessment, which is still a preprint, finds that mitigation has already reduced projected end-of-century burnt area in extreme events in its focal regions, which include north-western Iberia: relative to a worst case now considered implausible, the reduction is 4\% to 23\% under a high pathway, with a further 5\% to 36\% under a Paris-consistent pathway. These results suggest that Alpine fire danger is likely to depend on mitigation as well as on adaptation, although it is rarely presented this way.

Fire is thus arriving in Alpine valleys where the science, the management system and the exposed population have uneven experience of it. This gap is not new to Alpine forestry. More than a decade ago, \citet{jandl_management_2013} reviewed the risks facing Alpine Space forests for the MANFRED consortium and observed that, because of a history of active suppression, fire risk had not received full attention in the Alps; they suggested that future climate conditions might well require an improved alerting system. Since then the climate signal has emerged, whereas operational integration across Alpine hazard domains has followed more slowly.

\subsection{Why imported fire tools and doctrines misread Alpine risk}

Steep temperate mountains remain poorly resolved in both coarse-grid fire classifications and disciplinary reviews, which encourages the uncritical import of tools built elsewhere. Global pyrogeographies, for instance, cluster satellite records into five pyromes at half-degree resolution \citep{archibald_defining_2013}, or into wild, tamed and domesticated macroregimes at one-degree resolution in which managed burning accounts for roughly nine tenths of active fires \citep{pereira_wild_2022}; neither includes an altitudinal or relief term. Similarly, biome-scale assessments of fire-regime change aggregate across broad terrestrial biomes rather than elevation belts \citep{sayedi_assessing_2024}. Recent syntheses examine global fire trends \citep{jones_global_2022} and elevation-dependent mountain warming \citep{pepin_climate_2022} separately, while specialised reviews treat mountain fire meteorology \citep{sharples_overview_2009}, wind- and slope-driven spread \citep{hayajneh_wind_2026}, early detection technologies \citep{mohapatra_early_2022}, machine-learning methods \citep{jain_review_2020} and wildfire effects on land surfaces \citep{or_review_2023} in isolation. In the Alps, however, these processes act as a single coupled chain, and imported instruments meet structural limits at each of its links.

These limits appear first in the atmosphere and in observation. Danger indices imported from continental or Mediterranean practice assume a continuous fire-weather field that can be interpolated from valley-floor stations or coarse reanalyses. This assumption breaks down where upper winds, foehn and inversions interact with Alpine relief to produce discontinuous and locally amplified surface conditions \citep{sharples_overview_2009}. Evidence imported from a single Tasmanian fire illustrates the point: \citet{ozaki_impact_2022} showed that wind profiles that enhance surface winds become several times more frequent as terrain grows more rugged, and that simulations driven by surface winds alone were outperformed by those that included upper-air winds (Section~\ref{sec:climate}). Satellite archives face a different problem, in that they miss the modal event altogether. Roughly four fifths of Alpine fires burn less than a hectare, whereas European burnt-area mapping operates reliably only above about 30\,ha \citep{muller_forest_2020, beloiu_climatedriven_2026}.

Relief also undermines the separable wind and slope corrections used in standard spread simulators. As reviewed by \citet{hayajneh_wind_2026}, controlled experiments show that rate of spread accelerates non-linearly with upslope angle as preheating switches from radiation to convection (the thresholds are given in Section~\ref{sec:hazard}). At a larger scale, \citet{conedera_linking_2024} showed that across 21 Swiss pyroregions the length of continuous forested upslope paths, combined with forest aggregation, explains between 56\% and 65\% of the variance in large-fire statistics without any weather term. Linear fuel breaks imported from gentler terrain face a similar geometric constraint, since they function primarily as platforms for ground crews, who often cannot reach or hold steep Alpine slopes.

The deepest mismatch, however, lies in how impact is defined once the fire is out. Much of the Alpine forest is designated to protect settlements and corridors against rockfall, snow avalanche and shallow landslide; in South Tyrol, for example, about 58\% of the forest area is classed as protection forest \citep{hillebrand_contrasting_2023}. Fire in fire-intolerant conifer and beech stands can remove this protective function for years to decades \citep{melzner_wildfire-induced_2025, melzner_brief_2019}. Because the asset at risk is typically not the burnt stand itself but the town, road or railway beneath it, burnt area (a metric that serves Mediterranean risk accounting reasonably well) says little about consequence in the Alps.

\subsection{Objective, baseline and structure of this review}

In this review we synthesise the interdisciplinary evidence on wildfire in the European Alps and evaluate where imported fire tools, metrics and management doctrines hold, where they require local recalibration and where Alpine reality breaks their underlying assumptions (Figure~\ref{fig:cascade}). Our aim is to establish the evidentiary basis for treating Alpine wildfire as a systemic multi-hazard risk rather than as a scaled-down forest-loss problem.

Our starting point is the EUSALP white paper of February 2020 \citep{muller_forest_2020}, which already provides the Alpine fire community with a policy consensus statement. It set out the state of knowledge for decision makers, established the arc-wide statistical baseline, identified the loss of protective function as the central concern and proposed a costed integrated fire management framework. We take that document as given and ask what the peer-reviewed record has established since. Because a policy white paper is not indexed or peer reviewed in the ordinary way, setting its positions against subsequent empirical work allows us to distinguish the elements that are now securely supported from those that require qualification and those that remain unmeasured.

Some recent work extends this baseline. Arc-wide arguments that the Alpine regime is structurally distinct have been quantified vertically, through the upslope migration of European mountain fires \citep{beloiu_climatedriven_2026}, and operationally, through terrain-conditioned estimates of containment difficulty \citep{cardil_assessing_2025}. Similarly, the recognition that Alpine fire management is substantially a forestry problem can now draw on monetised valuations of rockfall protection \citep{accastello_asforesee_2019, moos_climate_2021}, on a controlled Alpine prescribed-burning trial \citep{gamba_prescribed_2025} and on burn-severity conceptualisations of how long protective capacity remains compromised across forest types \citep{melzner_wildfire-induced_2025}.

Other findings, however, complicate the 2020 baseline, particularly with respect to post-fire consequence and risk. Consequence runs simultaneously through the direct loss of protective structure, through guild-specific ecological change and through a hydrogeomorphic cascade in which fire primes a slope that a subsequent storm destabilises. Part of this cascade may fall outside the return-period design logic of Alpine hazard engineering. In one burnt catchment of the Susa Valley, for example, \citet{laporta_postwildfire_2025} reported a debris flow triggered at a radar-estimated intensity of about 24\,mm\,h$^{-1}$, below the 30\,mm\,h$^{-1}$ typical of unburnt basins of its type, although radar accuracy is limited in such complex relief. As this rests on a single event, it shows that sub-threshold triggering can occur rather than how often it does. At the same time, operational assessment still stops at hazard surfaces, because arc-wide exposure and vulnerability layers have not yet been joined to them and the Alpine observational record remains too sparse to constrain parameter-heavy models.

We organise the review by the disciplinary domains in which the evidence is produced. Section~\ref{sec:hazard} examines wildfire as a natural hazard (ignition, spread, danger modelling and Earth observation), and Section~\ref{sec:climate} treats climatic controls, mountain wind systems, lightning and projections. We then follow the water pathway in Section~\ref{sec:water}, from physiological drought and soil alteration through debris flows to water supply, snow and ice, before turning in Section~\ref{sec:forest} to land-use-driven forest change, the recovery of protective function, silviculture and fuel treatments. Section~\ref{sec:risk} addresses the exposure and vulnerability terms needed to move from hazard to risk, and Section~\ref{sec:governance} examines suppression constraints, policy fragmentation and economics. In Section~\ref{sec:discussion} we bring these strands together, synthesising the mismatch between imported doctrines and Alpine reality across all six domains (Table~\ref{tab:contrast}) and outlining a research agenda scaled to the observational record.

\section{Review Design and Scope}
\label{sec:scope}

\subsection{Alpine scope, intra-Alpine regimes and external process benchmarks}

We take as our geographical scope the European Alps as delimited by the Alpine Convention (which spans Austria, France, Germany, Italy, Liechtenstein, Monaco, Slovenia and Switzerland), together with the overlapping EUSALP macro-regional perimeter used in arc-wide policy and practitioner studies. This is the boundary within which harmonised multi-national fire statistics were first compiled \citep{valese_wildfires_2011, muller_forest_2020}, and it has since framed both the climate-change hazard projections of \citet{gerberding_mapping_2025} and the joint survey of authorities, scientists and fire services in which \citet{hoang_stakeholders_2026} covered seven of its member states.

However, focusing on the Alpine arc does not imply a single uniform regime. Steep climatic, lithological and socio-economic gradients divide the range into distinct pyroregions whose seasonal timing, dominant ignition causes and management pressures diverge sharply \citep{conedera_characterizing_2018, wastl_recent_2012, muller_forest_2020}. Table~\ref{tab:regimes} summarises the four principal intra-Alpine regime settings that recur throughout Sections~\ref{sec:hazard} to~\ref{sec:governance}, and in doing so illustrates why a tool or prescription calibrated in one sector of the Alps is unlikely to generalise automatically across the arc.

Although our synthesis is strictly Alpine in scope, the Alpine observational record has major blind spots, in that several physical and socio-ecological links in Figure~\ref{fig:cascade} rest on a single documented Alpine event or have not yet been measured in the range at all. Where Alpine evidence is absent or rests on fewer than three cases, we therefore draw selectively on process studies from other mountain settings, such as post-fire debris-flow threshold recovery in the Rocky Mountains, fire--snowpack and glacier-albedo measurements in the Sierra Nevada and Canadian Rockies, or strategic fire management in the Pyrenees. We apply an explicit transfer rule to those studies. General physical mechanisms can demonstrate that a process operates and can bound the blind spot of an Alpine assessment, whereas empirical coefficients never transfer and Alpine parameters are taken from Alpine data alone. In contrast, broader European or global fire and hazard studies enter as methodological context where they define the imported tools and policies that are applied to the Alps.

\subsection{The six-step review framework and evidence weighting}

We assembled and reviewed the literature in six steps (Figure~\ref{fig:flow}), and Appendix~\ref{app:protocol} gives the numbers at each step together with the reasons for excluding publications.

\textbf{Step 1, starting documents.} We started from the 2020 EUSALP white paper on forest fires in the Alps \citep{muller_forest_2020}, which is the most recent synthesis for the whole Alpine arc and whose conclusions this review sets out to extend and test (Section~\ref{sec:intro}). Alongside it we used a mapping of forest fire hazard across the Alps under climate change scenarios, co-authored at Eurac Research \citep{gerberding_mapping_2025}, because it adds the projected, spatially explicit view of hazard that the white paper does not cover.

\textbf{Step 2, keyword search.} We then searched Google Scholar by hand, initially with the terms wildfire, mountain and Alps and subsequently with wildfire and mountains, so that process studies on links unmeasured in the Alps would not be missed. We kept publications that fell within one of the disciplines represented at Eurac Research (which are the disciplines of Sections~\ref{sec:hazard} to~\ref{sec:governance}), so that a specialist could later assess each of them. Together with the two starting documents, this search gave an initial set of 49 publications.

\textbf{Step 3, citation chaining.} We expanded this set through its citations in both directions. Working backward, we went through the reference lists of the white paper and of the initial publications, whereas working forward we looked up later publications that cite the core Alpine ones.

\textbf{Step 4, targeted additions.} While drafting the sections, we searched specifically for publications on topics that were covered poorly by the literature gathered so far.

\textbf{Step 5, screening.} We screened every publication against a four-level inclusion rule, which distinguishes direct fire evidence from the European Alps, external mountain process benchmarks addressing links thinly covered in the Alps, methodological or baseline context, and exclusion. Each decision was recorded with its reason, and every source cited in the review was read and summarised in a structured evidence note.

\textbf{Step 6, discipline review.} Each thematic section is reviewed by the co-authors from Eurac Research who work in that discipline. Climatologists review the climate section and forest scientists the forest section, while specialists in drought and water, natural hazards and risk, and economics and governance review the others. These reviewers check that their field is covered, that each source is read correctly and that the text gives it appropriate weight. Publications they add return to Step~3, so that their references are followed and screened in the same way as the rest of the literature.

Our synthesis is thematic rather than meta-analytic, because the evidence does not support pooling. Alpine fire studies differ in their definitions of severity, burnt-area thresholds, reporting periods and the administrative basis of their fire records, so their effect sizes are not comparable. In their global review of the neighbouring literature on land abandonment and disturbance, \citet{mantero_influence_2020} documented the same obstacle, with study sites clustering strongly and coverage across disturbance types remaining uneven.

Because much of the Alpine evidence consists of individual documented events, we weight it by an explicit convention. We use case evidence to show that a process operates, to describe its mechanism and to bound its magnitude in one setting, whereas we reserve regional parameters for multi-site evidence. We therefore read a single-case magnitude as evidence that the process can reach that value rather than as a central estimate, and several of the quantities cited in Sections~\ref{sec:water} and~\ref{sec:forest} are of this kind. Wherever a claim rests on fewer than three independent cases, we state their number, and where multiple independent Alpine studies support a claim, we say so. A mechanism established outside the Alps, in contrast, is marked as imported at the point of use and never supplies an Alpine parameter.

\section{Wildfire as a Natural Hazard}
\label{sec:hazard}

\subsection{Initiation: the Alpine ignition regime and its modelling}

An Alpine ignition model needs to reproduce the shape of the fire regime more than its magnitude. \citet{muller_forest_2020} report harmonised statistics for 2006--2017 that place annual fire numbers between 37 in Slovenia and 1043 in Italy, and annual burnt area between 64\,ha in Austria and 9984\,ha in Italy. Normalised by forest area, burnt area thus spans two orders of magnitude, from 0.22 to 44.13\,ha per 10{,}000\,ha of forest per year. Although roughly 80\% of Alpine fires burn less than one hectare and median fire size falls below 0.1\,ha in Austria, Germany and Switzerland, the distribution is strongly heavy-tailed. In the French and Italian Alps the mean fire covers about a hectare, yet the 10\% of fires exceeding ten hectares account for some 85\% of total burnt area. We consider both parts of this statistic important, because either one alone gives a misleading picture. The many small fires set what monitoring must detect and what suppression routinely handles, whereas the few large ones account for most of the burnt area and, in the Alps, are the events that can remove protective function across a whole slope. For comparison, \citet{senf_mapping_2021} found a mean patch size of 1.09\,ha for all forest disturbance detected across Europe over three decades, which places Alpine fire at the fine end of an already fine-grained disturbance regime.

Seasonality is bimodal, and we consider this the most consequential structural feature of the regime. A spring season of fast-spreading surface fires at low elevation peaks in March and April, whereas a summer season peaks in July and August. Winter fires are also documented \citep{valese_wildfires_2011}, particularly in the foehn regions north and south of the Alps, where a thermal belt forms above the fog line \citep{muller_forest_2020, schunk_forest_2013}. This pattern is not confined to the Alpine arc. In the Czech Republic, \citet{bercak_combination_2024} found the same bimodality, attributing an April peak to forestry operations resuming after winter and a July--August peak to heat, drought and tourism, with a threshold response above 18\,\degree C. Because the two modes have different controls, we see little reason to expect them to change in step.

Ignition causes in the Alps are mixed in a way that has no Mediterranean analogue. Human causes account for approximately 90\% of Alpine fires and lightning for around 10\% \citep{muller_forest_2020}. However, the lightning share reaches up to 30\% in some regions and seasons and up to half of summer fires in Austria and parts of Switzerland, with lightning fires concentrated on the southern side of the Alps and at higher elevation \citep{muller_forest_2020, valese_wildfires_2011, conedera_lightninginduced_2006}. Anthropogenic ignition, in turn, appears to follow the access network rather than population alone. At national scale in Austria, \citet{arndt_modeling_2013} identified railway, forest-road and hiking-trail density, together with agricultural and forestry activity, as the main predictors, which resolve to municipality-level danger classes that can be used to target prevention. However, the relationship with access is not linear. In the Valais between 1956 and 2006, \citet{zumbrunnen_human_2012} showed that fire occurrence rose slightly with road density up to about 30\,m per hectare and then fell sharply, probably because the densest networks mark urbanised or intensively farmed ground with little fuel and rapid suppression. Relief complicates the proxy further, because in steep terrain roads follow geomorphological constraints and settlements cluster on valley floors and terraces. As \citet{bekar_crossregional_2020} point out, distance from either therefore increases with altitude while temperature decreases, which makes any attribution of fire occurrence to such anthropogenic proxies uncertain.

Statistically, the two seasonal modes can also be separated, and different variables drive each. In an inner-Alpine valley (the Aosta Valley), \citet{vacchiano_modeling_2018} found that summer ignition responds to maximum temperature and to precipitation of the wettest month, whereas winter forest fire responds to precipitation and elevation, and that proximity to infrastructure matters in winter but not in summer. Negligence in agro-silvicultural activity accounts for around two-thirds of identified ignitions in their record, and the lightning share rose over the study period. Thus, a single annual model fitted to this system would average across processes with different drivers acting in different seasons.

Although the spring anthropogenic peak is not unique to the Alps, comparison with the neighbouring Carpathians shows why empirical predictors cannot simply be imported, even from another European mountain range. \citet{manczinger_fire_2025} modelled susceptibility across some 286{,}295\,km$^2$ of the Carpathians in seven countries, using 5{,}173 fire incidents between 2010 and 2020. Their model reproduces the sharp spring maximum, which there peaks in March, and identifies cropland and pasture as the leading predictors ahead of any climatic term. The elevation relationship, however, is inverted: in the Carpathians lower elevations are more fire-prone and steeper slopes are comparatively protected, which the authors attribute to easier human access on gentle ground. In the Alps, by contrast, 42\% of large fires recorded above 800\,m burned above 1{,}400\,m, even though the broader regime type has so far shown modest upslope expansion \citep{beloiu_climatedriven_2026}. We therefore expect that importing an empirical elevation relationship across ranges would invert the spatial pattern of susceptibility.

Year-to-year departures from this seasonal structure are large. In the Austrian record between 1993 and 2013, \citet{muller_anomalies_2015} identified three consecutive anomalous seasons falling in different parts of the calendar. Autumn 2011 had 47 fires and 27\,ha against a mean of 11 and 3, and spring 2012 had 184 fires (the highest spring count in the series) against a mean of 57, while summer 2013 had 107\,ha burnt against a mean of 22. Across the arc, the larger anomalies occur in the eastern Alpine countries (Austria, Slovenia and northeastern Italy) rather than in the west. With interannual variance this high, a short record cannot separate trend from fluctuation, and the same study reports that roughly half of Austrian fire records lack a burnt area at all.

Despite this interannual variability, \citet{conedera_characterizing_2018} showed that the regime divides into coherent regions, with Southern, Northern and Maritime clusters separating the high fire density of the southern slope from the substantially lower burnt-area fraction of the north. These clusters also differ in drought frequency and duration, vegetation type, mean elevation, valley orientation, population density and educational level. Because the classification rests on only ten years of record, we treat it as a baseline for detecting future regime shifts and anomalous seasons rather than as a description of a stable state. Its authors also conclude that, on present evidence, fire does not rival storms and bark beetles for damage in the Alpine region, although future regimes may evolve under climatic and socio-economic change. We adopt this framing, which is more measured than much of the recent literature, in this review.

Of the modelling domains treated here, ignition has matured fastest, most likely because ignition points are the only Alpine fire observation available in reasonable numbers. Skill is high where models are fitted locally, and parsimony does not appear to be an obstacle. In Trentino--South Tyrol, for example, \citet{moreno_spacetime_2025} found (in a preprint) that a small set of dynamic meteorological predictors, combined with static proxies for fuel, topography and human influence, was sufficient. In the Aosta Valley, static climatic, topographic and land-use predictors reached an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.90 to 0.95 \citep{vacchiano_modeling_2018}. Thresholds and danger classes, rather than raw probability surfaces, have been the practical outputs of this work \citep{moreno_spacetime_2025, arndt_modeling_2013}.

What such locally fitted models lose, however, is transferability. When \citet{gelabert_assessing_2025} pooled five European regions (none of them Alpine) into a single ignition model, skill fell at every site, by up to 0.10 in AUC relative to site-specific fitting, which directly measures the cost of treating heterogeneous fire environments as one. Yet this cost buys a stability under transfer that local fitting cannot provide. \citet{bekar_crossregional_2020} fitted occurrence models separately for southern Switzerland, Carinthia, Valencia and south-western Turkey and found that they transferred well only between regions with similar environmental conditions, whereas a model trained on all four performed better than the regional models when transferred. The pooled model also drew more heavily on climate, with climatic predictors contributing about 68\% of its explanatory power against a regional mean of 49\%. We therefore read pooling as a trade of local skill for stability beyond present conditions, which is what a climate scenario requires. In the same study, resolution mattered as much as pooling: a 100\,m grid outperformed a 1\,km grid in every region, and in Carinthia the contribution of forest variables rose from about 2\% to over 40\% once the small-scale forest mosaic could be resolved.

\subsection{Spread: fire behaviour and spread modelling}

For spread, the Alpine observational record is far less developed than the process literature, and observational evidence for anomalous, terrain-driven fire behaviour in the Alps currently rests on a single documented case. \citet{melzner_brief_2019} describe how, during the 2018 Hallstatt fire in Austria, vertical rock walls, anabatic winds and patchy vegetation made the fire jump upward and left a spotty burn pattern, with spatially variable intensity and correspondingly heterogeneous changes in soil and rock mass. This variability matters because it carries through into burn severity and from there into post-fire hazard. As this rests on one fire, it shows that such behaviour can occur rather than how often it does; even so, it suggests that terrain-driven complexity in fire behaviour is a risk-assessment problem and not only an operational one.

In contrast, the process literature supplies both mechanism and magnitude. As reviewed by \citet{hayajneh_wind_2026}, wind effects on spread are strongly non-linear: preheating shifts from radiation-led to convection-led near 1.5\,m\,s$^{-1}$, and in controlled fuels rate of spread increases by an order of magnitude by 3\,m\,s$^{-1}$. Discontinuous fuels show discrete ignition regimes, with no ignition below about 1\,m\,s$^{-1}$, direct-contact ignition between 1.5 and 2.5\,m\,s$^{-1}$ and spotting above 3\,m\,s$^{-1}$. Because Alpine valley winds routinely cross these thresholds within a diurnal cycle, we expect the regime transitions they imply to be ordinary conditions rather than exotic ones.

Coupled fire-atmosphere modelling can now resolve explicitly what the Alpine literature documents case by case, as shown for a European event outside the Alps. \citet{couto_numerical_2024} simulated the 2017 Pedr\'og\~ao Grande fire at 80\,m resolution and reproduced updraughts above 15\,m\,s$^{-1}$ and a convective column reaching roughly 10\,km, with the plume top rising from below 4\,km to over 9\,km and a localised downward outflow of similar magnitude developing ahead of the fire front. In their simulation, changes in wind direction triggered acceleration and merger of fire fronts. Although Alpine valleys generate precisely the terrain-forced circulations that such models are built to resolve, we found no equivalent coupled simulation published for an Alpine fire, and we regard this as the methodological gap between descriptive and mechanistic work on Alpine fire meteorology.

Spread modelling (unlike ignition) is the least mature Alpine domain, and the instruments in use rest on incompatible foundations. In the only systematic Alpine evaluation, \citet{malanchini_modeling_2025} compared two simulators on three Lombardy fires between 2017 and 2022 (at Tirano, Campolaro and Sonico): FlamMap, a deterministic simulator that propagates a surface and crown spread solution across a gridded landscape, and PROPAGATOR, a stochastic cellular automaton. Both reproduced the observed propagation but diverged substantially on fireline intensity and rate of spread, a divergence that follows from their differing assumptions rather than from calibration. The authors conclude that FlamMap suits planning because it resolves fine-scale interaction between fuel and topography, whereas PROPAGATOR suits operations because it is fast and accepts time-varying wind; no Alpine instrument currently does both. This disagreement on intensity matters, because intensity determines severity and therefore the whole post-fire cascade (Section~\ref{sec:water}).

Experimental evidence from laboratory benches, wind tunnels and field burns (none of it Alpine), as reviewed by \citet{hayajneh_wind_2026}, suggests why such disagreement is to be expected. Operational spread models apply wind and slope as separate correction factors and so miss the non-linear interaction between them; on laboratory benches, standard formulations under-predicted spread on steep slopes with wide fronts, and such models cannot represent eruptive acceleration in confined geometries without ad hoc parameterisation. We therefore expect them to perform worst in the steep terrain where Alpine fires burn. The same synthesis also shows where the separable treatment stops working \citep{hayajneh_wind_2026}. On slopes, preheating becomes convection-led beyond roughly 30\degree{}, as it does above the wind threshold given above. In pine-needle beds, rate of spread rises about two and a half times between 20\degree{} and 30\degree{}, while the distance over which convective heating acts expands from some 7\,cm at 20\degree{} to more than 130\,cm at 30\degree{}. Eruptive transition appears where slope exceeds 25\degree{} to 30\degree{} in a channel of aspect ratio at least 0.2, and there the convective influence distance grows from about 8\,cm at 25\degree{} to nearly 3\,m at 40\degree{}. Because empirical and analogue formulations prescribe the coupling of wind and slope rather than resolving it, they miss these transitions and degrade outside their calibration envelope (which is defined by steep slopes, extreme winds and discontinuous fuels).

Terrain also determines where fire does not go, and this side of the relationship has been quantified outside Europe. Across 912{,}000\,ha of the Canadian Rocky Mountains, \citet{rogeau_effect_2018} mapped 557 patches of persistent fire refugia covering 27.7\% of the forested area. They predicted these refugia from terrain and fuel, with elevation the leading variable at 23.7\% relative influence, followed by the proportion of non-fuel in the surrounding area at 20.3\%, solar radiation (as an aspect proxy) at 14.6\% and topographic position at 10.1\%. Skill, however, depends strongly on scale: the AUC rises from 0.71 for refugia of all sizes to 0.84 for patches under 10\,ha, so terrain predicts small unburnt patches far better than large ones, and the authors note that topography may matter less during large fire events. We consider this scale dependence favourable in a range whose fire-size distribution is dominated by small events, and the same geometric argument appears to support terrain-based prioritisation in the Alps.

Terrain-forced circulation of the kind that coupled models resolve is itself a driver of spread. In a high-resolution simulation of a 2023 fire in the Greater Khingan Mountains, \citet{wang_dynamic_2026} reproduced the observed burnt area above 800\,m with a spatial similarity of 0.82, while overestimating it in the valleys, and attributed the fastest phase of the fire to nocturnal down-valley winds of 3 to 5\,m\,s$^{-1}$. During that phase, maximum spread rate rose from 0.3 to 1.1\,m\,s$^{-1}$ and peak heat flux from 11 to 159\,kW\,m$^{-2}$, before both fell back as daytime up-valley winds returned. Although the setting is Chinese boreal rather than Alpine and the coefficients do not transfer, the mechanism is the diurnal valley circulation that operates in every Alpine valley. This implies that the hours of fastest Alpine spread may be the hours when crews are least able to work.

Slope also governs a mode of spread that perimeter-based evaluation does not capture. Alpine lightning fires are reported as harder to extinguish because they burn below the surface, and the controlled smouldering experiments of \citet{shan_influence_2024} show that slope accelerates this mode of spread as well. Between level ground and a twenty-degree slope, peak temperature rose from 769 to 948\degree{}C and vertical and horizontal spread rates rose by roughly 75 to 90\%, with the slope effect on temperature exceeding its effect on rate. Because the experiments are Chinese boreal rather than Alpine, the coefficients do not transfer, but the direction of the effect plausibly does. This bears on the mop-up phase, which takes most of the effort in an Alpine incident.

Severity itself appears only partly predictable from terrain. Across 113 large Italian fires, \citet{malandra_burn_2022} found pre-fire land cover to be the strongest predictor of burn severity (with conifer stands highest), followed by elevation, whereas slope contributed weakly; distance to roads and railways entered positively, which suggests higher uncontrolled severity in remote terrain. We therefore read fuel type as the leading control and access as a modulating one, whereas slope, so dominant for spread, appears secondary for severity.

\subsection{Fire danger and hazard modelling}

As reviewed by \citet{zacharakis_environmental_2023}, the Canadian Fire Weather Index (FWI) is preferred in the Mediterranean countries of Europe, and when it is used outside Canada its danger classes are redefined by local calibration. Their review covered 63 danger systems and indices from 16 countries, few of which represent terrain at all beyond coarse slope classes or pressure-level adjustments. The Italian operational system (RISICO) is the clearest exception, and it contradicts the view that European danger rating ignores fuel and terrain. As described by \citet{perello_fuelaware_2025}, RISICO computes dead fuel moisture content, rate of spread and fireline intensity hourly on a one-kilometre grid, taking its fuel term from a susceptibility classification that splits the territory into twelve classes by combining vegetation type with a susceptibility level in which topography is already embedded. The underlying susceptibility model was validated against 102{,}904 wildfires over sixteen years and compared with twelve established indices; it reaches an AUC of 0.89 at European level, and larger fires concentrate in its highest susceptibility quartile. Its authors also state its limits: live fuel moisture is held at a fixed worst case rather than varying, and because of the one-kilometre grid the indices were evaluated only against fires larger than 70\,ha, which is above the size of almost every Alpine fire.

For one Alpine country, Austria, the size of the recalibration problem has been measured directly. \citet{arpaci_selecting_2013} tested twenty-two fire weather indices and two raw meteorological variables against 1{,}331 Austrian fire records from 1993 to 2007, using 26 stations that span 22 ecoregions from colline to alpine zones, and found that no single index performed best across regions or seasons. In summer, mean daily temperature and the Canadian Build-Up Index (BUI) were selected most often, with the BUI dominating once the analysis was restricted to fires above 1{,}000\,m$^2$. In winter, by contrast, M68dwd (a central European index of a different lineage) was selected at 14 of 25 stations for all fires and at 17 for fires above 100\,m$^2$. We therefore read index choice in this range as depending on season and ecoregion at the same time, which goes further than the usual observation that indices need local class boundaries. The same analysis reports that 42\% of the fire records lacked a size, a gap that bears directly on the observational problem discussed below (Section~\ref{sec:data}).

Alpine practice has responded to this dependence through local recalibration in some places and through integration in others. In Canton Ticino, a locally fitted danger index was developed with cantonal funding through the niche-modelling approach of \citet{de_angelis_modelling_2015}, who found that meteorological variables alone matched or outperformed the full FWI but that the resulting index was not transferable to other regions. They also concluded that model selection must be revisited when legislation or socio-economic conditions change the pyrological environment, and showed that usable indices can be built from small numbers of fires, which matters in regions with sparse records. In contrast, Austria chose integration. The system presented by \citet{muller_towards_2020} combines daily fire weather with human-ignition hazard, lightning hazard, a high-resolution fuel type map and topography, and in validation some integrated variants outperformed conventional weather indices for predicting ignitions and performed better on false alarms, although correlation with burnt area remained low across all variants. Adding physically motivated layers thus improved prediction of \emph{whether} a fire starts without improving prediction of \emph{how large} it becomes.

Taken together, these results suggest that the gap lies somewhere other than where it is usually assumed to be. Because a locally fitted weather-only model can perform well, the gap does not appear to be mainly one of variables; rather, it is a lack of local calibration data and of information on dynamic fuel condition. Operational danger systems also almost entirely omit terrain, even though terrain demonstrably controls the surface wind field \citep{ozaki_impact_2022, sharples_overview_2009}. On the fuel side the gap is literal in Austria, where no fuel models for predicting fire behaviour exist and behaviour modelling relies mainly on North American models. The Swiss models that do exist were developed in national parks and carry higher fine-fuel loads than the managed Austrian forests measured since \citep{neumann_fuel_2022}.

A way beyond static fuel representation already exists in the Alpine literature, but it has not been carried forward. Simulations for the Swiss Alps with the dynamic landscape vegetation model LandClim treated fire as an emergent property that fed back on vegetation \citep{schumacher_relative_2006, colombaroli_species_2010}, and \citet{colombaroli_species_2010} also combined palaeoecological reconstruction with dynamic simulation to constrain species response to fire and temperature. More recently, forest-dynamics models linked to protection indices \citep{hillebrand_contrasting_2023} or coupled to rockfall simulation \citep{moos_climate_2021} have quantified changes (often losses) in protective function under climate scenarios. On the evidence assembled here, we regard reconnecting this tradition to fire hazard assessment as the most valuable methodological opportunity in Alpine fire science.

\subsection{Data, records and Earth observation}
\label{sec:data}

Alpine fire science depends on records that differ in resolution by orders of magnitude, and we describe them here from the coarsest to the finest. Continental burnt-area products derived from coarse optical sensors map burnt pixels at 250\,m, which are aggregated to grids of roughly 9\,km for European fire modelling \citep{forrest_understanding_2024}. The European Forest Fire Information System (EFFIS) maps individual perimeters and is the source of harmonised European statistics, but its mapping is effective only from about 30\,ha upward \citep{beloiu_climatedriven_2026}. Medium-resolution optical archives support stand-scale disturbance mapping and have been used, for instance by \citet{miguel_unravelling_2026}, to classify fire, wind and bark beetle events across European forests since 1985 (Section~\ref{sec:forest}). National fire databases, in contrast, record events far below any satellite threshold, including an Austrian national register holding several thousand historical and current forest fires \citep{papathomakohle_towards_2025}.

We regard the mismatch between these records as the main methodological problem in Alpine fire science. Mapped fires account for approximately 95\% of the burnt area that European countries report, but for only a fraction of the number of fires \citep{jrc_advance_2026}. A gap of the same order has been quantified in another European mountain setting, where \citet{bakke_datadriven_2023} built a danger model for Fennoscandia from 444{,}030 monthly grid observations containing 1{,}439 satellite-detected fire occurrences, that is 0.3\% of the record. For 2017 and 2018, Norwegian national fire records held about 800 fire data points where the satellite product held 24, and a model trained on the national data performed considerably better. Satellite products systematically underestimate burnt area wherever small fires dominate, because coarse burned-area algorithms omit many events below roughly 500\,m resolution unless combined with active-fire detections \citep{jones_global_2022, bakke_datadriven_2023}. In an Alpine country the result is a stark asymmetry, with four fires mapped in Austria in 2025 against a national record running to the low hundreds annually \citep{jrc_advance_2026, papathomakohle_towards_2025}. Statements about the European fire regime derived from satellite mapping are therefore statements about area, whereas statements about the Alpine regime from the same source describe only its largest events.

Earth observation also supplies the severity products on which post-fire assessment depends, and here the Alpine case differs most from standard practice. Remotely sensed severity maps are commonly used to locate where the effects of fire on soil are likely to be greatest \citep{negri_wildfire_2026}, and in the Italian analysis described above, severity estimated in this way was related successfully to land cover, elevation, population density and distance to roads and railways \citep{malandra_burn_2022}. However, the proxy appears to fail where it matters most for mountains. Three years after a single Alpine mixed-severity fire, \citet{negri_wildfire_2026} found that the burnt soils most water-repellent in laboratory tests lay mostly in low-severity plots, whereas in the field the unburnt soils were the most repellent. Severity mapped from satellite and field data was thus decoupled from the soil condition it is meant to represent. Although this rests on a single fire, it suggests that the geomorphic cascade (Section~\ref{sec:water}) cannot currently be initialised from the product that operational practice would normally use.

Terrain narrows the Alpine observational window further. Steep, deeply incised relief produces illumination contrasts and cast shadow that vary with season and aspect, while seasonal snow and persistent cloud occupy part of the usable window, so that a difference computed between two dates confounds the change caused by fire with the change caused by solar geometry and plant phenology. The bias that results from imperfect seasonal timing has been measured in the Alps by \citet{morresi_mapping_2022}, who mapped ten fires in the mountains of Piedmont following the drought of autumn 2017. Severity maps built from phenologically matched reflectance composites, rather than from a single pair of cloud-free images, raised overall classification accuracy by 4.4 to 5.2 percentage points, reaching 83.7\% at best, although the gain fell to 2.8 to 3.2 points when the paired-image indices were calibrated. The composites also increased the extent classified as high severity by roughly 19\%, because suboptimal seasonal timing reduces the contrast between healthy and burnt surfaces and so understates what burned. It follows that an Alpine severity map produced without controlling for seasonal timing is likely to understate high severity, by a margin large enough to matter wherever severity class is used as an input rather than as a description.

National registers also hold variables that no satellite product contains, including the number of action forces, helicopters and brigades committed to each event \citep{muller_forest_2020}. Although they are therefore the only source from which Alpine suppression effectiveness could be estimated, they are not currently used for that purpose. The same report notes that detection technology faces its own version of the terrain problem \citep{muller_forest_2020}, and the wider review of early-detection systems by \citet{mohapatra_early_2022} shows that each of the four main technology families meets a distinct constraint in complex relief. Satellites trade spatial resolution against multi-hour revisit intervals, whereas stationary camera towers, although they measurably reduce burnt area over flat ground, are fixed in place, occluded by ridges and constrained by power supply and communications tethering \citep{muller_forest_2020, mohapatra_early_2022}. Uncrewed aerial vehicles (UAVs) avoid fixed sightlines, but high power demand prevents continuous patrol, and Alpine deployment is further limited by wind sensitivity and dense canopy \citep{muller_forest_2020, mohapatra_early_2022}. Ground-based wireless sensor nodes, for their part, are difficult to deploy and maintain in remote, rugged terrain, risk destruction during a burn and depend on line-of-sight radio links unless relayed across multi-hop meshes or aerial platforms \citep{mohapatra_early_2022}.

Validation practice in Alpine and adjacent fire modelling has improved and is now reasonably rigorous by the standards of the field. For Italy, \citet{trucchia_defining_2022} combined spatial cross-validation in national susceptibility mapping with an out-of-sample operational test against a severe subsequent season, and Alpine ignition modelling has used multi-perspective validation that explicitly accounts for spatial and temporal structure \citep{moreno_spacetime_2025}. Negative results have also been reported alongside positive ones, as in the Austrian finding that integration improves ignition prediction but not burnt-area correlation \citep{muller_towards_2020}. We therefore see the remaining problem as one of statistical power rather than rigour: in a region with few large fires, the events that dominate risk are the events on which models can least be tested.

Seasonal stratification is one of the few design choices that demonstrably helps, because it avoids averaging across the two seasonal modes described above, and the Italian national susceptibility mapping accordingly produced separate winter and summer surfaces \citep{trucchia_defining_2022}. Similarly, in their analysis of mountain wildfire in south-west China, \citet{wang_seasonal_2023} found that different drivers dominate in fire and non-fire seasons and that including the seasonal dimension improved understanding of lagged effects between drivers and occurrence. We would expect the same logic to apply more strongly to the Alps. In an inner-Alpine valley, the seasonal contrast is not only between active and quiet periods but between climate-driven summer fires and anthropogenic winter fires \citep{vacchiano_modeling_2018}, and different mechanisms also appear to drive the two fire seasons in the Czech Republic \citep{bercak_combination_2024}.

Underlying all of these records is the observational threshold itself, which we regard as the most basic constraint. Its magnitude has been measured directly, although not in the Alps: for the Netherlands, \citet{stoof_temperate_2024} found that the European reference dataset captured 0.2\% of recorded wildfires and 45\% of burnt area. There is little reason to expect better performance for an Alpine fire population that is smaller still and observed through terrain that obstructs detection. The Alpine fire-size distribution described above sits almost entirely below the thresholds at which the defining analyses operate, namely the burnt-area mapping threshold given at the start of this subsection \citep{beloiu_climatedriven_2026} and the 1\,ha minimum fire size adopted by a recent five-region European ignition model \citep{gelabert_assessing_2025}, while continental burnt-area products are coarser still. The consequences run in opposite directions and are easily confused. Detected trends in large Alpine fires are trends in the tail of the distribution and may not represent the regime; conversely, an absence of detected change may simply reflect that most of the regime is invisible to the detector. In either case, we conclude that Alpine regime detection requires purpose-built records at sub-hectare resolution rather than the reuse of continental products.

Across the modelling literature reviewed above, the models in current use are narrower than the process they represent, and consistently so in one direction. Weather-only danger indices omit vegetation, topography, lightning and human influence \citep{muller_towards_2020}; they are also tuned to the region in which they were developed and do not transfer directly \citep{de_angelis_modelling_2015}, while a pooled ignition model lost skill when evaluated at individual sites \citep{gelabert_assessing_2025}. Static fuel maps cannot represent a bimodal seasonal fuel bed, nor one that changes over decades, as Alpine forest cover and wood volume have \citep{bebi_changes_2017} and as the treeline has in one Central Alpine valley \citep{frei_evidence_2023}. At the Alpine scale, the scenario model of \citet{gerberding_mapping_2025} does not consider extreme events and the disturbance-driven fuel loading they favour, nor land-use change driven by social, economic and governance shifts. Continental burnt-area models are trained on satellite burnt-area products that miss many small fires \citep{forrest_understanding_2024}, most fire-enabled global vegetation models omit topographic effects on spread \citep{jones_global_2022}, and single-hazard susceptibility mapping cannot, by construction, represent the cascade \citep{trucchia_defining_2022}. Because each omission appears to remove a process that adds to hazard rather than reducing it, we suggest that the resulting estimates be read as lower bounds.

Widening the models would be the obvious remedy, but the Alpine record cannot support this. The harmonised regional record covers only the ten years from 2000 to 2009 \citep{conedera_characterizing_2018}, and most Alpine fires fall below the observational thresholds discussed above. Even the analysis of \citet{beloiu_climatedriven_2026} that establishes upslope migration excludes fires below the mapping threshold, while conceding that most historical Alpine fires are smaller than that. Post-fire geohydrological observation amounts to a first inventory with no long-term monitoring \citep{melzner_wildfire-induced_2025}. Documented post-fire debris flows in the western Italian Alps number two \citep{tiranti_wildfires_2021}, and systematic Alpine spread-model evaluation rests on three events \citep{malanchini_modeling_2025}. In their attribution study of the record 2025 European fire season, \citet{keeping_attribution_2026} exclude the Alps and central Europe altogether, as does the annual global assessment for the same season (still a preprint), whose three focal regions are Canadian, Iberian and Chilean \citep{burton_state_2026}.

Adding parameters to fit a record this thin would likely produce models that describe the observations they were fitted to and little else. The scoping review of 300 wildfire machine-learning studies by \citet{jain_review_2020} reinforces this caution. As that review documents, data-driven methods require large, consistent datasets rarely available at multi-decadal or fine spread scales, extrapolate uncertainly under climate change without physical domain constraints, and rarely reach operational agency use \citep{jain_review_2020}. We therefore take this observational constraint, more than any limitation of method, to define what an Alpine modelling programme can realistically attempt.

Even so, the Alpine literature shows a way forward, which is to widen scope through better-chosen structure rather than through more free parameters. A single upslope path-length metric, computed from a digital elevation model and a forest map, accounts for 56 to 65\% of the variation in Swiss large-fire statistics \citep{conedera_linking_2024}, and a parsimonious additive model with a representative sampling design and threshold-based output achieves high skill on Alpine ignition \citep{moreno_spacetime_2025}. In Ticino, the locally fitted, low-dimensional index of \citet{de_angelis_modelling_2015} matched the transferred FWI for two fire regimes and outperformed it for summer lightning fires. Even for post-fire debris flow, \citet{tiranti_wildfires_2021} proposed, in a preliminary study of two events, modifying catchment runoff parameters inside an existing warning system rather than building a coupled model.

On this reading, transferability appears selective rather than uniformly poor, and we consider the distinction a practical one: empirically tuned weather indices do not transfer, whereas physically grounded process models often do. \citet{diakakis_exploring_2023} illustrate this with the empirical post-fire debris-flow likelihood model used operationally in the western United States, which combines the proportion of moderate and high soil burn severity on gradients steeper than 23\degree{}, mean differenced normalised burn ratio (dNBR) and soil erodibility, each scaled by peak fifteen-minute rainfall. Running on European soil datasets, the model reproduced observed debris flows in a Mediterranean post-fire setting with an area under the ROC curve of 0.913. Similarly, the Initial Attack Assessment index of \citet{cardil_assessing_2025} combines simulated fire behaviour with a terrain sub-index of accessibility, penetrability, line-construction difficulty and slope; it was calibrated on nearly 27{,}000 Californian fires and has so far been demonstrated on only six fires elsewhere, one of them in Spain \citep{cardil_assessing_2025}. We therefore suggest that the operational rule the Alpine evidence supports is to import the mechanism and recalibrate the coefficients.

\section{Climate, Weather and Projections}
\label{sec:climate}

\subsection{Climatic controls on Alpine fire activity}

Alpine fire climate varies with elevation, between the moist northern and the insubric southern slopes, and between the dry continental inner valleys and the wetter outer ranges. Of these gradients, elevation currently sets the main constraint, through snow cover and temperature-limited fuel drying, and it is precisely this constraint that warming is relaxing. Whether warming is itself amplified with altitude is less clear. As synthesised by \citet{pepin_climate_2022}, mountain observations show no universal amplification of warming with altitude when datasets are pooled worldwide, although paired adjacent stations more often record faster warming at higher elevations, particularly near the retreating snowline where snow-albedo feedback operates \citep{rangwala_climate_2012, pepin_climate_2022}. Across midlatitude ranges, mountain precipitation increases have also been weaker than those in the adjacent lowlands, which has reduced the elevational precipitation gradient \citep{pepin_climate_2022}. In the European Alps, snow-cover duration and snow depth have declined strongly below 2{,}000\,m in recent decades, and roughly half of glacier area had already been lost by 2000 \citep{pepin_climate_2022}. At the scale of a single Italian Alpine district, \citet{pede_mountain_2022} report that warming and drying have already cut ski provision in the lower valleys, and they project that shorter snow cover will put high-altitude municipalities most at risk by 2021--2050.

Fuel moisture, rather than temperature, appears to be the direct link between climate and fire. Across European climate regions, multi-month drought indices, soil moisture deficit and vapour pressure deficit are the strongest and most consistent drivers of fire activity, although their interactions are region- and season-specific. \citet{miller_compounding_2025} found that productivity deficits matter in southern Europe in summer, whereas in northern Europe summer preconditions combine moisture deficit with productivity \emph{surpluses}, so that fuel production and fuel drying compound rather than trade off. Fuel moisture also shapes ignition: in a five-site European model of human-caused ignition, \citet{gelabert_assessing_2025} found that the annual anomaly in dead fine fuel moisture content ranked first in the pooled model and at the Swedish and central European sites, but lower at the three Mediterranean sites. Similarly, vapour pressure deficit is the strongest predictor of burnt area in European mountains \citep{beloiu_climatedriven_2026}.

Drought is not a single quantity, and the Alpine spring and summer fire seasons plausibly respond to different forms of it. In their analysis of extreme fires in south-eastern France, \citet{ruffault_extreme_2018} separated press drought, in which a long-lasting water deficit from reduced precipitation and high evapotranspiration intensifies wind-driven fires, from hot drought, in which a comparable deficit combines with a summer heatwave to intensify heat-induced fires. Alpine spring fires (carried by cured herbaceous fuel under foehn) match press drought in mechanism, whereas the emerging high-elevation fires, which across eight European mountain regions occur under extreme atmospheric aridity above 1400\,m \citep{beloiu_climatedriven_2026}, plausibly match hot drought. We therefore expect that a danger system calibrated on one form will not represent the other, and that a projection reporting a single aggregate trend would hide which of the two is changing.

Alpine-specific trend evidence comes from 25 long-term stations in six Alpine countries, grouped into climatically homogeneous regions. Mean annual fire danger (taken as the median of seven indices) rose clearly and significantly south and west of the Alps, whereas north of the Alps and in the inner valleys a high proportion of trends were not statistically significant. At the 95th percentile, however, \citet{wastl_recent_2012} found a trend towards higher index values at almost every station. These results suggest that warming is raising the extreme tail of Alpine fire danger even where the mean has not changed detectably, a pattern that monitoring designed around averages is likely to miss.

Rising danger is not the same as a changing fire regime, and the Alps offer much evidence for the former and little for the latter. Across Europe, \citet{venalainen_temporal_2014} found no clear trend in fire danger between 1960 and 1999 but a significant increase in southern and eastern Europe from 1980 onward, and at the national level in Greece, Spain and Finland the correlations between the mean seasonal Fire Weather Index and burnt area were of order 0.6. However, regime change is harder to detect in the Alps, because records are short and administratively fragmented, fires are small enough that recording thresholds dominate the statistics, and the strong seasonal and topographic structure lets opposing changes cancel out in regional aggregates. We therefore treat the lack of a detected signal as weak evidence that nothing has changed.

A long-term record from the driest Alpine valleys supports this caution. \citet{zumbrunnen_reconstructing_2010} reconstructed the fire history of Valais (a dry continental valley of the Swiss Alps) as the joint product of climatic, vegetation and anthropogenic factors, with regime shifts across the twentieth century in both Valais and Ticino. When some 900 of those fires were related to human and climatic proxies, the controls were found to change with the socio-economic regime. Between 1904 and 1955, population density was the strongest predictor, followed by wood harvest, and both forest pasturing and wood harvest were associated with fewer fires. Between 1956 and 2006, however, after the post-war turn from an agricultural to an industrial and service economy, temperature became the leading predictor, which \citet{zumbrunnen_human_2012} attribute to fire activity concentrating at lower elevations as farming and forestry withdrew from middle and high ground. Because both models explain only about a third of the variance, we read the change as a shift of emphasis rather than as one driver replacing another. Thus, Alpine fire regimes have shifted before under human forcing, and even the Valais temperature signal emerged through land use, which in our view cautions against attributing any present-day shift to climate by default.

\subsection{Mountain meteorology: wind systems, foehn and the thermal belt}

Synoptic control on Alpine fire danger has been characterised directly by combining a sixty-year weather-type classification with station data, danger indices and observed fires across the Alps. Elevated danger occurred mainly under persistent anticyclonic situations or in the lee of the range under katabatic foehn, and the southern Alpine region was the most fire-prone. \citet{wastl_largescale_2013} also found that differences between weather types narrowed for indices representing deeper soil layers, which reflects their longer drying response times. We therefore regard foehn as one of the two synoptic configurations that generate Alpine fire danger rather than as an incidental local phenomenon.

Below the synoptic scale, Alpine wind systems are, in our reading, what turns small ignitions into fast fires. Foehn can remove tens of percentage points of relative humidity within hours and is associated with the late-winter and spring fires of inner and southern valleys. Diurnal thermal circulations, in contrast, reverse between anabatic flow by day and katabatic flow by night, which imposes a daily cycle in spread direction that once-daily synoptic station grids cannot resolve, while relief also channels the synoptic flow along valley axes and through cols. Steep Alpine topography controls each of these regimes, and through them fire type, intensity and burn severity \citep{melzner_wildfire-induced_2025}.

Relief also breaks up the fire-weather field. Where upper winds meet complex terrain, surface conditions become discontinuous and locally amplified, so that a field interpolated from valley-floor stations loses validity \citep{sharples_overview_2009}. In one Tasmanian fire (an imported case, divided into three burnt sectors), atmospheric profiles capable of generating enhanced surface winds occurred in 6.5\% of time steps over the least rugged sector but in 40.2\% over the most rugged. Fire simulations driven by surface winds alone performed well on the gentlest terrain, whereas on rugged terrain \citet{ozaki_impact_2022} found them outperformed by simulations that included upper-air winds.

Temperature inversions add a less obvious structure, and the thermal belt is a recognised feature of the Alpine fire calendar. In foehn regions north and south of the range, winter fires occur because inversion places a sunny, dry belt above the fog line \citep{muller_forest_2020}. During the autumn 2011 drought in the Bavarian Alps, fire danger and fire incidents concentrated at mid-slope elevations in the thermal belt, while valley stations registered only moderate danger. As \citet{schunk_forest_2013} explain, the diurnal cycles of the controlling meteorological variables differ between valley floor and slope under inversion, and once-daily station observation cannot represent that difference. The same study recommends additional high-elevation stations, or high-resolution meteorological models feeding indices that accept at least hourly input. This remedy has not, however, been generally implemented in the Alpine danger-rating systems that we review in Section~\ref{sec:hazard}.

\subsection{Lightning, convection and the summer ignition mode}

In Alpine-scale statistical mapping, \citet{gerberding_mapping_2025} found lightning frequency to be one of the three strongest predictors of fire hazard, alongside mean annual temperature and precipitation seasonality, and we would not expect a Mediterranean hazard model to rank an ignition source so highly. Multi-decadal records describe the lightning regime across Valais, the Grisons, Valle d'Aosta and Ticino. Lightning fires there occur from May to October, with 78\% of events in July and August, and compared with anthropogenic fires they ignite at higher elevation and on steeper relief and are harder to extinguish because they burn underground. \citet{conedera_lightninginduced_2006} further report that in hot, dry summers such as 2003 they became markedly more frequent, with significantly greater burnt area and higher suppression costs. Lightning ignition is itself structured by terrain, through elevation-dependent cloud-to-ground discharge frequency, altitudinal fuel belts and strong vertical gradients in precipitation, as \citet{baranovskiy_deterministic_2021} describe for mountain forests in southern Siberia. \citet{muller_towards_2020} reflect this in an Austrian integrated danger-assessment prototype, which classifies lightning fire hazard jointly by elevation, slope, aspect and vegetation type, derived from the locations of past lightning fires. The compounding question is whether ignition potential and fuel receptiveness will rise together. In Croatia (which the Dinaric Alps cross), lightning ignited only 3.4\% of fires over six years, yet these burned on average twice the area of all fires, and some burned beneath transmission lines. According to \citet{bozicek_impact_2023}, summer fire weather there is projected to reach very high or extreme danger classes by the end of the century, with more thunderstorms considered possible.

\subsection{Climate projections and what they omit}

Scenario studies for the Alps span statistical hazard mapping and fire-weather projection, and in our view the limitations the two share say more than their individual results. Hazard mapping under shared socio-economic pathways yields the increases reported in Section~\ref{sec:intro}, with \citet{gerberding_mapping_2025} finding the largest relative changes in the northern and eastern sectors. Fire-weather projection for the heterogeneous landscapes of adjacent central Europe, using a regional large ensemble under RCP8.5, places the emergence of the forced fire-danger signal before 2050 in every subregion examined; in the same ensemble, \citet{miller_climate_2024} found present-day hundred-year fire weather shifting to a ten-year recurrence by 2090.

These shared limitations are, in our view, structural rather than incidental. Neither approach represents the disturbance-driven fuel loading by which drought, storm and bark beetle outbreak increase flammable material, nor the socio-economic and governance change that affects land use, and both state openly that local application carries far more uncertainty than the regional trend \citep{gerberding_mapping_2025}. Even atmospheric reanalyses at quarter-degree resolution (roughly 20\,km in the Alps, comparable to the ridge-to-valley spacing) smooth peaks and valleys and carry substantial winter errors in complex relief \citep{pepin_climate_2022}. Continental fire models show a related limit, since \citet{forrest_understanding_2024} report that the Burnt Area Simulator for Europe explains 32--36\% of deviance overall and does not reproduce fire occurrence in temperate and boreal Europe. Its skill in mountains is not evaluated separately, which we regard as a gap in itself. We therefore read a projection driven by climate normals as describing the climatic envelope rather than the fire regime.

Anticipation faces a further limit, although the evidence for it is indirect. A global analysis found that thirteen climate teleconnections together modulate around half of global burnt area, although these relationships are markedly weaker in Europe than on other continents. \citet{cardil_climate_2023} also found the European associations spread across several modes, including the North Atlantic Oscillation and the East Atlantic pattern in temperate Europe and Pacific and Indian Ocean modes in Mediterranean and temperate regions. Seasonal fire forecasting from remote climate modes is therefore likely to deliver less in the Alps than where a single mode dominates, which would put more weight on monitoring the land surface and the state of the fuel.

\section{Water, Drought and Water Use}
\label{sec:water}

\subsection{Drought, fuel moisture and the physiological chain}

Drought reaches fire through a chain of separable links, of which the fire literature has mostly studied the one closest to fire (fuel moisture), while the forest health literature has studied the rest. Fuel moisture is well established as a driver but poorly modelled. In a global evaluation of 1{,}603 measurements from 153 experiments in 21 countries, \citet{rodrigues_vpdbased_2024} found that vapour-pressure-deficit models performed competitively over the full moisture range (with mean absolute errors near 4\% and coefficients of determination around 0.4) and that machine-learning alternatives improved on them only marginally, whereas below 10\% moisture no approach exceeded a coefficient of determination of 0.16. This is, however, the range in which spread responds exponentially to dryness. Even so, fuel moisture and its atmospheric drivers remain the strongest predictors of ignition and burnt area (Section~\ref{sec:climate}).

Further back in the chain, drought acts physiologically and turns living trees into standing dead fuel. In Scots pine at the dry margins of its range (which include the inner Alpine valleys), carbon starvation may be the main route to drought-induced mortality, although evidence that reserves are actually exhausted is contested. \citet{giuggiola_droughtinduced_2010} argue that reduced assimilation under water stress would leave fewer resources for defensive compounds, so that pests and pathogens could amplify the effect of drought. A sapling experiment outside the Alps isolates this interaction. In Norway spruce, drought and root-rot infection compete for the same carbon pool, which must fund both compartmentalising the pathogen and maintaining turgor, while the infection reduces functional sapwood and so adds to water stress, and \citet{gomezgallego_interaction_2022} found that needle starch concentration predicted which saplings died. Scots pine showed no such interaction, and because the two species occupy different Alpine positions, we would expect them to fail by different routes.

Whether such decline proceeds gradually or abruptly is not settled, although the comparative evidence favours abruptness. In central Chile, \citet{miranda_widespread_2023} documented the browning of more than 90\% of a Mediterranean-type forest within fewer than a hundred days, under conditions unprecedented across all five Mediterranean-type ecosystems, and long-term recovery is uncertain given a continuing decline in regional water balance. Although the setting is not Alpine and the magnitude does not transfer, the mode plausibly does, because drought-driven decline can be synchronous and regional rather than incremental, which would turn a gradual fuel-loading trend into a step change.

Once dead and weakened trees become fuel, the chain is complete. Alpine hazard projections acknowledge that drought, heatwaves and storms favour bark beetle outbreak and windthrow and so increase flammable material, yet they leave this pathway out \citep{gerberding_mapping_2025}. Since disturbance is a normal part of Alpine forest dynamics rather than an exception \citep{bebi_changes_2017}, and European forest disturbance has become more frequent over three decades while its severity has fallen \citep{senf_mapping_2021}, we consider the omission unlikely to be small. Moreover, because the same stress drives both mortality and fuel loading, intervention against drought stress and intervention against fuel are largely the same operation in inner Alpine valleys. We develop this alignment, which management planning does not currently exploit, in Section~\ref{sec:forest}.

\subsection{Soil alteration and the limits of severity as a proxy}

Underlying the post-fire hazard chain is the assumption that a severity map can stand in for soil condition. As reviewed by \citet{santi_wildfire_2022}, fire alters mountain soils by removing vegetation and litter, depositing ash, creating or destroying water repellency and physically weathering rock. For the Alps, the questions are how long these changes persist and whether the burn severity products used in risk assessment can reveal them, and the present evidence suggests that they cannot.

Three years after a mixed-severity fire in an Alpine Scots pine forest, burnt plots still showed greater bare soil exposure and a pyrogenic surface horizon with elevated pH and pyrogenic carbon, and their sub-surface horizons were depleted in organic carbon and in sand-sized aggregates. These differences involved properties that normally recover quickly as well as properties that do not, and the magnitude of change was not proportional to mapped severity class. In laboratory tests, \citet{negri_wildfire_2026} found the strongest water repellency mainly in low-severity plots, associated with abundant uncharred organic matter and probably intermediate heating rather than intense heating, although field tests found unburnt soils the most repellent. Remote severity mapping, even when calibrated with field measurement, therefore appears to be a poor predictor of the soil condition that governs runoff generation. This would challenge the standard post-fire workflow, in which a severity map serves as the spatial proxy for hydrological and geomorphic change, and would also undermine the common fuel-management assumption that a low-intensity burn is hydrologically benign.

That so basic an inference has gone largely untested may reflect a wider neglect of mountain soils. As reviewed by \citet{wilson_distribution_2022}, above-ground ecosystems of the Australian Alps have received far more attention than the soils beneath them, although high-elevation humus and peat soils matter particularly for ecology, hydrology and carbon storage, and peat soils are particularly vulnerable. The European Alpine literature shows the same imbalance, which matters because the Alpine cascade runs through soil condition and not only through canopy loss. Wildfire modelling mirrors that neglect: as reviewed by \citet{or_review_2023}, spread models across physical, semi-empirical and coupled fire-atmosphere frameworks focus on flame propagation and energy release while omitting fire effects on underlying soil processes. Because they do not supply quantitative thermal boundary conditions at the soil surface, post-fire predictions of water repellency, mechanical stability loss, runoff and debris-flow initiation remain disconnected from simulated fire behaviour, and soil representation in ecohydrological recovery stays largely qualitative \citep{or_review_2023}.

Alpine natural-hazard consequences of fire were catalogued before the recent inventories, and the list goes well beyond debris flow to include new avalanche-prone slopes, mudslides, increased runoff, rockfall and soil erosion. \citet{muller_forest_2020} identified steep subalpine slopes with a thin humus layer over limestone as the setting where complete soil loss and failed regeneration are most likely. In our reading, the following decade added quantification rather than recognition.

\subsection{Erosion, debris flow and sub-threshold triggering}

Post-fire debris flow carries the highest damage potential in Alpine valleys, and the small Alpine record is now quantitatively informative. Two documented events in the Western Italian Alps, at Pallanzeno in 2005 and Bussoleno in 2018, both followed brief, localised, moderate-intensity rainfall over catchments burnt in the preceding months. In both cases, \citet{tiranti_wildfires_2021} describe deposits unusually rich in fine particles and burnt wood, consistent with a centimetres-thick layer of thermally reworked material produced by the fire and progressively redistributed by intervening rainfall.

Numerical modelling of the Bussoleno event makes the mechanism quantitative, although the sample remains a single catchment. An October 2017 fire burnt over 95\% of a 1.37\,km$^2$ catchment, and after a spring 40\% wetter than normal, a radar-estimated rainfall of 12\,mm in 30 minutes (an intensity of 24\,mm\,h$^{-1}$, a five-year return period) mobilised roughly 20{,}000\,m$^3$ of sediment and woody debris some eight months after the fire. The flow destroyed two buildings and forced the evacuation of around 150 residents of the alluvial fan. In situ tests found the shear strength of burnt soils reduced by a factor of about three. According to \citet{laporta_postwildfire_2025}, the triggering intensity lay below the threshold of more than 30\,mm\,h$^{-1}$ typical of unburnt basins of that type, although complex orography limits the reliability of the radar estimate. Fire therefore appears to lower the rainfall needed to trigger a flow, rather than only increasing the response to a given rainfall, and so moves the hazard into a return period that occurs several times per decade.

Compared with the range where this science is most developed, the Alpine value is not anomalous. In Glenwood Canyon (Colorado), after a fire that burnt 13{,}000\,ha, the fire-wide triggering threshold in the first post-fire year was 25.9\,mm\,h$^{-1}$, close to the Bussoleno value, and 89\% of the observed debris flows were triggered above the modelled threshold. In the second year, however, the operational threshold was 33.7\,mm\,h$^{-1}$, and eight storms exceeded it without producing a single debris flow. \citet{rengers_evaluating_2024} attribute this to vegetation recovery (from 24\% to 57\%) and to sediment depletion, and recalculating the threshold from imagery of the recovering vegetation raised it to 40\,mm\,h$^{-1}$. We therefore treat the threshold as a recovering quantity rather than a constant, which means that for Alpine application the length of the low-threshold window matters more than its depth. The same study found that the standard volume model overestimated observed erosion by a median factor of 4.4, so a threshold imported with confidence should not be accompanied by a volume estimate imported with the same confidence.

Thermal spalling (a sediment source specific to rocky burnt terrain) has been quantified in a European mountain setting. A fire burnt 131.64\,km$^2$ of Sardinia at temperatures above 800\,\degree{}C, with 63.1\% of the area at moderate-high or high severity, and spalling at 69 rock exposures removed between 0.07\% and 74.57\% of the exposed surface (with a median of about 20\%), in fragments from 3\,mm to 13\,cm. In the following two years, three rainstorms triggered 33 debris flows across 21 sub-basins, and spall fragments made up between 1.3\% and 36.6\% of their deposits, with freshly spalled angular cobbles forming 10 to 30\% of all cobbles in the deposits. \citet{pala_sediment_2025} found that spalling did not differ between high and moderate-high severity but was sparse at lower severity. These results suggest that fire in rocky terrain produces transportable sediment directly, rather than only exposing what was already there.

Sediment supply, rather than runoff alone, appears to set event magnitude, although this demonstration is imported rather than Alpine. Lidar differencing of the 2018 Montecito flows (California) showed 550{,}000\,m$^3$ of previously stored channel sediment progressively entrained and partly redeposited, with scour concentrated where coarse colluvium had accumulated and persisting beyond the mountain front. \citet{morell_sediment_2021} infer that flow volume is controlled by the sediment available for scour, and hence by catchment history. If this holds in the Alps, post-fire debris-flow magnitude would depend on the antecedent state of the channel network as much as on the fire.

\subsection{Water supply, water quality, snow and ice}

Beyond the fan apex, the cascade continues into the water system that the range supplies. Alpine catchments provide drinking water, hydropower and irrigation to populations far larger than those living in the mountains, and the processes that carry sediment onto a road also deliver it, with its chemistry, into storage.

However, the size of the water-quality signal has not been measured in the Alps. For a Mediterranean supply reservoir, a modelled scenario burning around 30\% of the contributing area raised median suspended solids by a factor of 4.7 and maximum nitrate and phosphate concentrations by a factor of four, although dilution within the reservoir removed roughly half the nitrate and nine tenths of the phosphate before the dam wall. Both nutrients stayed within drinking-water limits, whereas \citet{basso_potential_2021} found that suspended solids exceeded the recommended maximum value. We consider the mobilisation, and the dependence of the outcome on storage volume, general enough to transfer to the Alps. The buffering is contingent, however, since the same study notes that drought could weaken dilution, and drought is when the antecedent conditions for fire are present. In a review of 56 Greek studies of fire effects on ecosystem services, every recorded entry for hydrological cycle regulation was negative, as was every entry for cultural services, and across all services \citet{misseyanni_impact_2025} found effects most pronounced in the first two post-fire years. Similarly, water regulation features among the services assessed as at risk from wildfire in Mediterranean forest landscapes \citep{lecinadiaz_assessing_2021}.

Snow currently limits high-elevation Alpine fire, and the coupling between fire and snow runs both ways. Of the two directions, the effect of snow on fire is the better documented, although largely by inference. Mountain warming reduces winter and spring snowpack, with strong observed declines in Alpine snow-cover duration and depth below 2{,}000\,m \citep{rangwala_climate_2012, pepin_climate_2022}. Snow-cover duration is projected to shorten further in the upper valleys of one Piedmont zone \citep{pede_mountain_2022}, and in one Swiss valley \citet{frei_evidence_2023} found that forest has advanced and infilled near the treeline. A shorter snow season is therefore likely to lengthen the drying window where fuel is now accumulating. In contrast, the effect of fire on the cryosphere has no Alpine literature at all, and the comparative evidence suggests that this is a research gap rather than an absent process.

Fire alters snowpacks through several mechanisms at once. Canopy loss changes interception and the radiation balance at the snow surface, burnt debris and black carbon lower albedo and amplify snow-albedo feedback \citep{koshkin_wildfire_2022, pepin_climate_2022}, and the resulting changes to accumulation and ablation persist for years while the canopy recovers \citep{koshkin_wildfire_2022}. The radiative term has been measured directly on ice. On a well-instrumented glacier in the Canadian Rockies, \citet{aubrywake_fire_2022} found that upwind wildfire activity affected melt both through albedo reduction from deposited soot and through the effect of smoke on the overlying atmosphere, with smoky days warmer than clear days and a sustained reduction in surface shortwave irradiance of 103\,W\,m$^{-2}$ at peak.

Where the net effect on snow has been measured in a mountain range, it runs in an unexpected direction. Over eleven water years in the northern Sierra Nevada, snow-covered area in a single burnt basin increased significantly after the fire (compared with an unburnt control), regardless of slope aspect or burn severity. \citet{micheletty_application_2014} also report that days above a pre-fire high snow-cover threshold rose by 81\%, yet basin-average melt-out came earlier than in the control. On the evidence of this single basin, canopy removal appears to put more snow on the ground and to remove it sooner, with opposite effects on the two quantities that matter for an Alpine catchment (storage and timing). We see nothing that would prevent either mechanism from operating in the Alps, where snow and ice carry economic weight far beyond their hydrological role \citep{pede_mountain_2022}. The lack of Alpine work therefore leaves a term missing from the water-resource accounting of a range whose runoff supports downstream agriculture, hydropower and navigation, and it also leaves unexamined a feedback in which fire shortens the snow season that currently limits fire.

Drought strains the Alpine water system as well as driving fire, and the two stresses compound. During the 2022--2023 snow drought, glacier melt in two large Italian Alpine catchments rose to 148\% above its decadal mean, and its share of summer streamflow doubled to tripled, reaching 75\% and 83\% at peak against medians of 31.5\% and 22.6\%, respectively. Streamflow nonetheless stayed below the long-term average, so \citet{leone_20222023_2026} conclude that the glacial buffer only partly compensated for the precipitation deficit. On one glacier in the Canadian Rockies, soot deposited from upwind fires increased melt by up to about 10\% in later seasons, although smoke itself partly offset this by reducing net radiation \citep{aubrywake_fire_2022}. Taken together, the two results suggest a loop in which the drought that raises fire danger also exhausts the snowpack and shifts water supply onto glaciers, and soot from fire may then accelerate their melt, although this last link is imported from a single Canadian glacier. Because no Alpine study has attempted to quantify this loop, we identify it as a research priority rather than an established result.

\section{Forest: Change, Ecology and Management}
\label{sec:forest}

\subsection{Forest change, composition and fuel connectivity}

Alpine forest that now burns is largely a recent product of land use, and this origin determines which policy levers can change it. For centuries, grazing, litter collection and fuelwood extraction kept Alpine forests open in a mosaic that limited fire spread whatever the weather, and as that regime retreated the fuel bed filled in again \citep{bebi_changes_2017}. This expansion is not uniform, however, because it is fastest where recent agricultural abandonment coincides with climatic conditions favourable for tree establishment \citep{garbarino_contrasting_2020}. Anticipating future fuel continuity therefore requires knowing when land use changed relative to growing conditions, not only where abandonment occurred. Even so, we treat the view that abandonment always increases fire hazard as a regionally supported finding rather than a general rule, because in their global review \citet{mantero_influence_2020} found the reported effects of abandonment on disturbances other than fire to be inconsistent and sometimes opposite in direction.

Expansion also has a vertical component. In an intensively surveyed valley in Central Switzerland, \citet{frei_evidence_2023} found that the treeline advanced by an average of 10\,m per decade between the early 1970s and 2012 (with local maxima of 42\,m per decade). However, of the 765 cells of a 50\,m grid in which trees had established since 1972, only 21\% marked an advance beyond the former treeline, while 79\% were infilling below it. Fuel is therefore densifying as well as moving upslope, and it is plausibly this infilling below the treeline that turns scattered stands into continuous fuel. At Alpine scale, 24\% of the 10.5 million hectares of forest is in an open-canopy state \citep{stritih_alternative_2023}, and because this state is concentrated at high elevations, forest edges and warm dry sites, it coincides with the positions in which fuel is now filling in.

Where regrowth happens depends on economics and varies locally. In a spatially explicit economic analysis of the Swiss mountains, \citet{gellrich_agricultural_2007} found forest regrowth concentrated where cultivation costs were high and yield potential low (with non-linear relationships to slope steepness and soil stoniness). They concluded that general policy measures applied across the whole mountain area cannot prevent abandonment and regrowth, which instead require attention to local characteristics. Because Swiss forest area has risen by roughly a third over the last 150 years, mostly through natural regrowth rather than planting, fuel accumulation in the Alps appears to follow agricultural marginality, which would also explain why it concentrates on the steep ground where fire runs longest.

Over longer timescales, a multi-millennial record from the Apennines shows that the present composition of a European mountain forest can reflect land use rather than climatic equilibrium. At 1{,}390\,m in the Northern Apennines, \citet{moralesmolino_longterm_2021} found that species-rich mixed silver fir forests dominated from about 10{,}500 to 5{,}500 years ago, with fir making up to half the pollen assemblage, before beech expanded and eventually replaced them entirely. Charcoal influx and temperature each explained about a tenth of the variation in the vegetation record, and whereas fir, elm, ash and lime all declined strongly with burning, beech tolerated light to moderate fire and declined under heavy fire. The authors concluded that today's monospecific beech forests result from multi-millennial land-use intensification combined with late Holocene cooling, and that the displaced mixed fir forests were the more drought-tolerant system. We read the Alpine fuel bed as having the same kind of origin, since in both ranges the stand that now carries fire reflects how people used the land, which suggests that the correction must also come through land use.

This denser fuel bed has a direct effect on fire behaviour. Across 21 Swiss pyroregions, \citet{conedera_linking_2024} showed that potential fire-path length (which combines slope length with fuel connectivity) explains between 56 and 65\% of the variance in large-fire statistics. Under a scenario foresting Switzerland up to 2{,}500\,m, 16 of the 21 pyroregions would fall in the highest propensity class. Because forest expansion onto the steep marginal ground that was abandoned first increases precisely this quantity, a landscape can become more fire-prone without any change in weather.

Fuel loads have been measured destructively in one Alpine country, where \citet{neumann_fuel_2022} sampled 93 plots in Austrian Norway spruce, Scots pine and black pine forests and found that fuel load depended on region and forest type rather than on stand age. Dead surface fuel increased and live understorey fuel decreased with basal area, and whereas spruce stands carried the highest loads, black pine on the dry eastern lowland margin (the forest type that burns most often in Austria) carried the lowest. Black pine stands combined open canopies with more shrub and grass cover and the deepest fuel bed, which the authors take to indicate that fuel structure, together with the drier eastern climate, contributes to their fire frequency. However, these measurements leave out the layers that matter most once the fuel bed dries. The unsampled duff layer is estimated, from the literature and from Austrian forest-floor inventories, to add between 15 and 70\,t\,ha$^{-1}$ that can burn and smoulder under drought. Canopy fuel (roughly 10\,t\,ha$^{-1}$ in spruce stands), in contrast, is consumed only in crown fires, which are currently rare in central Europe. A drier climate could therefore bring both reserves into play.

Fire in the Alps also enters forests that are already shaped by other disturbances, and these disturbances interact rather than simply add up. Wind, snow break, bark beetles and avalanches shape Alpine stands continuously \citep{bebi_changes_2017}, and roughly half of disturbed stands passed into the open-canopy state described above \citep{stritih_alternative_2023}. A European baseline of disturbance regimes, resolved by causal agent, is now also available. Classifying more than 3.8 million fire, wind and bark beetle events in European forests between 1985 and 2023, \citet{miguel_unravelling_2026} separated fire regimes along a north-south gradient, in which fire is severe and rare in boreal and temperate forest but moderate, large and frequent in Mediterranean and transitional forest. Wind and beetle disturbance fall into four regimes, of which the frequent and small regime predominates in Central Europe.

For Alpine assessment, we consider the most important result to be that these regimes are unstable, since about 15\% of grid cells changed regime within the record \citep{miguel_unravelling_2026}. Fire frequency and severity fell in temperate and Mediterranean forest between the first and second periods, whereas wind and beetle disturbance then became more severe in temperate forest between the second and third. We therefore do not treat a disturbance regime as a fixed regional property on which a fuel model can be built once.

Susceptibility to disturbance also appears to depend partly on management, not only on climate. Reviewing earlier European work for the MANFRED programme (a transnational Alpine Space collaboration of fifteen partners in six countries), \citet{jandl_management_2013} noted that recorded storm damage is not driven by climate alone. The prevailing practice of letting increasingly dense forests mature has itself produced stand types that are highly vulnerable to storms, so fuel build-up and storm vulnerability appear to share a common origin in forest management.

Fire differs from the other disturbance agents, however, in what it leaves behind. Windthrow appears to leave biological legacies that continue to protect the slope, although this has been simulated explicitly in only one Alpine stand, a windthrown site in the Dolomites. There, rockfall modelling by \citet{costa_biological_2021} found that the barrier effect index improved after the storm, so that the fallen stand was more protective than the forest before it, because lying logs and stumps act as physical obstacles rather than as energy absorbers. This legacy can persist for up to thirty years, until decay and soil contact degrade it. In contrast, fire does not leave such a legacy. Burnt stems and rootstocks can fall during and after the fire and carry rock with them, as observed at a single Austrian site \citep{melzner_brief_2019}, and in fire-injured beech stands most damaged stems collapse within about two decades \citep{maringer_temporal_2016}. Simulations that remove the vegetation entirely show the corresponding loss of the barrier effect \citep{asteriou_assessing_2025}. Two disturbances with similar canopy loss can therefore have opposite short-term effects on slope protection, and for this reason we do not treat fire as interchangeable with the other Alpine disturbances.

It follows that salvage decisions after fire cannot simply adopt the consensus developed after windthrow, which advises against salvage in order to retain protective legacies \citep{costa_biological_2021} but rests on an evidence base that is less clear-cut than often assumed. Eighteen years after a Tatra windstorm, \citet{seben_impact_2024} found that salvaged areas retained an eighth of the deadwood volume of unsalvaged areas but supported higher regeneration diversity (5.40 against 3.85 species per plot), whereas unsalvaged plots were dominated by Norway spruce. These results suggest that the trade-off between protective legacy, regeneration composition and future fuel is real and differs between disturbance types, yet we found no evaluation of it for Alpine post-fire sites.

A comparative case from outside Europe illustrates both the parallel and its limits. In the southern Appalachian Mountains, \citet{reilly_drivers_2022} reported that decades of suppression preceded a regional outbreak of more than a dozen large wildfires during one of the warmest and driest droughts of the last century. The historical mosaic of that landscape had been maintained by frequent low- and mixed-severity fire, and land-use change had already reorganised its composition. Because the Alps never had a frequent-fire regime to suppress, the trajectory differs, and we take the comparison to show only that a landscape whose fuel structure no longer matches its disturbance history can support a regional outbreak once drought provides the trigger.

Which species grow back matters as much as how much forest grows back, because species composition both shapes fire and is shaped by it. Alpine tree species differ sharply in their capacity to survive fire, while Alpine forest composition is changing on a timescale comparable to that of the fire regime. Simulating Swiss Alpine landscapes, \citet{schumacher_relative_2006} found that climate-driven vegetation shifts will influence biomass and species distribution more than management will, and in their simulations fire emerges as a property of the landscape that then feeds back on vegetation.

Fire vulnerability depends on tree traits, and this now has independent experimental support. Simulating fire across 4438 French Alpine forest plots, \citet{dupire_vulnerability_2019} showed that cold-season fires (about half of all fires there) had limited impact except on low-elevation southern stands. Average summer conditions produced the highest mortality in mixed and broadleaved low-elevation forests (up to 75\% mortality in coppice). Extremely dry summers produced high mortality in all communities, with the lowest mortality in the thick-barked, high-canopied larch stands of the inner Alps, whereas subalpine Swiss stone pine and mountain pine ranked among the most fire-sensitive species \citep{dupire_vulnerability_2019}. By heating stem discs from six tree species common in Central Europe under controlled conditions, \citet{mutterer_bark_2026} reproduced this ranking experimentally. In their experiments bark thickness appears to be the most robust predictor of the time the cambium takes to reach the lethal threshold, placing larch, Douglas-fir and Scots pine above silver fir and Norway spruce, with beech lowest. Thermal resistance also declines with bark moisture and with height on the stem. Because the two methods are independent, we regard their agreement as a stronger basis than either alone.

This relationship also runs in the other direction, from fire to composition. After a stand-replacing fire in the Rhone valley, \citet{moser_potential_2010} observed that recruitment favoured pioneer birch, aspen and willow over the pre-fire larch, spruce and Scots pine. Because seedling density tracked moisture availability rather than fire intensity, they concluded that drought limits post-fire recruitment in the Central Alps. Palaeoecological records extend the same logic over millennia, showing that fire and grazing, rather than temperature alone, determined whether the timberline reached its climatic potential \citep{colombaroli_species_2010}. Similarly, in a doctoral thesis on two forest sites in the central Dinaric Alps of Montenegro, \citet{cagliero_effect_2022} concluded that fire and land use likely reduced old-growth fir-spruce-beech forest during the Middle Ages.

\subsection{Protective function, biodiversity and the recovery window}

On an Alpine slope, the consequences of fire depend on what fire removes, how long it stays removed and what else changes in the meantime. Among the post-fire processes involved, rockfall is the most specific to the Alps and the least studied internationally. As recently as 2019, \citet{melzner_brief_2019} noted that no work on post-wildfire rockfall activity had been published for the eastern Alps, and that even in the better-studied western United States the process had received little attention compared with debris flow.

Thermal effects on rock begin at known temperatures. In a review of three Spanish cases, \citet{sarro_effects_2021} describe fire acting at the same time on the source area, the propagation zone and the affected area. In the source area, mineral expansion, dehydration and decarbonation open microcracks, and granites lose strength appreciably above 500\degree{}C as quartz approaches its phase transition at 573\degree{}C, whereas limestones alter from about 300\degree{}C. In the propagation zone, the loss of vegetation removes the energy absorption that reduces both the reach and the frequency of falling blocks, while in the affected area heat degrades the cables and barriers installed to stop rockfall. However, the same review notes that the restitution and friction coefficients used in rockfall trajectory models have not been standardised for post-fire conditions, which suggests that the parameters needed to model a burnt slope are those least constrained by measurement.

Vegetation affects rockfall on two timescales. Because the litter and ground vegetation that stop small blocks are lost immediately, whereas the standing stems that intercept falling material are lost more slowly, exposure can rise for years as fire-killed trees decay and fall. Isolating the effect of vegetation through trajectory modelling, \citet{asteriou_assessing_2025} showed that it depends strongly on block size. Small blocks (of around 0.01\,m$^3$), which mostly came to rest on a vegetated slope, travel much further once cover is removed, whereas for blocks an order of magnitude larger, kinetic energy overwhelms the resistance of vegetation. Protection is therefore likely lost first against the frequent, small events that dominate long-run exposure.

For beech on the southern side of the range, the rate and duration of this loss have been quantified, and the results are more severe than the general disturbance-window literature implies. Across 244 plots in burnt beech stands of Ticino and Piedmont (where beech grows mainly between 600 and 1{,}700\,m), \citet{gehring_shallow_2019} found that root reinforcement fell by roughly 70\% within five years of high-severity fire and by 98\% by year fifteen. Moderate severity produced about 15\% loss in the first five years and 60\% by year twenty-three, whereas low-severity burns remained stable. After high-severity fire, modelled protective capacity against shallow landslides fell below a quarter of the unburnt value regardless of soil and slope class, and landslide probability stayed elevated for at least forty years. Rockfall protection follows a similar course on a different timescale. Across 34 burnt beech stands in the southern Alps, \citet{maringer_temporal_2016} found that low-severity burns retained close to unburnt protective capacity, while moderate and high severity reduced it significantly, most strongly between ten and thirty years after the fire.

These results change how we understand the consequence window. Protective capacity reaches its lowest point years after the fire rather than immediately, because standing dead stems keep working until they fall and regeneration has not yet replaced them. An assessment made in the first year after a fire therefore measures the wrong part of the recovery curve. For risk assessment, we therefore take the main parameter to be how long post-fire hazard remains elevated, rather than whether it is elevated. As reviewed by \citet{santi_wildfire_2022}, the general geomorphological literature cites a post-fire sediment disturbance window of three to four years, occasionally extending to thirty, while noting that recovery in high alpine terrain can span 50 to 500 years. The forty-year figure for shallow landslide disposition in southern Alpine beech exceeds this standard window by an order of magnitude, and we regard it as the clearest available evidence that short post-fire windows imported from gentler settings are optimistic for the Alps, and that the Alpine window is likely set by the recovery of biological structure rather than by the decay of a surface soil property.

Independent lines of evidence support this reading. \citet{negri_wildfire_2026} found soil properties still altered three years after an Alpine mixed-severity fire, including properties that usually recover quickly. Post-fire seedling establishment (the first step towards a protective canopy) was drought-limited at the low and medium altitudes of one Valais burn, where successful establishment required precipitation far above the long-term average \citep{moser_potential_2010}. At Alpine scale, 72\% of disturbed forests return to a closed-canopy state within 35 years, although submediterranean forests remain open for decades \citep{stritih_alternative_2023}. At the upper margin the window may not close at all, although this observation comes from a single massif. On burnt subalpine slopes in the Northern Limestone Alps at 1800--1950\,m, \citet{degiuli_arrested_2024} found tree seedlings nearly absent and no meaningful mountain pine re-establishment in about eighty years, a state better described as arrested succession than as delayed recovery. We therefore treat the length of the window as partly a management outcome, and one to which current Alpine restoration practice appears poorly matched (Section~\ref{sec:fuelbreaks}).

This protective function has been valued at one site, and it is itself exposed to climate change. For a protection forest above Martigny in the Valais, \citet{moos_climate_2021} coupled forest-dynamics and three-dimensional rockfall simulation and placed annual rockfall risk roughly five times higher without the forest than with it. Under climate scenarios, the risk rises within 150 years as composition shifts from beech to pubescent oak, and small blocks reaching the railway move from once per decade to once every three years (the monetary values are given in Section~\ref{sec:economics}). Simulating an elevation gradient in South Tyrol, \citet{hillebrand_contrasting_2023} showed that the loss of protection depends on elevation, because under severe warming montane forests decline drastically in avalanche protection, with drought-induced biomass reductions reaching 97\% in dry inner valleys, whereas subalpine stands remain stable or improve.

Fire in the Alps is not, however, uniformly destructive, and the review literature openly warns against assuming otherwise. In a systematic assessment of mountain wildfire against fifty ecosystem services, imported here from the Colorado Front Range of the Rocky Mountains, \citet{vukomanovic_systematic_2019} found that current fire regimes mostly reduce service provision and that effects on cultural services are mixed. Change in vegetation composition and structure was the dominant mechanism. However, the same assessment suggests that a bias towards negative characterisation may be built into how the research questions are posed.

Alpine forest can now be placed in a European ranking of ecological vulnerability. In a continental assessment combining ecological value, the resistance of vegetation to fire and post-fire recovery time, \citet{arrogantefunes_mapping_2024} rank temperate conifer forest second among European biomes (behind tundra and ahead of boreal forest), a high rank that is driven by recovery time rather than by resistance. Non-forested vegetation, including the grassland, heathland and shrubland of the subalpine and alpine belts, is assigned no coping capacity in that framework, which is a modelling convention rather than a measurement but follows from the absence of the bark and crown traits on which resistance is defined. Taken together, these results suggest that the Alpine problem lies in recovery: what fire removes is not resisted at the time of the fire and returns slowly afterwards, so the total ecological value lost over the recovery period can be large even where the burnt area is small.

Abundance and composition respond differently to fire, and confusing them can lead to wrong conclusions in either direction. At two burnt sites in the inner-Alpine Valais, \citet{rey_effects_2019} found that high-severity fire roughly halved bird abundance (21.68 against 41.59 territories per 10\,ha) without significantly changing species richness, with fourteen species more abundant in burnt stands and five in unburnt ones. Red-listed and priority species were substantially more common in burnt stands, and this effect strengthened with time since fire, with many species peaking eight to twelve years after the event and declining as the vegetation became denser. At these sites, therefore, fire removed individuals and rearranged the community rather than eliminating species, and the species it favoured were disproportionately those of conservation concern. Similarly, the burnt subalpine slopes in the Northern Limestone Alps described above carried significantly higher vascular plant richness than unburnt controls, including alpine specialists absent from the unburnt plots, even though tree regeneration failed entirely \citep{degiuli_arrested_2024}.

Neither measurement, however, captures animal mortality. As reviewed by \citet{demiguel_fires_2021}, individual animals are killed by burning, smoke inhalation and heat stress. The most exposed taxa are those that cannot outrun a fire front or that depend on structures the fire removes, such as small mammals, cavity-nesting birds, reptiles, and aquatic organisms affected by post-fire sediment delivery. Hollows used for denning and nesting are lost with the stems that contain them, and poor dispersers are slowest to recolonise. These mechanisms are general rather than Alpine, and the only order-of-magnitude figures available come from a single non-European event. Because we found no Alpine estimate of faunal mortality in the literature assembled here, we treat faunal losses in the Alps as unmeasured rather than known to be small.

We read these findings as refining the risk framing of this review rather than weakening it, because they show that Alpine losses have several components that behave differently. The protective function of forest on slopes above assets is spatially concentrated, can be valued in money \citep{moos_climate_2021} and is slow to recover \citep{stritih_alternative_2023, degiuli_arrested_2024}, and the ecological value lost over that recovery period is high for the forest type that dominates the Alpine belt \citep{arrogantefunes_mapping_2024}. For biodiversity, fire is negative for abundance and for closed-forest specialists and positive for open-habitat and disturbance-dependent species \citep{rey_effects_2019, degiuli_arrested_2024}, whereas faunal mortality remains unquantified. Combining these components into a single measure therefore tends to lead either to treating fire as uniformly destructive or to treating it as ecologically benign because some species benefit, and both errors appear in the wider literature.
\subsection{Managing for protection: silviculture, species and fuel treatment}

In Alpine forestry the principal product is often not wood. Because mountain forests serve a protective function against natural hazards \citep{dinca_timber_2025}, management across large parts of the range aims to maintain a stand structure that can stop rockfall, anchor the snowpack against avalanche release and hold soil on steep ground. As reviewed by \citet{dinca_timber_2025}, timber is a secondary output, since in difficult terrain harvesting costs are often not covered by timber revenue. This priority of protection is long established. Reporting on the same programme (MANFRED) that linked storm vulnerability to management practice, \citet{jandl_management_2013} described protection as by far the most important ecosystem service of mountain forests. They identified eco-engineering as the response where climate change puts that function at risk by increasing vulnerability to rockfall, avalanches and landslides.

Protection can also be given a monetary value. In the replacement-cost framework that \citet{accastello_asforesee_2019} harmonised for the Alpine Space, the service is valued by the cost of the engineered structures that would otherwise be required (designed to European standards), net of the management expenditure needed to sustain protective capacity. In a single demonstration case (a ten-hectare Scots pine stand in the Italian Alps assessed over a twenty-five-year horizon), the forest met local safety requirements without supplementary artificial measures, and the avoided-risk valuation of the Valais protection forest by \citet{moos_climate_2021} (described above) points the same way. Under either accounting convention the protective value is therefore substantial, whereas the thinning needed to sustain it often has a negative stumpage value on steep slopes \citep{accastello_asforesee_2019}, and it is this protective value that fire puts at risk. We give the monetary figures from both studies in Section~\ref{sec:economics}.

Valuing protection in this way exposes a management problem, because protective capacity is not a fixed attribute of a stand but depends on a structure that must be actively maintained, and under climate change the prospects for maintaining it depend on elevation. In the South Tyrol simulations (described above), \citet{hillebrand_contrasting_2023} project the steepest losses in montane forests, whereas subalpine stands hold or improve their rockfall protection. These projections suggest that silvicultural effort needs to be concentrated where the protective function is most exposed, which is not where the forest is most productive. Fire also sharpens conflicts between management objectives. In scenario modelling of Austrian forests over the century, \citet{jandl_strategies_2018} found stem biomass declining by between 9\% and 29\% under harvest-oriented strategies and rising by 34\% under a reduced-harvest, conservation-oriented strategy, with long-lived wood products emerging as the option that best balances carbon sequestration, economic return and disturbance risk. However, the reduced-harvest option, which maximises stored carbon, also maximises standing fuel, and increasing forest cover extends the area over which fire is possible \citep{gerberding_mapping_2025}. Carbon policy and fire policy therefore appear to pull in opposite directions in Alpine forests, and none of the Alpine studies we reviewed evaluates that trade-off explicitly.

If protective structure is the management objective and fire is an emerging threat to it, species choice becomes a fire management decision. Alpine forestry is already making that decision for other reasons, although its direction appears to be set by climate rather than by fire: in the Swiss simulations (cited above), \citet{schumacher_relative_2006} placed climate-driven change in composition ahead of management as the main driver of future biomass and species distribution. Upward shifts of altitudinal vegetation belts would require site types to be reassessed, and the treeline advance and infilling that \citet{frei_evidence_2023} recorded in one Swiss valley show that this movement is already under way. Because species suitable for a site today are not necessarily those suitable in fifty years, Alpine silviculture is moving towards mixtures and towards provenances from warmer analogue sites.

Fire interferes with this trajectory in a specific way, because the species favoured by warming and drought at low and mid elevations, and those that colonise after fire, are largely not the species that currently provide protective structure. Post-fire recruitment in the Rhone valley (described above) favoured pioneer broadleaves over the pre-fire conifers \citep{moser_potential_2010}, and \citet{moos_climate_2021} project the Valais protection forest to shift from beech to pubescent oak under warming, with rockfall risk rising as it does. Pioneer broadleaves establish faster than the conifers they replace but hold rock less effectively at the relevant stem diameters, and the transition period coincides with the consequence window described above. Where fire resistance can be built into species choice, however, the evidence is reasonably firm, because stand-level mortality simulation and stem-disc heating give the same ranking \citep{dupire_vulnerability_2019, mutterer_bark_2026}. Larch and Scots pine (two of the three species ranked highest) are already major components of Alpine protection forest, which in our view is a more favourable starting position than the fire intolerance of the range as a whole would suggest.

Provenance is a lever in its own right, since long-term trials for Norway spruce reported by \citet{jandl_management_2013} indicate that matching provenance to anticipated future site conditions, rather than to present ones, can substantially raise potential productivity and reduce risk over the stand lifetime. Browsing, by contrast, is a constraint, because adaptive silvicultural concepts can only be implemented where browsing damage from high ungulate densities is held to acceptable levels, and at the densities then prevailing, deer were inhibiting the establishment of species-rich forest. Thus, whereas species diversification is the main recommendation of Alpine adaptive management, ungulate management is its precondition. We therefore place a wildlife-management decision upstream of fire resilience.

What remains unresolved is the tension with the other objectives that silviculture must serve at the same time. Reduced-harvest strategies maximise carbon storage while building standing biomass (as noted above) \citep{jandl_strategies_2018}, whereas \citet{majlingova_sustainability_2025} calls for regeneration-oriented approaches to mountain forest management that actively restore degraded systems rather than maintain existing conditions. At the same time, the harvesting operations needed to implement any of this are technically and economically constrained in steep terrain \citep{dinca_timber_2025}. We found no Alpine study that optimises species selection jointly for protective function, carbon, timber and fire resistance, and until one does, the trade-off is likely to be made implicitly at every planting decision.

Alpine fuel load can be reduced mechanically, by grazing or by fire, and in this setting the evidence favours mechanical treatment and grazing. Mechanical treatment has a justification independent of fire. Reviewing drought-induced Scots pine mortality, \citet{giuggiola_droughtinduced_2010} suggest that thinning and shrub removal could ease drought stress by reducing competition for water, improving carbon uptake and lowering mortality risk, although whether thinning mitigates drought in the most sensitive areas remains unclear. The same operations reduce surface and ladder fuel and interrupt canopy continuity. In inner Alpine valleys, drought-driven Scots pine mortality is the main forest health problem and fuel accumulation is the emerging fire problem. We would therefore expect treating one to largely treat the other, so that the case for thinning does not depend on accepting a projection of future fire.

Grazing offers the broadest scope for policy. In a systematic review of the herbivore literature, \citet{rouetleduc_effects_2021} conclude that fuel reduction by grazing animals is a promising way to mitigate wildfire where land abandonment has led to shrub and tree encroachment, with effectiveness depending on herbivore type, density and feeding behaviour. The same review finds that agricultural and forest policy could support grazing far more effectively than they do, for example by regulating and promoting herbivory in forest areas from which animals have traditionally been excluded to prevent damage, and by directing agri-environmental subsidies towards extensive grazing and pastoralism. Using landscape simulation, \citet{pais_optimizing_2023} quantified the gain from embedding fuel treatment in land-use policy rather than applying it alone. Prescribed burning alone reduced subsequent wildfire by up to 36\% over thirty years, whereas the leverage of treatment reached unity (one hectare of wildfire avoided per hectare treated) only when combined with restoration of high nature value farmland and fire-smart forest conversion.

Effect sizes for grazing are large, but all of the available ones come from outside the Alps. As reviewed by \citet{xu_how_2026}, managed grazing has reduced rate of spread by more than 60\% in pure grass communities and by over 50\% in mixed grass and shrub, and Mediterranean studies report burnt-area reductions between 25.9\% and 60.9\%. Because grazing works by creating patchiness rather than by removing fuel uniformly, interrupting horizontal continuity, it behaves more like a distributed set of small breaks than like a thinning. Its effect is, however, non-linear and reverses at high intensity. Removing less than about half the photosynthetic leaf area reduces fuel and favours fire-resistant species, whereas heavier use, particularly during drought, increases erosion, admits flammable annuals and can leave a continuous, homogeneous fuel bed more flammable than the one it replaced. On erodible Alpine slopes above assets, overgrazing may carry a second cost by removing low, dense ground cover, which retained small blocks in the post-fire rockfall simulations of \citet{asteriou_assessing_2025} for Mediterranean shrubland in Greece. The threshold between the two regimes has not been located for any Alpine pasture. Timing adds a further complication, since grazing in high-altitude systems may be most effective in the drier spring and autumn (rather than in the humid summer, when Alpine stock is at its highest elevation) \citep{xu_how_2026}.

Within the Alps, the only evidence on grazing and fire is historical and correlational. In the Valais, \citet{zumbrunnen_human_2012} found that livestock density in forests was associated with fewer fires before the late 1950s, while wood pasturing remained widespread, whereas after 1956 high livestock densities coincided with more fires. Because those densities lay in the driest eastern part of the canton, we consider dryness rather than fuel removal the more likely explanation for the later association.

Where treatment is placed in the landscape matters as much as whether it happens. In their landscape modelling of a transboundary Iberian biosphere reserve over 2010--2050, \citet{lecinadiaz_incorporating_2023} found that restoring high nature value farmland saved roughly €3.9 million in suppression costs in present-value terms. Combining it with fire-smart forestry cut total societal cost (suppression plus ecosystem service damage) from €25.7 million under business as usual to €7.1 million. In contrast, fire-smart forestry applied alone \emph{raised} suppression costs, so the saving came from the agricultural-landscape component. Because abandonment of agricultural land is the proximate cause of fuel accumulation in the Alps, we regard this as the most directly relevant economic result available, and it suggests that the effective lever is agricultural policy rather than forest policy alone.

Empirical support for this argument now exists at national scale, though not for the Alps alone. \citet{spadoni_active_2023} analysed about 49{,}000 Italian fires larger than a hectare between 2007 and 2017 across 602 agro-forest districts spanning Alpine, temperate and Mediterranean climate regions. Districts with more active land governance showed smaller burnt shares of ecosystem services and of the wildland-urban interface, as well as lower forest burn severity, and this association held after accounting for climate, fire weather and simulated flammability, whereas land abandonment increased all three impacts. Governance was represented by proxies (sustainable forest management and certification, extensive agro-pastoral activity, protected areas, and rural development and LIFE funding), and its effect was moderate compared with climate and flammability and did not always act in the same direction. Severity, for instance, fell with the governance of rural and marginal areas up to the median value and rose beyond it, a reversal the authors relate to intensive harvesting.

How well a treatment performs also depends on the fire weather under which it is tested, and this dependence works against the direction in which the Alps are moving. \citet{brodie_forest_2024} illustrate this dependence with a randomised, replicated experiment in California that later burned in a wildfire. Relative to untreated controls, combined thinning and prescribed burning reduced the probability of tree mortality by 0.60, crown volume consumed by 86\% and bole char height by more than seven metres. However, the mortality benefit was greatest at intermediate values of the burning index and declined under the most extreme fire weather, although crown-fire metrics remained responsive. These results suggest that fuel treatment achieves most where danger is moderate. Similarly, at landscape scale the mitigating effect of land governance was strongest in warm, dry and flammable districts and weakened as fire weather during the events became more severe \citep{spadoni_active_2023}. Because Alpine fire danger is currently low over most of the range but projected to rise towards moderate levels \citep{gerberding_mapping_2025, miller_climate_2024}, the window in which treatment is most cost-effective is likely to be opening now.

\subsection{Prescribed fire, fuel breaks and post-fire restoration}
\label{sec:fuelbreaks}

For each of these instruments, the Alpine evidence is thinner than the confidence with which it is discussed. Prescribed burning is, in addition, unevenly practised. As \citet{muller_forest_2020} report, it is applied at scale in France, where 1{,}000 to 3{,}000\,ha are treated annually in the Alpes-Maritimes by trained technicians working to shared protocols. This happens in the Mediterranean south-east rather than in the Alpine region proper, and serves grazing recovery as much as hazard reduction. The technique, the training structure and the institutional model therefore exist within an Alpine country but have not been transferred to Alpine conditions.

Prescribed burning has been regarded as a poor fit for the Alps, and the structural reasons are real, because the dominant species have little fire adaptation. Silver fir, Norway spruce and especially beech rank lowest in the bark insulation that \citet{mutterer_bark_2026} measured experimentally, and the simulations of \citet{dupire_vulnerability_2019} for the French Alps give thin-barked beech and silver fir the highest post-fire mortality. Prescribed burning is little used or prohibited in most Alpine countries, and the early-spring conditions under which it would be carried out are those of the March--April peak of human-caused fires \citep{muller_forest_2020}, so we expect the usable burn window to be narrow. The consequences of an escape would also be severe, because the slopes that need treatment lie above assets. In Austria the obstacle is also legal, since the Forestry Act prohibits lighting fires in forest and prescribed burning is not considered a management option \citep{neumann_fuel_2022}. Moreover, burn severity appears to be a poor guide to soil effects: after one Alpine wildfire in Scots pine, \citet{negri_wildfire_2026} found the most water-repellent surface soils in low-severity plots, although fire had on the whole lowered the naturally high repellency of these soils.

An Alpine trial now exists, however, and its results are more favourable than the structural argument would predict. In a south-west Alpine Scots pine stand at 1600\,m on a south-facing slope, \citet{gamba_prescribed_2025} monitored eighteen treated and control plots at four points over a year, applying burning under manipulated fuel-intensity gradients. Fuel load fell by roughly half, whereas branch hydraulic conductivity and stem water potential showed no significant change, there was no heat-induced anatomical damage, and soil organic carbon and water repellency changed minimally, even though the trial spanned a severe drought. On this evidence, prescribed burning appears to be an ecologically sustainable fuel-reduction option for this forest type.

Evidence from soil organisms points the same way, though from outside Europe. In paired southern Appalachian watersheds, \citet{snyder_taxonomic_2026} found the bacterial and fungal communities of repeatedly prescribed-burnt stands to be statistically indistinguishable from those of fire-excluded stands, whereas wildfire-affected and partially harvested stands differed significantly from both. Ectomycorrhizal abundance held at 63 to 66\% under prescribed fire and exclusion but fell to 38\% after wildfire, with saprotrophs rising correspondingly. Because the comparison is confounded by differences in time since disturbance and in elevation, and the forest type is not Alpine, the result cannot be transferred directly. It does, however, identify the soil microbial community as a measurable endpoint that clearly separates prescribed fire from wildfire, which the Alpine prescribed-burning question currently lacks.

Meanwhile, one European mountain range has moved beyond the trial stage, in a setting closer to the Alps than the Mediterranean cases usually cited. As described by \citet{oliveres_path_2025}, the Aran valley in the Spanish Pyrenees covers some 634\,km$^2$, with a mean altitude of 1{,}840\,m and a mean slope of 27\degree{}, and has an Atlantic rather than a Mediterranean climate with a winter-dominant fire season from January to March. In 2022 the valley unanimously adopted a strategic fire management plan built around an explicit target for how much fire the landscape should carry, set at 0.66\% of natural land cover annually against a historical fire rotation of about 350 years. Its first implementation burnt 327\,ha over fourteen working days inside a pre-planned 346\,ha management area. In our view, the valley's winter and early spring burning windows, its transhumance history (in which shepherds used fire routinely) and its deep organic soils (in which fire can smoulder and reignite) all transfer directly to the Alps. These organic soils matter in particular because smouldering ground fire is the suppression problem that Alpine lightning fires present. Yet the Pyrenean case also suggests that adoption depends mainly on things other than ecological evidence. The plan rests on a community participation process with municipal representatives, on eight formally agreed goals covering severity reduction, emergency resilience, extensive livestock farming and biodiversity, and on landscape fire succession modelling to locate treatments \citep{oliveres_path_2025}. Its authors acknowledge, however, that economic sustainability has not yet been assessed, that managed fire can escape, and that explicit wildfire use under technical and strategic direction remains far from common European practice.

We think the structural objections and the favourable trial can be reconciled, and that doing so defines a research agenda. Whereas the favourable result comes from a fire-tolerant species, at a single elevation, on one aspect, at plot scale and over one year, the structural concerns apply to fire-intolerant stands, to operational scale, to escapes and to multi-year soil response. We therefore read the trial as showing that the question can be answered empirically in the Alps rather than that it has been answered, and the structural argument as indicating where the next trials should not begin.

In Mediterranean and continental fire management, the standard landscape instrument for limiting fire size is linear, and its forms are distinguished by how much vegetation survives treatment. Following \citet{ascoli_firebreak_2018}, a firebreak is a strip cleared to mineral soil, whereas a fuelbreak retains herbaceous and shrub cover while reducing fuel volume and flammability, and a shaded fuelbreak also retains the tree cover, with grass, shrubs and trees all kept at reduced density. This distinction matters because it determines whether the instrument is compatible with an Alpine protection forest.

On that criterion, a form suited to mountains already exists and is recommended. Linear and shaded fuelbreaks are considered better suited than cleared firebreaks to mountainous terrain with steep slopes and rough morphology, because retaining vegetation cover limits soil erosion \citep{ascoli_firebreak_2018}, and where terrain is very steep, ridgelines and valley bottoms are the preferred locations. Recommended widths run from 30 to 100\,m for primary networks and from 3\,m to over 50\,m for secondary and tertiary ones, and siting criteria already include maintenance cost, road access and firefighting considerations. European Mediterranean practice adds detailed prescriptions at the interface, where \citet{rigolot_recommendations_2003} distinguish an inner zone, in which trees are removed, from an outer zone, in which they are retained but pruned and thinned, with thresholds for shrub phytovolume and crown separation. In the Alpine literature we reviewed, however, we found no equivalent design guidance calibrated to Alpine fuels, slopes and protective requirements, which we interpret as a gap in application rather than in principle.

What such a network could deliver in the Alps is nonetheless limited, and the limits can be quantified, since linear barriers are less effective than their prominence in policy suggests. As reviewed by \citet{zong_effectiveness_2026}, firebreaks stopped only 28\% of large fires in southern California between 2017 and 2020, and some 73\% of wildfires in western Arnhem Land (northern Australia) breached them fully or partially between 1998 and 2001. A proposed national network in Portugal is projected to reduce fires above 500\,ha by 13\% but would still be breached in more than a third of such events. Firebrand transport across the gap is the dominant failure mechanism.

Where fuelbreaks do work, the reason runs against the usual understanding of what they do, and it points to the constraint that matters most in mountains. Analysing 563 fire-fuelbreak intersections from 220 wildfires in Andalusia, \citet{ortega_modeling_2024} found containment in 46.9\% of cases overall, with success depending strongly on whether suppression was operating on the line and rising from 6.66\% with no suppression to 15.9\% with aerial resources alone and 76.74\% with ground crews working the line. These results suggest that a fuelbreak is a platform for ground suppression rather than a barrier in its own right, whose value depends on crews reaching and holding it. In Alpine terrain, however, the slope, access and exposure conditions that motivate building a fuelbreak are the same conditions that prevent ground crews from occupying it. We consider the terrain-conditioned suppression-difficulty framework (Section~\ref{sec:governance}) the natural tool for identifying where this constraint applies, although it has not been applied to Alpine fuelbreak siting.

Geometry adds to the problem. In the Andalusian data, effectiveness also depended strongly on the angle at which fire met the line \citep{ortega_modeling_2024}: near-parallel intersections performed best, whereas without suppression support perpendicular intersections held in only 5.6\% of cases. Because Alpine fires run upslope along paths whose length predicts large-fire likelihood \citep{conedera_linking_2024}, a break aligned with the contour meets the fire run perpendicularly. Siting on ridgelines \citep{ascoli_firebreak_2018} partly resolves this, because a ridge intercepts the run at its end rather than across its flank, but it also places the treatment where it is most exposed to wind and least accessible to ground crews.

Even a well-sited network would then face obstacles of ownership and finance. Large, long linear networks are considered poorly suited where the wildland-urban interface is widespread and forest property is largely private (with small cadastral parcels) \citep{ascoli_firebreak_2018}, a description that fits much of the Alpine arc. The Catalan experience quantifies the resulting shortfall. \citet{gorrizmifsud_current_2026} report that after three funding calls, prevention subsidies had reached under 4\% of the designated strategic area, and that funded plots within groups lay an average minimum distance of 287\,m apart, so the spatial continuity the scheme required was achieved only in some cases. Nor does the financing model transfer. In their optimisation of a 3{,}538\,km network in the north-western United States, \citet{ager_optimizing_2023} found that about 97\% of the available network area required thinning with pile burning, with estimated net harvest revenue of US\$63--75 million before planning, road and maintenance costs. Because Alpine harvesting in steep terrain is technically demanding and economically marginal \citep{dinca_timber_2025}, such revenue is largely unavailable.

A trade-off specific to protection forests remains, and the shaded form does not remove it, since even a treatment that retains the canopy reduces the density of standing stems that slow falling blocks \citep{moos_climate_2021} and removes the lying material that adds a barrier effect \citep{costa_biological_2021}. Because, following \citet{accastello_asforesee_2019}, protective capacity can be valued by its replacement cost (with figures given in Section~\ref{sec:economics}), the acceptable degree of thinning is an empirical question with a monetary answer that no study has yet computed. On the evidence assembled here, we regard the most tractable unanswered question in Alpine fuel management as how much understorey and stem removal a protection forest can absorb before its protective function declines faster than its fire hazard.

Some support for these instruments comes from vulnerability rather than suppression, and it applies at a different scale. In the expert weighting described earlier (Section~\ref{sec:risk}), protective strips and firebreaks ranked highest among the building-environment indicators, but that judgement concerns the building rather than the landscape. A break next to a structure serves as a defensible margin, which is not the containment function evaluated in the effectiveness literature, and terrain that makes a landscape-scale line impossible to hold does not prevent a cleared margin around a house. Separating the two uses therefore resolves, in our view, the apparent contradiction between an instrument that performs poorly at landscape scale and one that practitioners rank first at the scale of the exposed asset.

Taken together, our reading is that Alpine fuel management is likely to be mainly area-based rather than linear, with linear elements confined to ridgelines, existing road corridors and the interface. The evidence supports grazing that reduces fuel across whole abandoned slopes \citep{rouetleduc_effects_2021}, thinning that removes fuel and may also ease drought stress \citep{giuggiola_droughtinduced_2010}, prescribed burning where the stand tolerates it \citep{gamba_prescribed_2025}, and prioritisation of the terrain positions from which large fires actually develop \citep{conedera_linking_2024}. Multi-criteria zoning of the kind that \citet{krsnik_spatial_2024} developed for Catalonia offers one way to set such priorities.

Post-fire management determines how long the consequence window described earlier in this section stays open, yet it is the least evidenced phase of the Alpine fire cycle. This deficit applies to fire science as a whole, not only to the Alps: in a bibliometric analysis, \citet{goncalves_fostering_2025} count roughly 3{,}976 post-fire publications against 5{,}802 for prevention and 7{,}041 for active fire, so that the recovery phase is around 46\% less studied than the pre-fire phase. The deficit is also institutional, and it has now been described at European level. \citet{vieira_postfire_2026} identify post-fire management as the least developed component of the fire management cycle across the European Union and locate the obstacle in a gap between available science and operational practice rather than in a lack of tools, since stabilisation measures, restoration techniques and monitoring protocols all exist. What is missing is a common assessment procedure, multidisciplinary operational teams, long-term funding and knowledge exchange between member states. That diagnosis draws on Greek, Portuguese and Spanish experience, so its transfer to the Alps remains an open question. We expect the Alpine case to be the harder one, because the intervention must prevent gravitational rather than agronomic consequences, within a window set by the recovery of protective structure.

Existing Alpine evidence is critical of current practice. Analysing short-term recovery after a large mixed-severity fire in the Western Alps, \citet{mantero_short-term_2023} concluded that the restoration techniques most often applied are poorly matched to the ecological context and to the consequences of global change. Intervention can either help recovery or cause further degradation. Restoration must also work within the biological limits described above, since drought limits recruitment at low and mid elevations \citep{moser_potential_2010} and succession at the upper margin may be arrested altogether \citep{degiuli_arrested_2024}. Salvage logging after fire is the decision on which the Alpine evidence is thinnest and the temptation to import evidence from windthrow is strongest. The windthrow literature advises retaining fallen stems for their barrier effect \citep{costa_biological_2021}. As discussed above, however, fire does not leave that legacy, and the salvage consensus is itself less settled than often assumed \citep{seben_impact_2024}. It follows that post-fire salvage in an Alpine protection forest is currently decided without directly relevant evidence, even though the decision determines both the immediate barrier effect and the fuel load available to the next fire.

\section{Risk, Exposure and Vulnerability}
\label{sec:risk}

\subsection{From hazard to risk: the missing terms}

A single-hazard wildfire assessment asks where fire is likely and how large it might be, a question that in the Alps stops short of what matters, because burnt area is small and only weakly related to consequence and because the exposed asset usually lies outside the burn perimeter but beneath it. Susceptibility, correctly defined, is the static probability that a place experiences fire given the intrinsic characteristics of the territory \citep{trucchia_defining_2022}. We regard it as a legitimate quantity. In mountains, however, the gap between susceptibility and risk is unusually wide. Risk combines hazard, exposure and vulnerability, and Alpine fire science has so far developed only the first. Hazard (the probability and intensity of fire) is reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:hazard}, whereas exposure describes what lies where fire and its consequences reach, and vulnerability the susceptibility of those elements to loss once exposed. In the natural hazards literature, vulnerability is defined by the conditions that make an exposed asset susceptible to damage \citep{papathomakohle_towards_2025}, and climate-risk assessments such as that of \citet{pede_mountain_2022} index hazard, exposure and vulnerability with separate indicators. Because neither has been assembled for the Alpine arc, Alpine assessments end at a hazard surface that is then read as if it were a risk map.

Both missing terms have instead been developed in a separate literature, with its own instruments and data requirements. Where damage records are absent, indicator sets are weighted by expert judgement \citep{papathomakohle_towards_2025}, whereas where such records exist, indices are calibrated on surveyed damage \citep{papathomakohle_wildfire_2022}. For the ecological component, the continental mapping of \citet{arrogantefunes_mapping_2024} integrates lost ecological value over the recovery period. None of these has yet been joined to an Alpine hazard surface, and we regard this as the gap that most directly separates the current Alpine literature from a usable risk assessment. Closing it would mean combining layers that already exist rather than improving any one of them (Section~\ref{sec:governance}).

\subsection{Exposure: habitats, assets, people and seasonality}

Exposure in mountains is neither static nor confined to the burn perimeter. Its ecological component is the one part already measured at continental scale, and the measured share is large. Of the 1{,}079{,}538\,ha burnt in the European Union in 2025 (the largest total in the European record), 424{,}023\,ha lay inside Natura 2000 sites, which is 39\% of the total and the highest share ever recorded, three times the 2024 figure and twice that of 2023. Spain, Portugal and Romania accounted for two thirds of it \citep{jrc_advance_2026}. European burning therefore does not touch the protected-area network only incidentally, since roughly two of every five hectares burnt lie inside it.

For the Alps, the same accounting is informative for a different reason. The four fires mapped in Austria in 2025 totalled 434\,ha, of which 420.6\,ha lay inside Natura 2000 sites and 334\,ha was agricultural land rather than forest \citep{jrc_advance_2026}. Where mapped Alpine fire occurs, it thus coincides almost exactly with the protected-area network, and it falls on abandoned or semi-natural agricultural ground rather than on the closed forest in which the fuel accumulation described in Section~\ref{sec:forest} is concentrated. However, these four fires are a small fraction of the Austrian annual fire count (Section~\ref{sec:data}). We therefore read the coincidence as established on the detected tail rather than on the regime, and the ecological exposure of the Alpine arc as a whole remains unmeasured, for the same reason that its fire regime does. Since much of what is exposed lies beyond the burnt area, we argue that exposure analysis must follow the cascade rather than the fire, and in this respect the vulnerability literature appears better equipped than the hazard literature. In south-eastern France, for example, \citet{riviere_mapping_2023} used a participative multi-criteria approach to distinguish exposure, sensitivity and adaptive capacity across populations, ecosystems and infrastructure. In doing so, they identified secondary vulnerability hotspots, as well as locations not yet affected by hazard where prevention was nonetheless warranted.

Exposure also changes over time, because human presence in Alpine forests is tourism-driven and seasonal. For one Piedmont zone, \citet{pede_mountain_2022} combine projections to 2050 of shorter high-altitude snow cover with an opportunity to attract summer visitors escaping lowland heat. The same assessment expects local fire danger to rise mainly in spring, whereas recorded burnt area there peaks in January, February and November \citep{pede_mountain_2022}, so how far visitor presence and the fire season will overlap remains open. Across five European pilot sites, \citet{gelabert_assessing_2025} found distance to the wildland--urban interface to be the most relevant human predictor in site-level ignition models. However, these models treat their human covariates as static, and their authors recommend that they vary with commuting and seasonal recreation. Exposure estimates may also depend on institutions: in the Saxon--Bohemian borderland, the transboundary simulation of \citet{avouris_exposure_2025} found modelled burn probability and flame length at settlements differing across a national border that fire does not recognise.

Compound structure enters the exposure picture at several points, which we keep apart here because conflating them has caused confusion. Preconditions compound, since European fire activity typically follows moderate but coincident hydro-meteorological and land-surface anomalies rather than single extremes \citep{miller_compounding_2025}. Disturbances compound as well, because drought and fresh windthrow deadwood favour bark beetle outbreaks in Alpine forests \citep{bebi_changes_2017}, and the resulting dead material loads the fuel bed before any fire arrives; the Alpine hazard projection of \citet{gerberding_mapping_2025} acknowledges this pathway but does not represent it. Consequences compound too, in the post-fire cascade of Section~\ref{sec:water}, which \citet{baselt_compound_2026} address in a research roadmap that treats post-fire and frozen-soil dynamics together and calls for multi-institutional monitoring networks and for governance linking geoscientists, engineers and policymakers.

Exposure of linear infrastructure to this compound cascade has been quantified, but not in Europe. Across California, \citet{moftakhari_increasing_2019} project that the likelihood of exceeding the historic mean coincidence of intense rainfall over burnt ground rises from 38\% to 68\% and 73\% under moderate and high emissions pathways, respectively. For the upper decile of events, it rises from 34\% to 90\% and 91\%, so that the likelihood that natural gas transmission in California faces this compound hazard is projected nearly to double. Although the coefficients would not carry over, the method is likely to transfer, yet an Alpine equivalent (covering roads, rail and pipelines beneath burnt slopes in confined valleys) has not been attempted.

\subsection{Vulnerability of the built environment}

For the Alps, building vulnerability has so far been constructed from expert judgement, and the exercise shows what is missing. \citet{papathomakohle_towards_2025} developed an Austrian index that scores buildings in the wildland--urban interface on twenty-one indicators, grouped as building environment, building exterior and building characteristics, and weighted these indicators by pairwise comparison across a national and an international expert panel. The index was then applied to twenty-three buildings in a Carinthian settlement on the southern slope of the Dobratsch. Both panels ranked protective strips or firebreaks first among the six environment indicators (weights of 0.286 and 0.198), the distance of tree crowns from the building first among the seven exterior indicators (0.258 and 0.212), and roof material first among the eight building indicators (0.203 and 0.164). These weights rest on expert judgement because no Austrian wildfire damage record exists from which to derive them.

Where a damage record does exist, the weights differ, and we find the difference informative. \citet{papathomakohle_wildfire_2022} calibrated an index on 423 buildings surveyed after a wildland--urban interface fire in Attica that killed 102 people and destroyed 1{,}200 buildings; it retained eight of thirteen candidate indicators and ranked roof material first, structural type second and terrain slope third, whereas both Austrian panels gave terrain slope a low weight. Even so, the two indices agree on roof material, and the physical reason is known. Structures ignite by wind-blown embers, radiant heat or direct flame contact. Because most buildings are lost to embers and surface fire rather than to a flame front reaching the wall, roofing and other building materials, and the vents, openings and eaves through which embers enter or lodge, matter alongside proximity to vegetation \citep{naser_vulnerability_2025}. Since roof material is stable across the two settings, it is the one indicator that an Alpine programme could adopt without local calibration. Slope, which the Alps have in abundance, is where the transfer is most likely to fail. Preferring either the judgement-based or the damage-based weighting does not resolve this, because the two disagree on the variable that most distinguishes the Alpine setting from the Mediterranean one in which the damage record was collected.

Where fire does reach structures directly, damage is shaped in part by the same wind interaction that terrain amplifies. As reviewed by \citet{ghodrat_existing_2021}, simulation of fire--wind interaction covers the fanning effect of wind, the combination of wind direction with slope angle, and the effects of fire--wind interaction on buildings as fire passes from wildland into the wildland--urban interface, although the same review notes that disturbance interactions affecting structures have been comparatively neglected. Alpine settlements sit in valleys where slope and channelled wind routinely combine, which suggests that this neglected interaction may bear directly on them.

\section{Governance, Policy and Economics}
\label{sec:governance}

\subsection{Suppression, preparedness and the practitioner constraint}

Imported suppression doctrine falters in the Alps before a crew is even dispatched, because it defines an extreme wildfire by size and containment failure rather than by downslope consequence. Whereas standard European practice understands an extreme event as one that burns thousands of hectares beyond suppression capacity and stops only when fuel runs out or the weather turns, in the Alps a fire of a few hectares is operationally extreme if its severity removes the protective forest above a settlement or transport corridor \citep{muller_forest_2020}. Escalation rules calibrated on burnt-area thresholds are therefore likely to miss the events that matter most on steep Alpine slopes. Once crews are deployed, Alpine suppression operates under compound terrain constraints. Relief makes fire behaviour harder to anticipate (a mechanism established mainly outside Europe \citep{sharples_overview_2009} and documented in the Alps for one steep-valley fire \citep{melzner_brief_2019}), while steep, remote ground restricts access \citep{muller_forest_2020}. Suppression also depends on shared aerial resources, and because drought and vapour pressure deficit are the dominant drivers of European wildfire activity and fire weather is shaped by synoptic weather patterns \citep{miller_compounding_2025}, demand for helicopters is likely to peak across the arc at the same moment.

In practice, these constraints produce a suppression system organised around water logistics and vertical distance, although ground and surface fires are still attacked directly at the front or flanks \citep{muller_forest_2020}. Where vehicles can reach, crews attack from tank tenders using ponds, hydrants and long hose lines, inserting additional pumps to overcome large differences in height, whereas where vehicles cannot reach, initial attack on ground fire uses rakes and beaters, and subsurface hot spots are reached with backpack pumps and high-pressure lances. Vehicles are chosen to suit the road rather than the fire; Italian forest-fire units, for example, use all-wheel-drive pickups sized for narrow Alpine forest roads instead of the heavier tankers used on open ground. In the southern Alps, sprinkler lines wet vegetation along fuel breaks, particularly at night. Tactical or counter-firing during an incident (as distinct from prescribed burning for prevention) is practised mainly in France and Italy and is uncommon elsewhere in the range.

Aerial support is a structural part of this system rather than an add-on, and it serves a different function than in the Mediterranean: instead of laying retardant along a long front, helicopters in Austria and Bavaria increasingly ferry water to mobile basins near the fire, from which ground crews lay hose. Every Alpine country maintains units trained for bucket work and winch insertion. Road density is not the main limitation, since forest road density in the Alpine region is high compared with Mediterranean or Scandinavian Europe, although road practicability and barriers to vehicles repeatedly cause problems \citep{muller_forest_2020}. Thus, the constraint is vertical rather than horizontal, which corrects an assumption that is easily imported from Mediterranean practice, since what limits access is the slope, exposure and vertical separation between the road and the fire. \citet{cardil_assessing_2025} approximate this with a terrain difficulty index, calibrated on Californian initial attack, that combines slope, fuel penetrability and travel time along the road network.

Alpine crews also face different hazards. Rockfall and falls are ordinary features of the working environment, and burning material rolling downslope ignites new fires below crews working above it, while in sectors of the range where the two world wars were fought, buried munition remains a recognised hazard. Where terrain is steep enough, mountain rescue teams join the action force to secure the firefighters themselves \citep{muller_forest_2020}. Confined topography adds a behavioural hazard, since canyons can produce sharp increases in fire intensity without any increase in wind \citep{muller_forest_2020, hayajneh_wind_2026}. We therefore doubt that a suppression doctrine written for terrain where the main danger is the fire front can describe this working environment.

Organisation also differs, and this is the part of the Alpine system least represented in the modelling literature. In Austria and Switzerland, dense networks of volunteer brigades and a population that reliably reports smoke by telephone mean that most fires are attacked soon after ignition \citep{muller_forest_2020}. We take this to be part of the explanation for a fire-size distribution dominated by events under a hectare, although the inference has not been tested; the same fire weather in a range without that response density would plausibly produce a different distribution, yet no Alpine hazard model contains a term for it. This capability also carries a demographic exposure of its own, because it rests on voluntary participation in valley communities that are ageing and shrinking (Section~\ref{sec:economics}).

A quantitative framework for the accessibility constraint now exists, although it has not yet been applied in the Alps. To assess initial attack, \citet{cardil_assessing_2025} combine a fire behaviour index with a terrain difficulty index built from accessibility, penetrability, line-construction difficulty, mean slope and road proximity. In its highest class, the odds of successful initial attack are 90\% lower than in the lowest, and mean burnt area rises from 18.5 to 1731\,ha across that range, while response time interacts with terrain difficulty to reduce containment probability further. Because the framework has been validated outside its region of origin, we see the Alpine gap as one of application rather than of method, and one that could be closed. Since suppression cannot be fast everywhere, it would need to be fast in the right places, and these places can be identified in advance from terrain and fuel connectivity, which is the operational case for targeted pre-suppression infrastructure such as water points for aerial firefighting \citep{conedera_linking_2024}.

How suppression capability and fuel treatment interact matters in a range whose danger distribution is shifting. Fuel treatment reduces the severity that suppression crews have to confront, although its benefit for tree mortality is greatest under intermediate fire weather and declines under the most severe conditions \citep{brodie_forest_2024}. In contrast, initial-attack success falls steeply with terrain difficulty in Californian data (an effect separate from that of fire behaviour) \citep{cardil_assessing_2025}. In accessible Alpine terrain under moderate danger, we would therefore expect the two to act as substitutes, so that the cheaper one can be chosen. On steep slopes with long, continuous forested upslope runs, where most large Swiss fires have spread rapidly and proved hard to suppress \citep{conedera_linking_2024}, suppression is weak and treatment is the only lever that acts before the fire. These are also the sites where mechanised treatment costs most \citep{dinca_timber_2025}, which is why the pastoral and silvicultural instruments of Section~\ref{sec:forest} are likely to matter most there.

Alpine fire management currently spends several times more on response than an integrated prevention framework would cost, a ratio that Section~\ref{sec:economics} gives together with econometric evidence from Italian regions pointing the same way. What no Alpine study provides, however, is the quantity that would settle the allocation, namely the marginal effectiveness of a euro spent on suppression capability against a euro spent on fuel treatment, conditioned on terrain. Beyond allocation, the strategic question is how much of the projected increase management can absorb, and the continent-scale projections introduced in Section~\ref{sec:intro} set limits on the answer. \citet{billing_future_2026} found that sustained investment in capacity offsets most of the climate-driven increase in burnt area but may not offset all of it under strong warming. Even with rising capacity, one of their two models still projects increases of 200 to 300\% along the Alpine foothills under high emissions \citep{billing_future_2026}. For the Alps, we therefore regard investment in capacity as the largest single lever within the region's control rather than a marginal adjustment, although it is unlikely to be sufficient on its own in the foothills.

\subsection{Prevention: placement, early warning and integrated fire management}

Section~\ref{sec:forest} set out the instruments of Alpine prevention, so the governance question here is whether they are used where they are needed. Fire-smart management is already the operational focus in Mediterranean regions, whereas in the currently less affected Alps the case for building a pre-fire capability rests on anticipation rather than experience \citep{gerberding_mapping_2025}. Allocation is therefore a political as well as a technical problem, and the Alpine evidence on it is discouraging. Mapping 97 nature-based solutions for climate adaptation across the European Alps, \citet{dubo_nature-based_2023} found that most addressed drought or soil instability and aimed to deliver several benefits at once. Yet they were generally not located within hotspots of the hazards they claimed to address, nor within hotspots of the ecosystem contributions they aimed to secure, nor where the two coincided. This suggests that measures are being placed where they are institutionally feasible rather than where they are physically needed. Closing that gap would require local climate projections and quantified ecosystem contributions to be integrated into planning, through a macro-regional strategy combined with local stakeholder engagement.

A low-cost correction is nonetheless available. \citet{conedera_linking_2024} showed that large-fire likelihood in mountain terrain can be mapped from slope length and fuel connectivity using only a digital elevation model and a forest map, and \citet{accastello_asforesee_2019} value protective function per hectare by replacement cost. The two layers needed to rank candidate treatment sites by expected avoided loss therefore already exist for most of the Alpine arc. In our assessment, what is missing is the institutional mandate to combine them, because the hazard layer sits with fire and forest services and the value layer with the authorities responsible for the assets below.

For the prioritisation itself, a transferable method already exists. In a multi-criteria analysis of some 238{,}000 forest polygons across Catalonia, \citet{krsnik_spatial_2024} ranked management zones on fuel hazard, simulated fire behaviour, exposure of settlements and infrastructure, and landscape continuity. They also ranked them on accessibility (the criterion that matters most in mountains), expressed through terrain aspect, road density and distance to water points and strategic management areas. We consider the method directly portable to Alpine conditions, where accessibility is the binding constraint on suppression, and its sensitivity is also instructive: expert weighting designated 20.69\% of the territory as high priority, whereas equal weighting designated 7.98\%, which suggests that the participatory step shapes the result as much as the data do.

In our view, the most tractable near-term gain is not a new Alpine model but the extension of early warning systems that already exist. Although the Alps have dense hydro-meteorological monitoring, mature torrent and avalanche warning services and long institutional experience of gravitational hazards, these systems lack a fire term. The concrete proposal in the Alpine literature, made by \citet{tiranti_wildfires_2021}, is to adjust catchment runoff parameters automatically for burnt catchments, so that a debris-flow early warning system lowers its rainfall thresholds without waiting for a field survey. The thematic layers this requires (catchment and fan delimitation, geology, bedrock outcrop, land use and satellite-derived fire perimeters) are already available in geographic information systems. A single event, the Bussoleno debris flow analysed by \citet{laporta_postwildfire_2025}, suggests why this is needed, because its radar-estimated triggering intensity appears to have fallen below the threshold that a system designed for unburnt basins would have been watching for.

How such a system should be designed is also informed by the modelling evidence. We expect its outputs to be most useful as decision thresholds rather than probability surfaces, with the asymmetry between missed detections and false alarms built into the specification rather than considered afterwards. Alpine ignition modelling has already been built this way, since the preprint of \citet{moreno_spacetime_2025} derives dynamic thresholds from combinations of true-positive and false-alarm rates, and integrated danger assessment has likewise reported false-alert performance as a main result \citep{muller_towards_2020}. We emphasise this design choice because a missed detection can mean an unwarned slope above a village.

For the same reason, integrated fire management is best specified around indicators that different institutions can each observe and act on, which is exactly what the Alpine post-fire inventory work of \citet{melzner_wildfire-induced_2025} proposes. Their work identifies indicators for burn severity and for subsequent geohydrological processes, sets out systematic documentation procedures, and is framed for use by policymakers within an integrated fire management strategy rather than by researchers. The costed Alpine framework already allocates between a tenth and a fifth of a proposed annual budget to post-fire management and a further share to knowledge transfer \citep{muller_forest_2020}, while the compound-hazard roadmap of \citet{baselt_compound_2026} outlines the monitoring options from which networks supplying the indicators could be built. Each component has thus been proposed separately, and what is missing, as we see it, is their assembly. Indicators also need targets against which progress can be read, and these are still largely missing from European wildfire risk management, although they are better established for flood risk \citep{berchtold_towards_2025}. Portugal is the clearest exception, since its 2021 National Action Plan sets measurable targets, among them keeping fires larger than 500\,ha below 0.3\% of all fires. Where fire has been rare or long suppressed, however, historical fire data are scarce or unreliable, so such targets would have to rest on local assessments of fuel, exposure and vulnerability rather than on past fire statistics alone \citep{berchtold_towards_2025}.

\subsection{Fragmentation, harmonisation and cross-border coordination}

At every link, the Alpine wildfire cascade crosses an institutional boundary. Prevention sits with forest administrations and suppression with fire services and civil protection, whereas post-fire rockfall and torrent control sit with geological surveys and infrastructure operators, and the land-use decisions that govern fuel continuity sit with agricultural and planning authorities. The sequence described in Section~\ref{sec:water} passes through all four. Fire drivers also differ between countries, which is one reason a single Alpine prescription is unlikely to fit. Rural abandonment dominates in France and Italy, and tourist activity in Austria, Germany and Switzerland, while negligence is the leading proximate cause throughout \citep{muller_forest_2020}. Prevention that works through agricultural policy in the western and southern Alps would therefore have to work through visitor management in the north and east.

Fragmentation extends to basic facts. Surveying three stakeholder groups across seven Alpine countries, \citet{hoang_stakeholders_2026} found significant differences in perceptions of fire numbers, burnt area and future trend. These differences persisted even though the groups agreed that precipitation, temperature and wind are the dominant natural drivers and that recreation, rural abandonment and owner awareness are the dominant socio-economic ones. In our view, a practitioner community that does not share a factual picture of the hazard is poorly prepared for its growth. How this plays out where citizens have formal standing has been surveyed in one Alpine setting. In the Locarnese region of Ticino, \citet{bardsley_wildfire_2021} found that residents valued local amenity highly and strongly supported firefighting services, while judging that the main driver of risk at the forest and settlement interface is the current level of urban expansion rather than the hazard itself. Although the planning system is built on deliberative instruments, younger, locally rooted and less formally educated respondents were the least satisfied with their influence over it, and the availability of popular referenda was judged to foster a disproportionate sense of entitlement over local decisions. Although this evidence comes from a single setting, it suggests that, in a range where land-use decisions govern fuel continuity, public support for suppression capacity is not evidence of support for the land-use restraint that would reduce exposure. Moreover, formal participation rights do not appear to distribute influence evenly among those who carry the risk.

Practitioners within a single Alpine country give the same diagnosis when asked directly. In a structured survey of 79 wildfire experts across seven Italian regions (three of them Alpine), \citet{kirschner_governance_2024} found the institutional structure highly fragmented, with responsibility increasingly spread across separate organisations, and emergency response persisting as the default strategy despite formal commitments to precaution. Respondents reported that local stakeholder participation had been displaced by a shift towards extra-local actors, and judged state-level resources disproportionate to regional capacity. Prevention dominated both halves of the assessment, with 46\% of the coded shortcomings concerning prevention deficits and 58\% of the identified opportunities concerning enhanced prevention. That one theme dominates both what is missing and what is possible is, we suggest, the clearest available evidence that the constraint is institutional rather than technical.

Fragmentation is also visible one level up, in the European policy framework itself. Reviewing forty EU sectoral policies and initiatives across seven clusters, \citet{plana_framing_2024} found integrated fire management (or a homologous term) named seven times in the whole corpus, and only once, in passing, in a law or policy text. The term extreme wildfire event appeared four times in total, law and policy texts treated fire only as a threat without its beneficial role, and nature-based solutions appeared only marginally in the forestry and disaster-risk clusters, despite their prominence in climate and biodiversity policy. Similarly, \citet{kalapodis_towards_2025} compared European and United States arrangements for critical infrastructure and reached a parallel conclusion, identifying insufficient integration of scientific evidence into policy and recommending standardised cross-border risk assessment. We therefore read the Alpine coordination problem as lying not only in the failure of eight countries to align with one another but also in a supranational framework that barely names the practice they would align to.

Harmonisation has been attempted, but it has been driven by projects. The first harmonised Alpine fire database and regime characterisation came from a transnational project \citep{valese_wildfires_2011}, and its data layers were later reused in national danger assessment \citep{muller_towards_2020}. A later EUSALP project (drawing on an expert panel, a multilingual survey and a workshop) produced an Alpine-scale white paper that sets out prevention, suppression, post-fire and knowledge-transfer priorities with costed estimates \citep{muller_forest_2020}. What does not yet exist at Alpine scale is standing infrastructure, such as the monitoring networks and post-fire documentation procedures discussed above. According to \citet{baselt_compound_2026}, compound hazard research requires those networks to combine remote sensing, in situ instrumentation and geophysical methods, and the documentation procedures are what would make indicator-based integrated fire management operable \citep{melzner_wildfire-induced_2025}. At a central European transboundary interface, \citet{avouris_exposure_2025} have shown directly that fire crosses national borders (Section~\ref{sec:risk}).

Standing coordination across the jurisdictions of a mountain range does exist elsewhere, and the one documented case shows both what it can achieve and where it stops. As described by \citet{lockwood_biodiversity_2014}, the protected areas of the Australian Alps fall under the legislation of three states and territories, and since 1986 a memorandum of understanding between four governments has supported a cooperative management programme coordinated by the Australian Alps Liaison Committee. This programme treats the range as one biogeographic region for fire management, water and catchments, invasive species and Aboriginal engagement, and it has survived political and funding cycles and major fire events. However, coordination did not insulate fire policy from politics. After the 2009 Victorian fires, a hazard-reduction burning target of 5\% of public land per year became state policy, although such burning is not undertaken in the treeless alpine landscape. In 2010, cattle were returned to the Victorian Alps for a trial of their contribution to reducing fire risk, despite published evidence suggesting that alpine grazing has no significant effect on it. The federal government later removed them on the strength of the range's national heritage listing. Although the case is single and imported, it suggests that a standing body can align agencies without settling the land-use disputes that fuel management raises.

\subsection{The economics of Alpine wildfire}
\label{sec:economics}

Alpine wildfire economics run against intuition, because what the forest protects is worth more than what it produces, and prevention costs little beside either. Protective function dominates the value at risk. \citet{accastello_asforesee_2019} value a ten-hectare Alpine Scots pine protection stand by replacement cost at €30{,}440 per hectare, or €950 per hectare per year. In the avoided-risk valuation of a Valais protection forest by \citet{moos_climate_2021}, annual rockfall risk is 19{,}100\,CHF with the forest and 95{,}500\,CHF without it, and under moderate and high emissions scenarios it rises to 59{,}250 and 70{,}100\,CHF per year, respectively, within 150 years as forest composition shifts (Section~\ref{sec:forest}). Neither figure is a timber value, and in terrain where extraction is technically demanding and economically marginal \citep{dinca_timber_2025}, the timber value is frequently the smaller number.

On the expenditure side, response dominates. Direct firefighting and post-fire management across the Alpine arc are estimated at around €75 million a year, whereas a proposed integrated fire management framework is costed at roughly €10 million a year, of which €5--7 million would go to prevention, €1.5--2.5 million to suppression capability, €1--2 million to post-fire management and €0.5 million to knowledge transfer \citep{muller_forest_2020}. Whatever the precision of these estimates, we take the ratio to frame prevention as inexpensive relative to the response it would displace. In an econometric analysis of Italian regions that projects more frequent fires under climate change, \citet{michetti_forest_2019} reached the same recommendation from a different direction, namely that investing in prevention would be more efficient than suppression.

Their analysis also gives the clearest quantitative statement of how socio-economic and climatic drivers combine in a European mountain setting. Across nineteen Italian regions, a ten-millimetre increase in monthly precipitation reduced fire frequency by 8.9\% in the same month, with effects persisting longer in the north than in the south, whereas a one-degree temperature increase raised frequency by up to 15\% in the south and by between 0.9\% and 5.7\% elsewhere. Socio-economic terms were of comparable size: a one-percentage-point rise in tertiary education reduced fire events by 11\% in the north, and in the south a single additional extortion per hundred thousand inhabitants raised fire frequency by 4.6\% and burnt area by 7\%. Projected to 2016--2035, the northern cluster gains 11--12\% in annual fire events and 23--27\% in annual burnt area \citep{michetti_forest_2019}. These results suggest that education and institutional quality are fire management variables, although they are not usually treated as such.

Delivery is a further problem, which fragmented Alpine forest ownership shares with the one case in which it has been measured, in Catalonia. There, \citet{gorrizmifsud_current_2026} found that wildfire prevention subsidies funded by a vehicle carbon tax, offering €5{,}000 to €200{,}000 per proposal, reached only 3.87\% of the 151{,}196\,ha designated as strategic. Because the average minimum distance between subsidised parcels was 287\,m, the scheme did not achieve the spatial continuity it was designed to create, and the barriers identified were non-adjacent parcels, uneven social capital between owner associations, and reimbursement rules that required owners to advance costs. Taken together, these findings suggest that fuel treatment that must be contiguous to work is being funded through instruments that allocate to whoever applies.

Structural incentive problems identified in the economics literature have not yet been examined in Alpine studies. As reviewed by \citet{bayham_economics_2022}, a private parcel owner deciding on mitigation weighs private costs and benefits while ignoring spatial spillover externalities (such as fire spreading across parcel boundaries) and conventional externalities such as smoke. Liability rules and insurance markets then shape mitigation incentives in ways that vary by jurisdiction. In an Alpine context of small private parcels and eight national legal systems, these are likely to be first-order determinants of whether fuel treatment happens at all. Catalan subsidies designed to capture this spillover illustrate how hard it is to secure adjacent treatment even when coordination is paid for \citep{gorrizmifsud_current_2026}.

Pastoral systems sit on both sides of the Alpine ledger, and neither side has been priced. Grazing is the fuel-treatment instrument with the broadest policy reach (Section~\ref{sec:forest}). As reviewed by \citet{xu_how_2026}, it is cost-effective relative to mechanical clearance because it relies on natural animal behaviour, and, in our reading, also because the work is done by animals kept for other reasons, so that its marginal cost to a fire budget is largely the cost of sustaining a pastoral economy that already exists and is contracting. On the other side, alpine pasture, its access infrastructure and the stock on it are exposed assets, and transhumant animals are at their highest elevation during the summer fire season. We found no Alpine study that values either side: there is no estimate of the fuel-management service that extensive grazing supplies without payment, and no record of livestock or pasture loss to fire against which to set it. In the one European landscape scenario analysis available (from an Iberian transboundary reserve), \citet{lecinadiaz_incorporating_2023} found that reversing agricultural abandonment supplied almost all of the suppression-cost saving. This quantitative gap therefore bears directly on how agri-environmental payment is justified.

What the economics cannot yet resolve is distribution. Alpine valley communities are small, ageing and economically specialised, and they bear the costs of both the hazard and its prevention, while a large share of the protective benefit accrues to through-traffic, downstream water users and regions outside the mountains. As reviewed by \citet{vukomanovic_systematic_2019}, the services at stake propagate across scales, because the vegetation changes through which fire acts on them operate locally but register regionally. Risk assessment frameworks that make adaptive capacity an explicit component of vulnerability appear to come closest to representing this asymmetry \citep{riviere_mapping_2023, lecinadiaz_assessing_2021}. Tourism also sits on both sides of the ledger, since in Mediterranean evidence fires depress visits while visitors raise fire risk \citep{abrham_wildfires_2024}, and in one Italian Alpine area warming is projected to draw more summer tourists to high-altitude municipalities while fire hazard moves towards them (Section~\ref{sec:risk}) \citep{pede_mountain_2022}. We found no Alpine study that quantifies who bears the costs and who receives the benefits.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Across Sections~\ref{sec:hazard} to~\ref{sec:governance}, the same structural diagnosis emerges in every discipline: tools, monitoring products and management doctrines imported from Mediterranean or continental fire science lose skill or fail outright when confronted with Alpine reality (Table~\ref{tab:contrast}). Because these mismatches arise from distinct physical, biological and institutional mechanisms along the chain in Figure~\ref{fig:cascade}, no single statistical adjustment can resolve them. Moreover, the mismatches compound one another. Satellite detection thresholds that omit four fifths of Alpine fires starve local danger and spread models of calibration data, and this scarcity in turn encourages practitioners to borrow empirical indices, severity proxies and vulnerability weights calibrated elsewhere, together with assumptions about weather continuity, fuel origin, recovery speed and asset geometry that the Alpine evidence contradicts.

Several rows in Table~\ref{tab:contrast} reverse the sign of an operational relationship rather than merely rescaling it. Thinning a protection forest to reduce fuel can degrade the rockfall barrier it is meant to defend, and ground-crew containment is weakest on the long, steep forested runs where large-fire propensity is highest. After fire, a steep catchment can fail under a common rainstorm rather than an extreme one. Even an empirical susceptibility model transferred from a neighbouring European mountain range fails where land-use access differs, as the opposite elevation dependence of fire in the Alps and the Carpathians shows (Section~\ref{sec:hazard}).

Taken one at a time, recent Alpine trends might still invite a cautious reading, since fire danger is rising from a modest base in the north and east, fuel is accumulating gradually, most fires remain sub-hectare, and documented post-fire debris flows remain few (Section~\ref{sec:data}). However, read together, these trajectories are converging on the same slopes. The climatic envelope is expanding fastest (in relative terms) where operational fire experience is least \citep{gerberding_mapping_2025}, and the forced fire-danger signal emerges earliest where historical danger is lowest \citep{miller_climate_2024}. On the same ground, abandoned pasture and open woodland are infilling where climate permits \citep{garbarino_contrasting_2020}, which lengthens the continuous forested upslope runs that \citet{conedera_linking_2024} associated with large Swiss fires. In one Central Alpine valley, \citet{frei_evidence_2023} found that roughly four fifths of newly treed cells reflect infilling below the treeline rather than upward treeline advance. At the same time, snow-cover duration has shortened strongly below 2{,}000\,m across the Alps, with warming often amplified near the retreating snowline \citep{rangwala_climate_2012, pepin_climate_2022}, while fire elevation across European mountains has shifted upslope \citep{beloiu_climatedriven_2026}. In one Italian Alpine district, warming is also projected to draw more summer visitors to high-elevation municipalities as fire hazard rises around them \citep{pede_mountain_2022}.

Where consequence scales linearly with burnt area, a modest rise in fire activity produces a modest rise in loss. In the Alps, however, we read the shift as qualitative, because fire acts on protective forests standing above fixed, high-value valley corridors and settlements. At Bussoleno, the analysis of \citet{laporta_postwildfire_2025} suggests that fire lowered the triggering rainfall intensity so that a storm of about five-year return period produced a damaging debris flow. Post-fire alterations persist for at least three years in Alpine soil \citep{negri_wildfire_2026} and, after severe fire in beech, for at least forty years in shallow-landslide disposition \citep{gehring_shallow_2019}. Canopy closure after disturbance typically takes decades \citep{stritih_alternative_2023}, and on the one subalpine limestone massif studied, \citet{degiuli_arrested_2024} found that tree cover has not returned some eight decades after fire and may remain arrested. We therefore interpret climate and land-use change as altering how frequently Alpine fires cross the severity and location thresholds at which downslope loss escalates.

None of this implies that the hydrogeomorphic cascade dominates total Alpine wildfire loss. We have examined the cascade in detail because existing fire-hazard assessments omit it and because it breaks the return-period assumptions of Alpine engineering. Direct loss of protective stand structure, guild-specific ecological change, smoke, wildland--urban interface damage, and transport or tourism disruption all operate without subsequent rainfall, and several of them rest on broader Alpine datasets. In their arc-wide regime classification, \citet{conedera_characterizing_2018} concluded that wildfire does not currently rival windstorms and bark beetles for overall forest damage in the Alps, and we know of nothing published since that overturns that assessment for present conditions. Our claim therefore concerns the trajectory of systemic risk and the blind spots of imported burnt-area metrics, rather than any suggestion that fire already outranks wind or beetles across the arc.

If this reading is right, the appropriate response is coexistence with fire rather than its exclusion. Fire is unusual among natural hazards because the dominant institutional response is to fight it, whereas for earthquakes, floods and storms the emphasis is on identifying vulnerabilities and adapting to them; as \citet{moritz_learning_2014} argued, a command-and-control approach also neglects the role of fire regimes in sustaining biodiversity and ecosystem services, and the alternative requires place-based approaches linked to land-use planning. In this respect the Alps are in an unusual position, because they are building their fire management now rather than reforming an entrenched system. They could therefore, in principle, adopt an adaptation-led approach without first passing through a century of suppression.

These implications differ for each community that would have to act on them. For fire science, the Alpine case tests whether the discipline's tools are general or only tuned to the settings where they were built, and in our reading the evidence more often supports the second answer. Danger indices require recalibration \citep{de_angelis_modelling_2015, zacharakis_environmental_2023}, and pooled ignition models lose skill at regional level \citep{gelabert_assessing_2025}. In the one Alpine comparison, \citet{malanchini_modeling_2025} found that two spread models that both reproduced the perimeters of three fires disagreed on fireline intensity, which is the quantity that, as we read the evidence, drives the downstream processes. Similarly, severity maps failed to identify the most severely burnt soil at the one Alpine fire where this was tested \citep{negri_wildfire_2026}, and continental models are neither calibrated on the small fires that dominate the Alpine record nor evaluated in mountains separately \citep{forrest_understanding_2024}. More constructively, the Alps may offer a good test bed for tools that rely on well-chosen structure rather than on many fitted parameters, because sparse records and shifting climates limit unconstrained data-driven extrapolation \citep{jain_review_2020}.

Mountain geomorphology faces the same problem from the other side. Fire is arriving as a new preparatory factor in a field whose frameworks were built around rainfall, snowmelt, freeze--thaw and permafrost degradation. Its Alpine fire literature still consists of first inventories \citep{melzner_wildfire-induced_2025} and individual documented events \citep{tiranti_wildfires_2021, laporta_postwildfire_2025, melzner_brief_2019}. In our view, the efficient path is probably to make existing infrastructure fire-aware rather than to build a parallel system. \citet{tiranti_wildfires_2021}, for example, proposed adjusting curve-number values automatically for burnt catchments in an operating debris-flow warning system, and the successful transfer of an empirical post-fire debris-flow likelihood model from the United States to one Greek fire \citep{diakakis_exploring_2023} suggests that such adaptation is feasible.

For climate adaptation, the implications are less comfortable. \citet{dubo_nature-based_2023} found that Alpine nature-based solutions are mostly not located where their target hazards concentrate (those addressing drought and floods being the exceptions), which warns against planning that starts from institutional feasibility rather than from hazard. Several adaptation and mitigation policies (among them carbon sequestration, reduced management intensity and protective forest expansion) also add standing biomass and fuel continuity (Section~\ref{sec:forest}), and expanding forest cover enlarges the area that can burn \citep{gerberding_mapping_2025}. We do not offer this as an argument against those policies; rather, we argue that fire hazard should enter their appraisal, which at present it generally does not.

Each of these implications ultimately depends on governance. No single body (whether the fire service, the forest administration or the geological survey) can manage Alpine wildfire risk alone. One workable arrangement would divide responsibility over time. Forest and fire authorities would own prevention and suppression, after which a post-fire susceptibility phase lasting years would be owned jointly with torrent, avalanche and infrastructure authorities. In such an arrangement the fire itself triggers the handover between these phases, yet at present that handover has no institutional form.

Research needs follow from the same diagnosis, although not all of them are equally within reach. Some work could begin at once, because it applies instruments already validated elsewhere. A terrain-conditioned suppression-difficulty framework, for instance, could be applied to the Alpine arc immediately, replacing an evidence gap with a measurement \citep{cardil_assessing_2025}. Ranking candidate treatment sites by expected avoided loss requires a large-fire likelihood surface and a per-hectare valuation of protective function. \citet{conedera_linking_2024} produced such a surface for Switzerland, and it can be computed elsewhere from a terrain model and a forest map, whereas \citet{accastello_asforesee_2019} demonstrated a replicable per-hectare valuation of protective function in one Alpine stand. What is mainly missing, in our assessment, is the mandate to extend and combine them (Sections~\ref{sec:data} and~\ref{sec:governance}). A cascade-explicit assessment, followed from an Alpine fire scenario to the consequences for a named settlement or transport corridor, could also be assembled now. It could build on the template for automatic post-fire threshold modification that \citet{tiranti_wildfires_2021} proposed from two Alpine catchments, with the coincidence-likelihood method that \citet{moftakhari_increasing_2019} applied to Californian energy infrastructure supplying the exposure component.

Much of the remaining work depends on observations that do not yet exist, and it is slower because it is foundational rather than incremental. As \citet{melzner_wildfire-induced_2025} note, the Alps have no long-term monitoring of wildfire and geohydrological processes comparable to that of the western United States, and the instrumentation design that \citet{baselt_compound_2026} proposed for compound mountain hazards offers a reasonable starting specification. Without post-fire soil products independent of spectral severity, the post-fire hazard chain continues to rest on a link that failed at the one Alpine fire where it was tested \citep{negri_wildfire_2026}. Without finer detection of small fires, change in the Alpine regime is likely to be underestimated, because the continental analyses that define current understanding consider only fires larger than 30\,ha, whereas most Alpine fires are smaller than 10\,ha \citep{beloiu_climatedriven_2026}. Converting the existing hazard surfaces into risk would require an arc-wide exposure and vulnerability register, validated against Alpine damage as it occurs. The building-scale indices that do exist disagree on terrain slope, the variable that most distinguishes the setting \citep{papathomakohle_towards_2025, papathomakohle_wildfire_2022}.

Once such records exist, several modelling questions become answerable. Under foehn, valley-wind and channelling regimes, valley fire weather departs from interpolated fields, and whether correcting for this improves danger prediction is testable, although the size of the departure has not yet been quantified \citep{ozaki_impact_2022, sharples_overview_2009}. Spread-model evaluation would probably be most useful if it concentrated on intensity and surface thermal boundary conditions rather than on perimeter. Intensity drives the cascade, yet the two simulators that \citet{malanchini_modeling_2025} compared on Alpine fires agree on propagation and disagree on intensity, and existing spread frameworks omit the soil-heating terms required by post-fire hydrological and slope-stability models \citep{or_review_2023}. Reviewing more than 150 experimental and modelling studies, \citet{hayajneh_wind_2026} also conclude that empirical formulations using simple wind and slope adjustment factors degrade outside their calibration envelope, including on steep slopes. Reconnecting the Alpine tradition of dynamic vegetation modelling to hazard assessment would let fuel condition, rather than fuel type, enter the models \citep{schumacher_relative_2006, hillebrand_contrasting_2023, muller_towards_2020}.

Coupling between fire, snow and glaciers belongs to the same programme, and it is the one loop in this system that no Alpine study has yet followed through. Drought shifts summer water supply onto glaciers \citep{leone_20222023_2026}, and on one Canadian glacier, \citet{aubrywake_fire_2022} found that soot deposited in the years after record fires increased melt by up to 10\%, although smoke itself reduced net radiation. In the western United States, fire also alters how much snow is stored and when it melts \citep{koshkin_wildfire_2022}.

In contrast, the questions that matter most to managers are empirical, and none yet has an Alpine answer. The most tractable is probably the acceptable degree of thinning in a protection forest (that is, the point at which protective capacity declines faster than fire hazard falls), whose protective side can already be quantified \citep{costa_biological_2021} and monetised \citep{accastello_asforesee_2019, moos_climate_2021} with methods in use. In Andalusian data, \citet{ortega_modeling_2024} found that fuel breaks contained fire mainly where suppression crews worked on them, and that ridgeline breaks performed best where fire met narrow breaks at an angle. Steep Alpine terrain may, however, prevent crews from reaching and holding a break, and whether any siting resolves this tension remains untested in the range. The single Alpine prescribed-burning trial (Section~\ref{sec:fuelbreaks}) was carried out in one stand of a fire-tolerant species, and while \citet{gamba_prescribed_2025} call for higher intensities, repeated burns, more than one location and observation beyond the first year, in our view the trial also needs extending to fire-intolerant stands. The Pyrenean experience described by \citet{oliveres_path_2025} suggests that a participatory, goal-setting process may matter as much as further ecological evidence. No Alpine post-fire salvage trial exists, and the evidence for retaining deadwood comes from windthrow \citep{costa_biological_2021, seben_impact_2024}. That evidence may not transfer, because burnt logs and snags may not provide the same barrier effect as windthrown stems \citep{costa_biological_2021}. Nor has anyone estimated, with terrain taken into account, the marginal return on suppression capability compared with fuel treatment, and without that estimate the allocation implied by current spending cannot be evaluated \citep{cardil_assessing_2025, brodie_forest_2024, muller_forest_2020}.

Some gaps fall outside the usual framing and are easily overlooked for that reason. Only part of the faunal component of Alpine fire loss has been measured. Bird communities have been followed for 13 years after fire in one inner-Alpine valley, and earlier Swiss work addressed arthropods \citep{rey_effects_2019}. The response of soil fauna, and the trajectory of habitat quality through the recovery period across the range, have not yet been described \citep{degiuli_arrested_2024, arrogantefunes_mapping_2024}. Extensive grazing reduces fuel at effect sizes measured elsewhere, but its effect reverses above a threshold not yet located for any Alpine pasture. Neither the unpaid fuel-management service that grazing supplies nor the pasture and livestock exposed to fire has been valued in the Alps, and in their recent review \citet{xu_how_2026} call for cost--benefit analyses comparing grazing with other fuel treatments. The distribution of costs and protective benefits across valley communities, through-traffic and downstream water users likewise remains unquantified, although the valuation methods to address it already exist \citep{accastello_asforesee_2019, moos_climate_2021}.

\label{sec:limitations}
These findings should be read within the limits of the evidence base, the most basic of which concerns the composition of the corpus. We built the corpus from two starting documents, a keyword search and one iteration of citation snowballing, and a single iteration of this kind inherits the blind spots of its seed documents. We document the keyword search (Step~2) by its search strings, because Google Scholar ranks results by relevance and its result sets cannot be exported for exact reproduction. Selection was made with in-house expertise in view, which favours the disciplines represented at one institute. Similarly, backward chaining favours works that the seed authors already considered relevant, while forward chaining favours works indexed in open citation infrastructure, which under-represents grey literature, national reports and publications in languages other than English. We consider this gap material, because a substantial part of Alpine fire knowledge exists in Italian, German, French and Slovenian technical reports, and the harmonisation of Alpine fire statistics has itself been driven by individual projects \citep{valese_wildfires_2011}. Restricting the expansion phase to sources whose full text could be retrieved widens the gap further, since it biases the added corpus towards open-access and therefore recent publications.

Because the synthesis is thematic, the studies cannot be pooled quantitatively (for the definitional and administrative reasons set out in Section~\ref{sec:scope}), and we therefore treat the numbers reported from individual studies as illustrations of magnitude rather than as estimates of Alpine parameters.

A larger limitation is the weight carried by single cases. Several quantities on which this review relies come from one documented site or event. The Alpine post-fire rockfall record rests on one fire, and the numerically analysed Alpine post-fire debris flow on one catchment \citep{melzner_brief_2019, laporta_postwildfire_2025}, whereas the monetised protective function comes from two case studies and the arrested subalpine regeneration of Section~\ref{sec:forest} from one massif \citep{accastello_asforesee_2019, moos_climate_2021, degiuli_arrested_2024}. We have marked these in the text and treated their magnitudes as observed values rather than central estimates. A synthesis built on such a base can show that a mechanism operates in the Alps and indicate an order of magnitude, but it cannot yet support a regional parameter. Confidence in the Alpine cascade argument therefore depends on whether these few observations are representative rather than exceptional, which is why we place systematic post-fire monitoring at the foundation of the research programme outlined above. External process benchmarks help bound that uncertainty where independent mountain observations show similar behaviour (as with post-fire debris-flow thresholds in Colorado). Yet they cannot substitute for Alpine measurement.

Borrowed process evidence raises a related difficulty. Several mechanisms examined here are established mainly outside the Alps, in laboratories or in other mountain ranges, including terrain effects on surface winds, convective wind--slope coupling, post-fire channel sediment supply, fire--snowpack interactions and terrain-conditioned containment difficulty \citep{sharples_overview_2009, hayajneh_wind_2026, morell_sediment_2021, koshkin_wildfire_2022, cardil_assessing_2025}. We have used such studies strictly to show that a physical or operational mechanism exists and to identify what imported tools omit, never to assign an Alpine coefficient. Maintaining that boundary requires judgement, as syntheses of mountain global-change research have noted \citep{payne_nature_2020}, and until these processes are tested locally their Alpine magnitude remains unmeasured.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

In this interdisciplinary review we set the emerging wildfire evidence from the European Alps against the tools, monitoring products and management doctrines routinely imported from Mediterranean and continental fire science. Our principal conclusion is that Alpine wildfire is not a smaller version of the Mediterranean problem but a structurally different risk system, in which imported assumptions break down along the entire chain from weather observation to post-fire governance.

This mismatch begins with the regime itself and with how it is observed. Alpine fire is bimodal, combining a late-winter and spring season of human-caused surface fires, often driven by foehn or thermal inversions, with a summer season in which convective lightning accounts for up to half of ignitions locally \citep{valese_wildfires_2011, muller_forest_2020}. Roughly four fifths of fires burn less than a hectare \citep{muller_forest_2020}, below continental satellite mapping thresholds, and they burn in stands dominated by thin-barked, fire-intolerant species (such as silver fir, Norway spruce and beech) \citep{dupire_vulnerability_2019, mutterer_bark_2026}. Meanwhile, the boundary conditions of this regime are shifting. Large fires across European mountains have moved upslope by roughly 72\,m per decade since 2000 \citep{beloiu_climatedriven_2026}, and hazard across the Alpine Space is projected to rise most strongly in the less experienced northern and eastern sectors \citep{gerberding_mapping_2025}, while pastoral abandonment and sub-treeline infilling have assembled continuous fuel on the long, steep slopes from which large Alpine fires develop \citep{frei_evidence_2023, conedera_linking_2024}. Because this fuel continuity originates in land-use change rather than fire exclusion, agricultural and pastoral policies are likely to carry as much weight for Alpine hazard as silviculture does.

These shifts matter disproportionately in the Alps because consequence decouples from burnt area. Much of the Alpine forest belt is managed primarily to arrest rockfall, snow avalanches and shallow landslides above fixed settlements and trans-Alpine transport corridors, and where rockfall protection has been monetised in two Alpine stands, its annual value reached roughly €950 and 1{,}350\,CHF per hectare, far exceeding marginal steep-slope timber returns \citep{accastello_asforesee_2019, moos_climate_2021}. Fire compromises that service across multiple pathways at once. It initiates a hydrogeomorphic cascade that can lower debris-flow rainfall thresholds into ordinary storm return periods \citep{laporta_postwildfire_2025}, degrades root reinforcement and rockfall arrest over windows of ten to forty years or longer \citep{maringer_temporal_2016, gehring_shallow_2019, degiuli_arrested_2024}, and reorganises biodiversity in opposite directions for closed-forest and open-habitat guilds while faunal mortality goes unmeasured \citep{rey_effects_2019}. Process evidence imported from Colorado indicates that debris-flow thresholds recover as vegetation regrows \citep{rengers_evaluating_2024}, which would make the biological recovery window a decisive risk parameter, and no Alpine monitoring network yet tracks it continuously.

Operational assessment tools have yet to catch up with this cascade. Gridded danger indices, separable wind--slope spread formulations, coarse satellite burned-area products and paired-date spectral severity maps consistently lose skill or misrepresent soil and intensity conditions in steep Alpine valleys. Fitting more complex data-driven models will not solve the problem while harmonised Alpine records remain short and sparse. We therefore see progress instead in selective transfer, that is, in importing physically grounded model structures while refitting coefficients locally, coupling existing Alpine hazard-warning and protection-forest tools to fire, and joining downslope exposure and vulnerability registers to hazard surfaces so that risk can be estimated directly.

Managing Alpine wildfire is therefore an interdisciplinary problem spanning forestry, agriculture, torrent engineering and cross-border governance. Prevention costs a small fraction of current firefighting and post-fire expenditure \citep{muller_forest_2020}, yet protective benefits flow largely to valley corridors and downstream users, whereas management costs and thinning trade-offs fall on small, fragmented mountain communities. Unlike regions locked into a century of suppression-led doctrine, much of the Alpine arc is still designing its wildfire institutions and, in our view, can build them from the outset around adaptation, area-based fuel stewardship and formal post-fire handovers between fire, forest and geological authorities.

\section{Search Protocol and Screening Register}
\label{app:protocol}

\subsection{Counts at each step}

Below we give the six steps of Section~\ref{sec:scope} and Figure~\ref{fig:flow} together with the numbers that each produced. We chose to combine starting documents, a keyword search and citation chaining because the literature is administratively fragmented, multilingual and heavily dependent on national technical reports. Database searches restricted to English, of the kind used in the recent global review of land-abandonment effects by \citet{mantero_influence_2020}, acknowledge that they may miss part of such a literature.

\textbf{Step 1, starting documents.} The EUSALP white paper \emph{Forest fires in the Alps: state of knowledge, future challenges and options for an integrated fire management} \citep{muller_forest_2020} covers the whole Alpine Convention area and combines harmonised fire statistics for the arc, a survey of authorities, scientists and fire services across the Alpine countries, and costed recommendations for prevention, suppression, post-fire management and knowledge transfer. Its reference list contains 120 publications and web sources, and it draws on six national fire databases. Alongside it, the study by \citet{gerberding_mapping_2025}, co-authored at Eurac Research, maps the probability of forest fire hazard across the arc under climate change scenarios.

\textbf{Step 2, keyword search.} The Google Scholar searches combined the terms wildfire, mountain and Alps, and subsequently wildfire and mountains. We kept publications where they fell within a discipline represented at Eurac Research (natural hazards and risk, climate, drought and water, forest science, and economics and governance), which are also the disciplines of the co-authors who review the sections in Step~6. The initial set holds 49 publications, including the Eurac Research study, of which 47 were available in full text. Of the 49, 31 were published in 2020 or later; three also appear in the white paper's reference list and six cite it.

\textbf{Step 3, citation chaining.} Working backward, we compiled the reference lists of the white paper and of 45 of the initial publications (two had no usable reference list), which together contained 3{,}575 references, 120 of them from the white paper. Once duplicates were removed, 3{,}375 distinct publications remained; of the white paper's 120 references, 62 were also reached through the initial publications and 58 through the white paper alone. Working forward, we used the OpenCitations index to look up the publications citing the 14 initial publications most central to the Alpine question. This search returned 508 citing publications, of which 319 could be identified with full bibliographic details, whereas the other 189 are kept in the register for a later search.

\textbf{Step 4, targeted additions.} We limited additions to publications whose full text could be obtained and archived. This step therefore weights the literature towards open-access and recent publications, and it is also the step that can least be reproduced from the initial set alone.

\textbf{Step 5, screening.} Once duplicates across all sources had been removed, we screened 3{,}228 publications and assigned each to one of four levels, recording the reason: \emph{include} (fire content in the European Alps or an Alpine Convention country); \emph{process benchmark} (fire content in another mountain system, admitted selectively where an Alpine process link is unmeasured or thinly covered, following the transfer rule of Section~\ref{sec:scope}); \emph{context} (Alpine hazard or forest processes without fire, or European and global fire tools and policies applied to the Alps); and \emph{exclude} (general fire science with no bearing on the Alpine chain). Screening excluded 2{,}332 publications and retained 896: 290 at the include level, 194 external process benchmarks and 412 context. We then read the retained publications in order of their relevance to the thematic sections. Together with the starting documents and the targeted additions from Step~4, 159 sources are cited in the text, each summarised in an evidence note (Section~\ref{app:recorded}).

\textbf{Step 6, discipline review.} Climatologists review the climate and meteorology evidence of Section~\ref{sec:climate}, drought and water researchers Section~\ref{sec:water}, and forest scientists Section~\ref{sec:forest}, while specialists in natural hazards and risk review Sections~\ref{sec:hazard} and~\ref{sec:risk} and economists and governance researchers review Section~\ref{sec:governance}. Publications added by the reviewers go through Steps~3 and~5 in the same way as the rest of the literature.

\subsection{What was recorded for each source}
\label{app:recorded}

Each screened record carries a tier, a decision, a reason and a mapping to one or more sections of this review. For every source cited in the text, we also captured a structured evidence note recording study region, method, quantitative results and stated limitations, and archived it alongside the screening register; both are provided as supplementary material. Where a claim in the text rests on fewer than three independent cases, the number is stated in the text, and mechanisms imported from outside the Alps are identified as imported at the point of use. Our synthesis is thematic rather than quantitative, for the reasons of definitional and administrative heterogeneity set out in Section~\ref{sec:scope}.

\subsection{What the method determines about the corpus}

Our method shapes what the corpus contains, and its effects should be read as constraints on coverage rather than on the reliability of individual sources. Because citation chaining reproduces the coverage of its starting point, topics well represented in the white paper and among the seeds tend to be recovered densely, whereas topics absent from both are recovered only weakly. Forward chaining, in turn, operates on open citation infrastructure, which indexes journal literature comprehensively but national technical reporting and non-English publication sparsely; this may be a material consideration for a range whose fire statistics were harmonised only recently \citep{valese_wildfires_2011}. Similarly, the expansion phase was confined to material whose full text could be retrieved, which weights the added corpus towards open-access and recent publication.

To address these constraints, a subsequent iteration should add database searching with recorded strings and dates, resolve the 189 forward records held over, and target the national fire and forest reporting of the Alpine Convention countries directly, since much of the operational and grey-literature knowledge base resides there.

\section{Glossary of Terms}

\begin{description}
    \item[Fire danger] The meteorological and fuel-condition potential for ignition and spread, expressed by an index; a statement about conditions, not about realised fire.
    \item[Fire regime] The realised distribution of fire frequency, size, severity, seasonality and cause over a region and period.
    \item[Burn severity] The degree of fire-induced change to vegetation and soil, commonly estimated remotely; see Section~\ref{sec:water} for the limits of this proxy.
    \item[Fuel connectivity] The extent and spatial aggregation of contiguous burnable vegetation, distinct from fuel load.
    \item[Protection forest] Forest whose primary managed function is mitigation of gravitational natural hazards affecting assets below.
    \item[Geohydrological process] Collective term used in the Alpine literature for erosion, rockfall, slides and debris flows driven by the interaction of water and slope material.
    \item[Cascade] A sequence in which one hazard alters the susceptibility of a system to a subsequent, distinct hazard.
    \item[Firebreak] A linear strip from which fuel is removed entirely, intended to stop fire spread.
    \item[Fuel break (\emph{coupure de combustible})] A linear strip in which fuel load and vertical continuity are reduced but the stand is retained; functions primarily as a platform for ground suppression rather than as a barrier.
    \item[Exposure] The elements (people, buildings, infrastructure, habitats, protective forest, livestock and pasture) located where a hazard and its consequences can reach them.
    \item[Vulnerability] The susceptibility of an exposed element to loss given a hazard of stated intensity; distinct from exposure and from hazard, and measured by damage-calibrated or judgement-weighted indicators.
    \item[Ecological vulnerability] Potential loss of ecological value integrated over the post-fire recovery period, combining the value of what burns, the resistance of the vegetation to fire and the time taken to return to pre-fire condition.
\end{description}
