\begin{abstract}
\noindent
European wildfire science relies heavily on tools, satellite products and management doctrines developed for large fires outside the Alps, many of them in Mediterranean landscapes, leaving the Alps at its margin. Large fires in European mountains have moved upslope, most markedly after 2015, fire hazard is projected to rise across the Alpine arc, and land abandonment has created continuous fuel on steep former pasture. We synthesise the Alpine evidence across hazard, climate, water, forest, risk and governance, testing imported tools and doctrines against Alpine conditions. Roughly 80\% of Alpine fires burn less than a hectare, below continental satellite thresholds. Many Alpine stands are fire-sensitive and managed to hold rock, snow and sediment above settlements and transport corridors, so we argue that where a fire burns may matter more for its consequences than how large it is. At the few Alpine sites studied, moderate and high-severity fire reduced protection against rockfall and shallow landslides for decades, and at two sites burnt slopes produced debris flows after brief, moderate rainfall. Because land use governs fuel continuity, and one continental model finds management capacity strongly modulates projected burnt area, forest and agricultural policy may matter as much as climate in shaping outcomes. We examine evidence that valley-interpolated weather indices, separable spread formulations, linear fuel breaks, windthrow salvage conventions and hazard maps lacking exposure or vulnerability can misfire on steep slopes. We separate trends supported by several independent studies from single-case findings, and outline a research agenda scaled to the sparse Alpine record.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:}
European Alps; mountain wildfire; protection forest; post-fire cascades; model transferability; fuel management; drought and water availability; Earth observation; wildfire governance and economics; multi-hazard risk assessment

\noindent\textbf{Key Points}
\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}
\item Tools, satellite products and doctrines built for large summer wildfires can misread risk in the small-fire, bimodal European Alps.
\item Where forest protects valleys, fire location may matter more than burnt area; at the few sites studied, protection was lost for decades.
\item Turning Alpine hazard maps into risk requires local calibration, downslope exposure data and post-fire institutional handovers.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent\textbf{Plain Language Summary}\\
Wildfires are becoming a growing concern in the European Alps as temperatures rise, snow cover shortens and forests expand across abandoned mountain pastures. However, most European wildfire monitoring systems, weather indices and prevention strategies were developed for larger fires elsewhere, often in Mediterranean regions. This interdisciplinary review brings together Alpine research across natural hazards, climate, hydrology, forest ecology, risk assessment and governance to test how well those imported tools fit Alpine conditions. Most Alpine fires burn less than one hectare and go undetected by continental satellites. Many Alpine forests shield valley towns, roads and railways from rockfall, avalanches and landslides, and at the few sites studied, fire weakened this protection for decades. Burnt slopes can also produce debris flows during brief, moderate rainstorms, as documented at two Alpine sites. Managing Alpine wildfire therefore requires locally adapted warning systems, agricultural and forest stewardship on steep slopes, and coordinated post-fire handovers across hazard agencies.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{An emerging fire frontier in the European Alps}

European fire science has largely been shaped by where burnt area is concentrated. Because the Mediterranean basin accounts for most of the continent's burning, it has also supplied most of its fire statistics, its operational danger rating and much of its prevention doctrine. This geographical bias matters because the distribution of burning is now changing. Independent studies at global, European and mountain scale point in the same direction: fire-weather extremity has risen across most burnable regions \citep{jones_global_2022}, burning in Europe is moving out of the Mediterranean into mid- and high-latitude regions with abundant fuel and little adaptation to fire \citep{miller_compounding_2025}, and in European mountains it is moving upslope \citep{beloiu_climatedriven_2026}. Across eight European mountain regions, for instance, \citet{beloiu_climatedriven_2026} found that fires of at least 30\,ha above 800\,m shifted upslope by approximately 72\,m per decade, a change that appears to reflect a break after 2015 rather than a gradual trend.

Projections for the European Alps point in the same direction. Three independent modelling studies project fire hazard, fire danger or fire-weather-driven burnt area to rise across the Alps and adjacent central Europe in the course of this century \citep{gerberding_mapping_2025, miller_climate_2024, billing_future_2026}. Across the Alpine Space cooperation area (which spans the Alps and their surrounding foothills), \citet{gerberding_mapping_2025} project mean hazard probability to increase by 35.4\% under SSP5-8.5 by 2070--2100, with the largest relative increases in the northern and eastern sectors, and the forced fire-danger signal in adjacent central Europe is projected to emerge before 2050 \citep{miller_climate_2024}. Fire may thus become a routine hazard within the planning horizon of the Alpine institutions that would have to absorb it (Section~\ref{sec:climate}). Climate alone, however, may not determine how much of this increase is realised. In one continental modelling study, \citet{billing_future_2026} found that continued improvement in management capacity could offset much, though not all, of the climate-driven increase in burnt area, with residual hotspots under the higher pathway in medium-elevation ranges such as the Alps. Because this rests on a single study, it indicates only that management capacity may matter as much as climate for Alpine outcomes.

What burns in the Alps, and what is lost when it does, also departs from the Mediterranean template. Roughly four fifths of Alpine fires burn less than a hectare, with a heavy tail of larger events, whereas European burnt-area mapping operates reliably only above about 30\,ha, so satellite archives miss the modal Alpine fire \citep{muller_forest_2020, conedera_characterizing_2018, beloiu_climatedriven_2026}. At the same time, much of the Alpine forest protects settlements and corridors against rockfall, snow avalanche and shallow landslide. Fire in fire-intolerant conifer and beech stands can remove this protective function for years to decades, and recovery at some subalpine sites stalls altogether \citep{melzner_wildfire-induced_2025, melzner_brief_2019, maringer_temporal_2016, gehring_shallow_2019, degiuli_arrested_2024}.

Fire is thus arriving in Alpine valleys where the science, the management system and the exposed population have uneven experience of it. This gap is not new to Alpine forestry. More than a decade ago, \citet{jandl_management_2013} observed for the MANFRED consortium that, because of a history of active suppression, fire risk had not received full attention in the Alps. Since then the climate signal has emerged, whereas operational integration across Alpine hazard domains appears to have followed more slowly.

\subsection{Why imported fire tools and doctrines misread Alpine risk}

Steep temperate mountains remain poorly resolved in both coarse-grid fire classifications and disciplinary reviews, which encourages the uncritical import of tools built elsewhere. Global pyrogeographies classify fire regimes without an altitudinal or relief term \citep{archibald_defining_2013, pereira_wild_2022}, and biome-scale assessments of fire-regime change aggregate across biomes rather than elevation belts \citep{sayedi_assessing_2024}. Similarly, recent syntheses and specialised reviews treat fire trends, mountain warming, mountain fire meteorology, wind- and slope-driven spread, early detection, machine learning and wildfire effects on land surfaces in isolation \citep{jones_global_2022, pepin_climate_2022, sharples_overview_2009, hayajneh_wind_2026, mohapatra_early_2022, jain_review_2020, or_review_2023}. In the Alps, however, these processes act as a single coupled chain, and imported instruments meet structural limits at each of its links.

These limits appear first in the atmosphere and at the fire front. Danger indices imported from continental or Mediterranean practice assume a continuous fire-weather field that can be interpolated from valley-floor stations or coarse reanalyses, and standard spread simulators treat wind and slope as separable corrections. Relief weakens both assumptions, because upper winds, foehn and inversions produce discontinuous and locally amplified surface conditions, and because rate of spread accelerates non-linearly with upslope angle \citep{sharples_overview_2009, ozaki_impact_2022, hayajneh_wind_2026}. This evidence is imported from outside the Alps, chiefly from Australian terrain and from experimental and modelling studies (Sections~\ref{sec:hazard} and~\ref{sec:climate}). For the Alps themselves, a single Swiss study suggests that continuous forested upslope paths and forest aggregation can explain much of the variance in large-fire statistics without any weather term \citep{conedera_linking_2024}.

Perhaps the deepest mismatch, however, lies in how impact is defined once the fire is out. In South Tyrol, for example, about 58\% of the forest area is classed as protection forest \citep{hillebrand_contrasting_2023}, so the asset at risk is typically not the burnt stand itself but the town, road or railway beneath it. We therefore expect burnt area (a metric that serves Mediterranean risk accounting reasonably well) to be a poor guide to consequence in the Alps, although no Alpine study has yet related damage to burnt area directly.

\subsection{Objective, baseline and structure of this review}

In this review we synthesise the interdisciplinary evidence on wildfire in the European Alps and evaluate where imported fire tools, metrics and management doctrines hold, where they require local recalibration and where Alpine reality breaks their underlying assumptions (Figure~\ref{fig:cascade}). Our aim is to establish the evidentiary basis for treating Alpine wildfire as a systemic multi-hazard risk rather than as a scaled-down forest-loss problem.

Our starting point is the EUSALP white paper of February 2020 \citep{muller_forest_2020}, which set out the state of knowledge for decision makers, established the arc-wide statistical baseline, identified the loss of protective function as the central concern and proposed a costed integrated fire management framework. Because a policy white paper is not peer reviewed in the ordinary way, we take it as given and set its positions against the empirical work that followed, so as to distinguish the elements now securely supported from those that require qualification and those that remain unmeasured.

Some recent work extends this baseline. The structural distinctiveness of the Alpine regime has been quantified vertically, through the upslope migration of European mountain fires \citep{beloiu_climatedriven_2026}, whereas terrain-conditioned indices of containment difficulty remain imported, having been developed from Californian ignitions \citep{cardil_assessing_2025}. Similarly, the view of Alpine fire management as substantially a forestry problem can now draw on monetised valuations of rockfall protection \citep{accastello_asforesee_2019, moos_climate_2021}, on a single controlled Alpine prescribed-burning trial \citep{gamba_prescribed_2025} and on burn-severity conceptualisations of how long protective capacity remains compromised \citep{melzner_wildfire-induced_2025}. Other findings, however, complicate the 2020 baseline, particularly with respect to post-fire consequence. Fire can prime a slope that a subsequent storm destabilises, and part of this cascade may fall outside the return-period design logic of Alpine hazard engineering. In one burnt catchment of the Susa Valley, \citet{laporta_postwildfire_2025} reported a debris flow triggered below the rainfall intensity typical of unburnt basins of its type; as a single event, it shows that sub-threshold triggering can occur rather than how often it does (Section~\ref{sec:water}). Operational assessment, moreover, still stops at hazard surfaces, because arc-wide exposure and vulnerability layers have not yet been joined to them.

We organise the review by the disciplinary domains in which the evidence is produced, and each section opens with the trends supported by multiple independent studies before turning to evidence that rests on fewer cases. Section~\ref{sec:hazard} examines wildfire as a natural hazard, Section~\ref{sec:climate} climatic controls, mountain wind systems and projections, and Section~\ref{sec:water} the water pathway from soil alteration through debris flows to water supply, snow and ice. Section~\ref{sec:forest} turns to forest change, the recovery of protective function and fuel treatments, Section~\ref{sec:risk} to the exposure and vulnerability terms needed to move from hazard to risk, and Section~\ref{sec:governance} to suppression constraints, policy fragmentation and economics. In Section~\ref{sec:discussion} we draw these trends together, set imported doctrines against Alpine reality (Table~\ref{tab:contrast}) and outline a research agenda scaled to the observational record.

\section{Review Design and Scope}
\label{sec:scope}

\subsection{Alpine scope, intra-Alpine regimes and external process benchmarks}

Our geographical scope is the European Alps as delimited by the Alpine Convention (which spans Austria, France, Germany, Italy, Liechtenstein, Monaco, Slovenia and Switzerland), together with the overlapping EUSALP perimeter, within which harmonised multi-national fire statistics were first compiled \citep{valese_wildfires_2011, muller_forest_2020}. Focusing on the Alpine arc does not, however, imply a single uniform regime. Steep climatic, lithological and socio-economic gradients divide the range into distinct pyroregions whose seasonal timing, dominant ignition causes and management pressures diverge sharply \citep{conedera_characterizing_2018, wastl_recent_2012, muller_forest_2020}. Table~\ref{tab:regimes} summarises the four principal intra-Alpine regime settings that recur throughout Sections~\ref{sec:hazard} to~\ref{sec:governance}, and thereby illustrates why a tool calibrated in one sector of the Alps is unlikely to generalise across the arc.

Because several links in Figure~\ref{fig:cascade} rest on a single documented Alpine event or have not been measured in the range at all, we draw selectively on process studies from other mountain settings, such as post-fire debris-flow threshold recovery in the Rocky Mountains, fire--snowpack and glacier-albedo measurements in the Sierra Nevada and Canadian Rockies, or strategic fire management in the Pyrenees. Such studies can show that a mechanism operates and can bound the blind spot of an Alpine assessment, but their empirical coefficients do not transfer. In contrast, broader European or global studies enter as methodological context where they define the imported tools and policies applied to the Alps.

\subsection{The six-step review framework and evidence weighting}

Literature was assembled and reviewed in six steps (Figure~\ref{fig:flow}); detailed counts and the screening register are given in Supporting Information (Supporting Information (Text~S1)). The starting documents were the 2020 EUSALP white paper \citep{muller_forest_2020} and a Eurac Research mapping of Alpine forest fire hazard under climate change \citep{gerberding_mapping_2025}, which adds the projected, spatially explicit view that the white paper lacks. A manual Google Scholar search (wildfire, mountain and Alps, then wildfire and mountains, so that process studies on links unmeasured in the Alps would not be missed) was restricted to disciplines represented at Eurac Research, so that a specialist could assess each publication. The set was then expanded by citation chaining in both directions and by targeted searches on topics that proved thinly covered. Every publication was screened against a four-level rule (direct Alpine fire evidence, external mountain process benchmarks, methodological context, exclusion), and every cited source was summarised in a structured evidence note. The Eurac Research co-authors working in each discipline review the corresponding section for coverage, reading and weight, and publications they add return to citation chaining.

Our synthesis is thematic rather than meta-analytic, because the evidence does not support pooling. Alpine fire studies differ in their definitions of severity, burnt-area thresholds, reporting periods and the administrative basis of their fire records, so their effect sizes are not comparable, an obstacle that \citet{mantero_influence_2020} also documented in the neighbouring literature on land abandonment and disturbance.

Because much of the Alpine evidence consists of individual documented events, we weight it by an explicit convention, around which the review is organised. We call a trend robust when at least three independent studies support it, counting work from the same team, site or event once and allowing a review that pools many studies to count; such trends open each section and are stated firmly, with their sources cited together. Where two independent studies agree, we say so, and where a claim rests on a single study, site or event, we state this and hedge the claim. Case evidence thus shows that a process operates, describes its mechanism and bounds its magnitude in one setting, so a single-case magnitude is read as a value the process can reach rather than as a central estimate. Regional parameters are reserved for multi-site evidence, and a mechanism established outside the Alps is marked as imported at the point of use and never supplies an Alpine parameter.

\section{Wildfire as a Natural Hazard}
\label{sec:hazard}

\subsection{Initiation: the Alpine ignition regime and its modelling}

Independent records agree that the Alpine fire regime is dominated by very small fires, while a few large ones account for most of the burnt area \citep{muller_forest_2020, conedera_characterizing_2018, beloiu_climatedriven_2026, papathomakohle_towards_2025, jrc_advance_2026}. Roughly 80\% of Alpine fires burn less than one hectare, and median fire size falls below 0.1\,ha in Austria, Germany and Switzerland. Yet in the French and Italian Alps the 10\% of fires exceeding ten hectares account for some 85\% of burnt area, a figure that rests on a single review of national records \citep{muller_forest_2020}. We consider both parts of this statistic important, because the many small fires set what monitoring must detect, whereas the few large ones are the events that can remove protective function across a whole slope.

Seasonality is bimodal, and the two modes have different drivers \citep{muller_forest_2020, valese_wildfires_2011, schunk_forest_2013, vacchiano_modeling_2018, trucchia_defining_2022}, a pattern also found outside the Alps \citep{bercak_combination_2024}. A spring season of mostly human-caused surface fires at low elevation peaks in March and April, whereas a summer season tied to heat and lightning peaks in July and August (winter fires in the foehn regions are treated in Section~\ref{sec:climate}). In the Aosta Valley, for example, summer ignition responds to maximum temperature, whereas winter fire responds to precipitation and elevation, and proximity to infrastructure matters only in winter \citep{vacchiano_modeling_2018}. The Italian national susceptibility mapping accordingly produced separate winter and summer surfaces \citep{trucchia_defining_2022}. A single annual model would therefore average across processes acting in different seasons, and there is little reason to expect the two modes to change in step.

Ignition is predominantly human, with human causes accounting for approximately 90\% of Alpine fires and lightning for around 10\% \citep{muller_forest_2020, valese_wildfires_2011}, and regional analyses of separate records agree on this dominance \citep{zumbrunnen_human_2012, vacchiano_modeling_2018}. Lightning nonetheless forms a distinct summer mode at higher elevation, reaching up to half of summer fires in Austria and parts of Switzerland \citep{muller_forest_2020, conedera_lightninginduced_2006}. Human ignition follows the access network rather than population alone, with railway, forest-road and hiking-trail density among the main predictors in Austria \citep{arndt_modeling_2013}. However, a single study in the Valais suggests that the relation is not linear, since fire occurrence rose with road density up to about 30\,m per hectare and then fell sharply \citep{zumbrunnen_human_2012}. Because the arc-wide statistics on size, season and cause come from one consortium lineage whose authors overlap \citep{muller_forest_2020, valese_wildfires_2011, conedera_characterizing_2018}, we count them once and take the regional analyses of \citet{vacchiano_modeling_2018} and \citet{zumbrunnen_human_2012} as the independent checks.

Year-to-year departures from this structure are large, as three consecutive anomalous Austrian seasons between 2011 and 2013 showed \citep{muller_anomalies_2015}, so a short record cannot separate trend from fluctuation. Even so, \citet{conedera_characterizing_2018} found coherent Southern, Northern and Maritime clusters, which we treat as a baseline for detecting regime shifts rather than as a stable state, because the classification rests on only ten years of record.

Ignition modelling has matured fastest of the domains treated here, probably because ignition points are the only Alpine fire observation available in reasonable numbers. Several independent studies show that locally fitted, parsimonious models perform well where they were fitted \citep{vacchiano_modeling_2018, arndt_modeling_2013, de_angelis_modelling_2015, moreno_spacetime_2025}, the last of these a preprint; in the Aosta Valley, for instance, the area under the curve (AUC) reached 0.90 to 0.95 \citep{vacchiano_modeling_2018}. However, such models transfer poorly. A single ignition model pooled across five non-Alpine European regions lost skill at every site relative to site-specific fitting \citep{gelabert_assessing_2025}, whereas in the study of \citet{bekar_crossregional_2020} regional models, including those for southern Switzerland and Carinthia, transferred well only between similar environments and a model trained on all four regions transferred better. We therefore read pooling as a trade of local skill for the stability beyond present conditions that a climate scenario requires. Predictors can even invert between ranges, since in the Carpathians lower elevations are more fire-prone \citep{manczinger_fire_2025}, whereas in the Alps 42\% of large fires recorded above 800\,m burned above 1{,}400\,m \citep{beloiu_climatedriven_2026}.

\subsection{Spread: fire behaviour and spread modelling}

Reviews of laboratory, wind-tunnel and field experiments, none of them Alpine, agree that relief shapes the surface wind field and that wind and slope act on spread non-linearly and interactively \citep{hayajneh_wind_2026, sharples_overview_2009, ozaki_impact_2022}. As reviewed by \citet{hayajneh_wind_2026}, preheating becomes convection-led near 1.5\,m\,s$^{-1}$ of wind and beyond roughly 30\degree{} of slope, and eruptive transition appears where slope exceeds 25\degree{} to 30\degree{} in confined channels. Because operational spread models apply wind and slope as separate correction factors, they miss these transitions, and we therefore expect them to perform worst in steep Alpine terrain, where valley winds routinely cross the wind threshold within a diurnal cycle.

Alpine observation of such behaviour rests on one documented case, the 2018 Hallstatt fire in Austria, where rock walls, anabatic winds and patchy vegetation made the fire jump upward and burn in patches \citep{melzner_brief_2019}, and as this rests on one fire, it shows that such behaviour can occur rather than how often it does. In the only systematic Alpine evaluation of spread models, on three Lombardy fires, the deterministic simulator FlamMap and the stochastic cellular automaton PROPAGATOR both reproduced the observed propagation but diverged substantially on fireline intensity and rate of spread \citep{malanchini_modeling_2025}. Although this rests on three fires studied by one group, the divergence matters, because intensity determines severity and therefore the post-fire cascade (Section~\ref{sec:water}).

Valley circulation is itself a driver of spread, but here too the evidence is imported. In a simulation of a fire in the Greater Khingan Mountains, \citet{wang_dynamic_2026} attributed the fastest spread to nocturnal down-valley winds, which suggests that the hours of fastest Alpine spread are those when crews are least able to work, although the coefficients do not transfer from that Chinese boreal setting. We found no coupled fire-atmosphere simulation published for an Alpine fire. Laboratory experiments outside the Alps also show that slope accelerates smouldering \citep{shan_influence_2024}, so terrain may also prolong sub-surface burning and with it the mop-up phase.

\subsection{Fire danger and hazard modelling}

Danger indices need local recalibration before they can be used outside the region where they were developed, as Alpine tests in Austria and Ticino show \citep{arpaci_selecting_2013, de_angelis_modelling_2015}; similarly, a review of 63 danger systems and indices from 16 countries notes that the danger classes of the Canadian Fire Weather Index are redefined by local calibration wherever it is used outside Canada \citep{zacharakis_environmental_2023}. As that review documents, the Canadian Fire Weather Index (FWI) is preferred in Mediterranean Europe, and few systems represent terrain beyond coarse slope classes. The Italian operational system (RISICO) is the clearest exception, computing fuel moisture, rate of spread and fireline intensity hourly with a topography-informed susceptibility layer, but its one-kilometre grid limited its evaluation to fires larger than 70\,ha, above the size of almost every Alpine fire \citep{perello_fuelaware_2025}. For Austria, the size of the recalibration problem has been measured directly. \citet{arpaci_selecting_2013} tested twenty-two fire weather indices at 26 stations spanning 22 ecoregions and found that no single index performed best across regions or seasons, so we read index choice there as depending on season and ecoregion at once. Alpine practice has responded through local recalibration in some places and through integration in others. In Canton Ticino, a locally fitted index built on meteorological variables alone matched or outperformed the full FWI but did not transfer to other regions \citep{de_angelis_modelling_2015}. Austria, by contrast, chose integration, and its system improved prediction of \emph{whether} a fire starts without improving prediction of \emph{how large} it becomes \citep{muller_towards_2020}.

Taken together, these results suggest that the gap lies less in the choice of variables than in a lack of local calibration data and of information on dynamic fuel condition. In Austria this gap is literal, as no fuel models for predicting fire behaviour exist \citep{neumann_fuel_2022} (Section~\ref{sec:forest}). A way beyond static fuel representation is present in the Alpine literature, since LandClim simulations for the Swiss Alps treated fire as an emergent property feeding back on vegetation \citep{schumacher_relative_2006, colombaroli_species_2010}, and forest-dynamics models have quantified changes in protective function under climate scenarios \citep{hillebrand_contrasting_2023, moos_climate_2021}. In our judgement, reconnecting this tradition to fire hazard assessment is the most valuable methodological opportunity in Alpine fire science.

\subsection{Data, records and Earth observation}
\label{sec:data}

Independent comparisons between satellite products and national records agree that continental fire products capture the burnt area of large fires but miss most Alpine fires \citep{jrc_advance_2026, beloiu_climatedriven_2026, papathomakohle_towards_2025, jones_global_2022, stoof_temperate_2024}. The European Forest Fire Information System (EFFIS) maps perimeters effectively only from about 30\,ha upward \citep{beloiu_climatedriven_2026}, and continental burnt-area products are coarser still \citep{forrest_understanding_2024}, whereas national registers record events far below any satellite threshold \citep{papathomakohle_towards_2025}. Mapped fires thus account for approximately 95\% of reported burnt area but for only a fraction of fires \citep{jrc_advance_2026}, and in 2025 four fires were mapped in Austria against a national record running to the low hundreds a year \citep{jrc_advance_2026, papathomakohle_towards_2025}. The gap has been measured directly only outside the Alps, where the European reference dataset captured 0.2\% of recorded Dutch wildfires \citep{stoof_temperate_2024}. We regard this mismatch as the main methodological problem in Alpine fire science, because detected trends in large Alpine fires are then trends in the tail of the distribution, whereas an absence of detected change may reflect that most of the regime is invisible to the detector.

Severity products, on which post-fire assessment depends, also appear biased in mountain terrain, although the Alpine evidence comes from two studies by one group. At a single mixed-severity fire, mapped severity was decoupled from the soil water repellency it is meant to represent \citep{negri_wildfire_2026} (Section~\ref{sec:water}). Steep relief also produces illumination contrasts that vary with season and aspect, and for ten fires in Piedmont, phenologically matched composites increased the extent classified as high severity by roughly 19\% \citep{morresi_mapping_2022}. An Alpine severity map produced without controlling for seasonal timing is therefore likely to understate high severity. National registers, in turn, hold the number of action forces, helicopters and brigades committed to each event, and so are the only source from which suppression effectiveness could be estimated, although they are not currently used for that purpose \citep{muller_forest_2020}. Detection meets its own terrain problem, as ridges occlude camera towers and rugged ground hampers uncrewed aerial vehicles and sensor networks \citep{muller_forest_2020, mohapatra_early_2022}.

Validation in Alpine and adjacent fire modelling is now reasonably rigorous \citep{trucchia_defining_2022, moreno_spacetime_2025, muller_towards_2020}, so we see the remaining problem as one of statistical power rather than rigour. The models in current use are also narrower than the process they represent. Static fuel maps cannot represent a bimodal seasonal fuel bed, nor one that changes over decades as forest cover and the treeline have \citep{bebi_changes_2017, frei_evidence_2023}, and the Alpine scenario model of \citet{gerberding_mapping_2025} does not consider extreme events or land-use change. At larger scales, continental burnt-area models are trained on products that miss many small fires \citep{forrest_understanding_2024}, most fire-enabled global vegetation models omit topographic effects on spread \citep{jones_global_2022}, and single-hazard susceptibility mapping cannot represent the cascade \citep{trucchia_defining_2022}. Because each omission appears to remove a process that adds to hazard, we suggest that the resulting estimates be read as lower bounds, an inference across separate sources rather than a tested result.

Widening the models would be the obvious remedy, but the Alpine record is too thin to support it. The harmonised regional record covers ten years \citep{conedera_characterizing_2018}, the analysis that establishes upslope migration excludes fires below the mapping threshold \citep{beloiu_climatedriven_2026}, post-fire geohydrological observation amounts to a first inventory \citep{melzner_wildfire-induced_2025}, and documented post-fire debris flows in the western Italian Alps number two \citep{tiranti_wildfires_2021}. Attribution studies of the record 2025 European fire season also exclude the Alps \citep{keeping_attribution_2026, burton_state_2026}, the latter a preprint. As reviewed by \citet{jain_review_2020}, data-driven methods need large, consistent datasets and extrapolate uncertainly without physical constraints. The Alpine literature instead points to better-chosen structure, such as an upslope path-length metric for Swiss large fires \citep{conedera_linking_2024}, a parsimonious additive ignition model \citep{moreno_spacetime_2025} or a low-dimensional local index \citep{de_angelis_modelling_2015}. Transferability is thus selective rather than uniformly poor, since empirically tuned weather indices do not transfer, whereas models such as the post-fire debris-flow likelihood model \citep{diakakis_exploring_2023} and the Initial Attack Assessment index \citep{cardil_assessing_2025} have been carried to new settings outside the Alps, each on few cases. We therefore suggest that the operational rule the Alpine evidence supports is to import the mechanism and recalibrate the coefficients.

\section{Climate, Weather and Projections}
\label{sec:climate}

\subsection{Climatic controls on Alpine fire activity}

Fuel moisture, rather than temperature, is the direct link between climate and fire, and on this point multiple independent studies agree. Across European climate regions, multi-month drought indices, soil moisture deficit and vapour pressure deficit are the strongest and most consistent drivers of fire activity, although their interactions are region- and season-specific \citep{miller_compounding_2025, venalainen_temporal_2014, ruffault_extreme_2018}. In a five-site European model of human-caused ignition, \citet{gelabert_assessing_2025} found the annual anomaly in dead fine fuel moisture content to be the most influential variable at every pilot site. This evidence is largely pan-European or Mediterranean, and it reaches the Alps mainly through the finding that vapour pressure deficit is the strongest predictor of burnt area across eight European mountain regions \citep{beloiu_climatedriven_2026}.

Within the Alps, fire climate varies with elevation, between the moist northern and the insubric southern slopes, and between dry inner and wetter outer ranges. Of these gradients, elevation currently sets the main constraint, through snow cover and temperature-limited fuel drying, and it is this constraint that warming is relaxing. Snow-cover duration and snow depth have declined strongly below 2{,}000\,m, and paired adjacent stations more often record faster warming at higher elevations, particularly near the retreating snowline \citep{rangwala_climate_2012, pepin_climate_2022}. Locally, warming has already cut ski provision in the lower valleys of one Italian district \citep{pede_mountain_2022}, and forest has infilled near the treeline in one Swiss valley \citep{frei_evidence_2023}, from which we infer that a longer drying window is opening over ground where fuel is accumulating.

Fire danger itself has risen, most clearly in its extremes. Across Europe, \citet{venalainen_temporal_2014} found a significant increase in southern and eastern Europe from 1980 onward. For the Alps, the only arc-wide observed analysis, based on 25 long-term stations, found mean annual danger rising significantly south and west of the range but not consistently elsewhere. At the 95th percentile, however, \citet{wastl_recent_2012} found a trend towards higher values at almost every station. The extreme tail of Alpine fire danger is thus rising even where the mean has not changed detectably. Yet rising danger is not the same as a changing fire regime, and because the Alpine record is short and dominated by recording thresholds (Section~\ref{sec:data}), we treat the lack of a detected regime signal as weak evidence that nothing has changed.

How aridity translates into Alpine fire also depends on the form of drought and on land use, and for each the evidence is a single study. In south-eastern France, \citet{ruffault_extreme_2018} separated press drought, a long-lasting deficit behind wind-driven fires, from hot drought, in which a deficit combines with a heatwave to drive heat-induced fires. In our reading, Alpine spring fires under foehn match the former and the emerging high-elevation fires above 1400\,m \citep{beloiu_climatedriven_2026} the latter, so a danger system calibrated on one form would miss the other. Land use, moreover, appears to mediate the climatic signal itself. In Valais (a dry continental valley of the Swiss Alps), \citet{zumbrunnen_human_2012} found that fire was best predicted by population density and wood harvest in the first half of the twentieth century and by temperature in the second, as farming and forestry withdrew from higher ground. Since this rests on one valley, and both models explain only about a third of the variance, it shows that controls can shift with the socio-economic regime rather than how generally they do, but it still cautions against attributing present-day shifts to climate by default.

\subsection{Mountain meteorology: wind systems, foehn and the thermal belt}

Relief breaks up the fire-weather field, as independent studies in several mountain ranges agree, although the clearest measurements are imported. Where upper winds meet complex terrain, surface conditions become discontinuous and locally amplified, so that a field interpolated from valley-floor stations loses validity \citep{sharples_overview_2009}. In one Tasmanian valley fire, \citet{ozaki_impact_2022} found conducive wind profiles far more frequent over rugged than over gentle terrain, where simulations including upper-air winds performed better. In the Alps, steep topography controls foehn, diurnal valley winds and channelled flow, and through them fire type, intensity and burn severity \citep{melzner_wildfire-induced_2025}. In our reading, these wind systems turn small ignitions into fast fires, on a daily cycle that once-daily station grids cannot resolve.

At the synoptic scale, a single arc-wide analysis of sixty years of weather types found elevated danger mainly under persistent anticyclones or in the lee of the range under foehn, with the southern Alps the most fire-prone \citep{wastl_largescale_2013}. On this evidence, we regard foehn as one of the two synoptic configurations that generate Alpine fire danger rather than as an incidental local phenomenon.

Temperature inversions add a less obvious structure, which two independent Alpine sources document. In foehn regions north and south of the range, winter fires occur because inversion places a sunny, dry belt above the fog line \citep{muller_forest_2020}. During the autumn 2011 drought in the Bavarian Alps, \citet{schunk_forest_2013} found fire danger and fire incidents concentrated at mid-slope in this thermal belt while valley stations registered only moderate danger. They recommend high-elevation stations, or high-resolution meteorological models feeding indices that accept hourly input. However, this remedy has not been generally implemented in the danger-rating systems reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:hazard}.

\subsection{Lightning, convection and the summer ignition mode}

Lightning forms a distinct summer ignition mode in the Alps, which three independent groups document. Multi-decadal records from Valais, the Grisons, Valle d'Aosta and Ticino show that 78\% of lightning fires occur in July and August, and that compared with anthropogenic fires they ignite at higher elevation and on steeper relief and are harder to extinguish because they burn underground. In hot, dry summers such as 2003, \citet{conedera_lightninginduced_2006} report that they became markedly more frequent and costlier to suppress. In Alpine-scale mapping, \citet{gerberding_mapping_2025} found lightning frequency among the three strongest predictors of fire hazard, a rank we would not expect in a Mediterranean model. Similarly, an Austrian prototype classifies lightning fire hazard by elevation, slope, aspect and vegetation \citep{muller_towards_2020}. These studies leave open whether ignition potential and fuel receptiveness will rise together as Alpine summers become hotter and drier.

\subsection{Climate projections and what they omit}

Observed and projected evidence converge on a continuing rise in fire danger, with the forced signal emerging before mid-century \citep{wastl_recent_2012, venalainen_temporal_2014, gerberding_mapping_2025, miller_climate_2024}. Alongside the Alpine increases reported in Section~\ref{sec:intro}, a regional large ensemble for adjacent central Europe under RCP8.5 places the emergence of the forced signal before 2050 in every subregion, and \citet{miller_climate_2024} found present-day hundred-year fire weather shifting to a ten-year recurrence by 2090. The spatial pattern is less secure, since the emphasis on the northern and eastern Alps rests on the central European projection and on the mapping of \citet{gerberding_mapping_2025}, which was co-authored at Eurac Research.

These projections share limitations that are, in our view, structural rather than incidental. Neither represents the fuel loading by drought, storm and bark beetle outbreak (Section~\ref{sec:water}), nor the socio-economic change that drives land use, and both state that local application carries far more uncertainty than the regional trend \citep{gerberding_mapping_2025}. Continental fire models show a related limit, since the Burnt Area Simulator for Europe does not reproduce fire occurrence in temperate Europe and is not evaluated separately in mountains \citep{forrest_understanding_2024}. We therefore read a projection driven by climate normals as describing the climatic envelope rather than the fire regime.

Anticipation may face a further limit. In a global analysis, \citet{cardil_climate_2023} found the links between climate teleconnections and burnt area markedly weaker in Europe than elsewhere and spread across several modes. Seasonal forecasting from remote climate modes is thus likely to deliver less in the Alps, which would put more weight on monitoring the state of the fuel.

\section{Water, Drought and Water Use}
\label{sec:water}

\subsection{Drought, fuel moisture and the physiological chain}

Drought reaches fire through a chain of separable links, and at the end where trees become fuel the evidence is consistent. Disturbance is a normal part of Alpine forest dynamics rather than an exception \citep{bebi_changes_2017}, European forest disturbance has become more frequent over three decades \citep{senf_mapping_2021}, and drought-induced mortality of Scots pine is documented at the dry margins of its range, which include the inner Alpine valleys \citep{giuggiola_droughtinduced_2010}. Taken together, these largely review-based sources (two of which share an author) indicate that drought, storms and bark beetles are loading Alpine forests with dead fuel. Alpine hazard projections acknowledge this pathway yet leave it out \citep{gerberding_mapping_2025}, and we consider the omission unlikely to be small.

How drought produces that fuel is less settled. In Scots pine, carbon starvation may be the main route to mortality, although evidence that reserves are actually exhausted is contested, and \citet{giuggiola_droughtinduced_2010} argue that reduced assimilation would leave fewer resources for defence against pests and pathogens. Because the same stress drives both mortality and fuel loading, we suggest that intervention against drought stress and against fuel are largely the same operation in inner Alpine valleys, an alignment we develop in Section~\ref{sec:forest}. At the near end of the chain, fuel moisture is well established as a driver (Section~\ref{sec:climate}) but poorly modelled where it matters most. In a global evaluation, \citet{rodrigues_vpdbased_2024} found that below 10\% moisture no model exceeded a coefficient of determination of 0.16, which is the range in which spread responds exponentially to dryness.

\subsection{Soil alteration and the limits of severity as a proxy}

Underlying the post-fire hazard chain is the assumption that a severity map can stand in for soil condition. Fire alters mountain soils by removing vegetation and litter, depositing ash, changing water repellency and weathering rock \citep{santi_wildfire_2022}, and in the Alps complete soil loss is most likely on steep subalpine slopes with thin humus over limestone \citep{muller_forest_2020}. Whether severity products reveal such changes has been tested at a single Alpine fire. Three years after a mixed-severity fire in a Scots pine forest, \citet{negri_wildfire_2026} found burnt soils still altered, but not in proportion to mapped severity class, and the strongest laboratory water repellency occurred mainly in low-severity plots. On this one fire, severity mapping was a poor predictor of the soil condition that governs runoff, which would challenge both the standard post-fire workflow and the assumption that a low-intensity burn is hydrologically benign. Spread models leave the same gap, since they supply no thermal boundary conditions at the soil surface, so predicted repellency, runoff and debris-flow initiation stay disconnected from simulated fire behaviour \citep{or_review_2023}.

\subsection{Erosion, debris flow and sub-threshold triggering}

Fire lowers the rainfall intensity needed to trigger debris flows, which is well established outside the Alps as a review-based mechanism \citep{santi_wildfire_2022, rengers_evaluating_2024}. The Alpine record holds two documented events \citep{tiranti_wildfires_2021, laporta_postwildfire_2025}. At Pallanzeno in 2005 and Bussoleno in 2018, both in the Western Italian Alps, debris flows followed brief, moderate rainfall over catchments burnt in the preceding months, and \citet{tiranti_wildfires_2021} describe deposits rich in fine particles and burnt wood.

Bussoleno, in the Susa Valley, is the only Alpine event analysed numerically. An October 2017 fire burnt over 95\% of a 1.37\,km$^2$ catchment, and some eight months later a radar-estimated rainfall of 12\,mm in 30 minutes (an intensity of 24\,mm\,h$^{-1}$, a five-year return period) mobilised roughly 20{,}000\,m$^3$ of sediment and woody debris. The flow destroyed two buildings and forced the evacuation of around 150 residents. In situ tests found the shear strength of burnt soils reduced by a factor of about three. According to \citet{laporta_postwildfire_2025}, the triggering intensity lay below the threshold of more than 30\,mm\,h$^{-1}$ typical of unburnt basins of that type, although complex orography limits the reliability of the radar estimate. Fire therefore appears to lower the rainfall needed to trigger a flow, and so to move the hazard into a return period that occurs several times per decade. As this rests on a single event, it shows that sub-threshold triggering can occur in the Alps rather than how often it does.

Imported evidence suggests that the Alpine value is not anomalous and that the low threshold is temporary. In Glenwood Canyon (Colorado), the triggering threshold in the first post-fire year was 25.9\,mm\,h$^{-1}$, close to the Bussoleno value, and recalculated from the recovering vegetation it rose to 40\,mm\,h$^{-1}$ in the second year \citep{rengers_evaluating_2024}. We therefore treat the threshold as a recovering quantity rather than a constant, so that for Alpine application the length of the low-threshold window matters more than its depth. The same study found the standard volume model overestimating observed erosion by a median factor of 4.4, which argues against importing volume estimates with the same confidence as thresholds.

Sediment supply, rather than runoff alone, may set event magnitude, although each part of this argument rests on one study outside the Alps. In Sardinia, \citet{pala_sediment_2025} found that thermal spalling after a severe fire removed a median of about 20\% of exposed rock surface and that the fragments entered later debris flows. Similarly, lidar differencing of the 2018 Montecito flows (California) led \citet{morell_sediment_2021} to infer that flow volume is controlled by the stored channel sediment available for scour. If both mechanisms hold in the Alps, debris-flow magnitude would depend on rock exposure and channel history as much as on the fire.

\subsection{Water supply, water quality, snow and ice}

Fire degrades water regulation and water quality according to three independent Mediterranean sources, although the signal has not been measured in the Alps, whose catchments supply water far downstream. For a supply reservoir, \citet{basso_potential_2021} modelled a fire scenario that raised median suspended solids by a factor of 4.7, beyond the recommended maximum, while dilution removed much of the added nutrients. However, drought could weaken this dilution, and drought is when fire conditions are present. In a review of 56 Greek studies, every recorded entry for hydrological regulation was negative \citep{misseyanni_impact_2025}, and water regulation is among the services assessed as at risk from fire in Catalonia \citep{lecinadiaz_assessing_2021}. We consider the mobilisation, and its dependence on storage volume, general enough to transfer to the Alps, but not the magnitudes.

Snow currently limits high-elevation fire (Section~\ref{sec:climate}), but the reverse coupling, from fire to the cryosphere, has no Alpine literature, although imported evidence from three independent groups shows that fire changes how much snow mountain catchments store, how fast it melts and the albedo of snow and ice. Canopy loss changes interception and the radiation balance at the snow surface, and burnt debris and black carbon lower albedo \citep{koshkin_wildfire_2022, pepin_climate_2022}, with effects persisting while the canopy recovers. On a glacier in the Canadian Rockies, \citet{aubrywake_fire_2022} found that upwind fires affected melt both through deposited soot and through smoke. The net effect on snow need not run in one direction. In a single burnt basin of the Sierra Nevada, \citet{micheletty_application_2014} found that snow-covered area increased after fire relative to an unburnt control, yet melt-out came earlier. Canopy removal thus put more snow on the ground but removed it sooner, with opposite effects on storage and timing.

Drought strains the Alpine water system as well as driving fire, and the two stresses compound. During the 2022--2023 snow drought, glacier melt in two large Italian Alpine catchments rose to 148\% above its decadal mean, yet streamflow stayed below average, so \citet{leone_20222023_2026} conclude that the glacial buffer only partly compensated for the deficit. On the Canadian glacier, soot from upwind fires increased melt by up to about 10\% in later seasons, although smoke partly offset this \citep{aubrywake_fire_2022}. Taken together, the two results suggest a loop in which drought raises fire danger, exhausts the snowpack and shifts supply onto glaciers, whose melt fire-borne soot may then accelerate, while fire, by shortening the snow season, could relax the constraint that snow places on fire. Because the Alpine side rests on one drought and the soot link on one glacier, we treat this loop as a research priority rather than a result.

\section{Forest: Change, Ecology and Management}
\label{sec:forest}

\subsection{Forest change, composition and fuel connectivity}

Alpine forest that now burns is largely a recent product of land use. For centuries, grazing, litter collection and fuelwood extraction kept Alpine forests open in a mosaic that limited fire spread whatever the weather, and as that regime retreated, forest expanded and the fuel bed filled in again \citep{bebi_changes_2017, garbarino_contrasting_2020, gellrich_agricultural_2007, frei_evidence_2023, stritih_alternative_2023}. Expansion is fastest where recent abandonment coincides with climatic conditions favourable for tree establishment \citep{garbarino_contrasting_2020}. In the Swiss mountains, regrowth has concentrated where cultivation costs are high and yield potential low, and forest area has risen by roughly a third over the last 150 years, mostly through natural regrowth \citep{gellrich_agricultural_2007}. Much of this change is densification rather than upslope advance, since in an intensively surveyed valley in Central Switzerland 79\% of the cells in which trees had established since 1972 lay below the former treeline \citep{frei_evidence_2023}. At Alpine scale, 24\% of forest is in an open-canopy state concentrated at high elevations, forest edges and warm dry sites \citep{stritih_alternative_2023}, which are the positions where fuel is now filling in. Because fuel accumulation follows agricultural marginality, it concentrates on the steep ground where fire runs longest. We suggest that this origin also determines which policy levers can change it.

This denser fuel bed has a direct effect on fire behaviour, although the link has so far been quantified in a single Alpine study. Across Swiss pyroregions, \citet{conedera_linking_2024} found that potential fire-path length (which combines slope length with fuel connectivity) explains a large share of the variance in large-fire statistics. Because expansion onto steep marginal ground increases precisely this quantity, a valley can become more fire-prone without any change in weather. What the fuel bed holds is less well known. The only destructive Alpine fuel inventory, in Austrian spruce and pine forests, found that fuel load depended on region and forest type rather than on stand age, and it left the duff layer unsampled \citep{neumann_fuel_2022}. Fire also enters forests already shaped by wind, snow break, bark beetles and avalanches, which interact rather than simply add up, and roughly half of disturbed Alpine stands have passed into the open-canopy state \citep{bebi_changes_2017, stritih_alternative_2023}. Disturbance regimes are themselves unstable, since about 15\% of European grid cells changed regime within the 1985--2023 record \citep{miguel_unravelling_2026}, and we therefore do not treat a regime as a fixed regional property on which a fuel model can be built once.

Which species grow back matters as much as how much forest grows back, because composition both shapes fire and is shaped by it. Two independent methods agree on how Alpine and Central European tree species rank in fire tolerance. Simulating fire across French Alpine forest plots, \citet{dupire_vulnerability_2019} found mortality in extremely dry summers lowest in the thick-barked larch stands of the inner Alps, whereas Swiss stone pine and mountain pine ranked among the most fire-sensitive species. By heating stem discs of six Central European species, \citet{mutterer_bark_2026} obtained a consistent ranking, with bark thickness appearing to be the most robust predictor of the time the cambium takes to reach the lethal threshold, placing larch, Douglas-fir and Scots pine above silver fir and Norway spruce, and beech lowest. In the other direction, recruitment after a stand-replacing fire in the Rhone valley favoured pioneer birch, aspen and willow over the pre-fire conifers, although this rests on a single burn \citep{moser_potential_2010}. Over millennia, palaeoecological records suggest that fire and grazing rather than temperature alone determined whether the timberline reached its climatic potential \citep{colombaroli_species_2010}, which supports our reading that any correction of the fuel bed must also come through land use.

\subsection{Protective function, biodiversity and the recovery window}

Protective function lost to fire in the Alps recovers over decades rather than years, and in places it may not recover at all \citep{maringer_temporal_2016, gehring_shallow_2019, stritih_alternative_2023, degiuli_arrested_2024, moser_potential_2010}. As reviewed by \citet{santi_wildfire_2022}, the general geomorphological literature cites a post-fire sediment disturbance window of three to four years, occasionally extending to thirty, whereas 72\% of disturbed Alpine forests return to a closed canopy within 35 years and submediterranean forests remain open for decades \citep{stritih_alternative_2023}. Soil properties can still be altered three years after an Alpine mixed-severity fire \citep{negri_wildfire_2026}, and in one Valais burn, seedling establishment required precipitation far above the long-term average \citep{moser_potential_2010}. At the upper margin, on burnt subalpine slopes in the Northern Limestone Alps, \citet{degiuli_arrested_2024} found no meaningful mountain pine re-establishment in about eighty years, which they describe as arrested succession, although this comes from a single massif.

How this loss unfolds has been quantified by one research group in southern Alpine beech. In burnt stands of Ticino and Piedmont, \citet{gehring_shallow_2019} found that root reinforcement fell by roughly 70\% within five years of high-severity fire, with modelled shallow-landslide probability elevated for at least forty years, while \citet{maringer_temporal_2016} found rockfall protection reduced most strongly between ten and thirty years after moderate and high-severity fire. Protection thus appears to reach its lowest point years after the fire, because standing dead stems keep working until they fall and regeneration has not yet replaced them. An assessment made in the first year may therefore measure the wrong part of the recovery curve. As this rests on one group's work in one forest type, it shows that an Alpine window can exceed the standard one by an order of magnitude rather than how often it does. For risk assessment, we therefore take the main parameter to be how long post-fire hazard remains elevated, and we treat that duration as partly a management outcome, to which current Alpine restoration practice appears poorly matched (Section~\ref{sec:fuelbreaks}).

Fire also differs from the other Alpine disturbances in what it leaves on the slope. In the only Alpine simulation of windthrow legacies, in the Dolomites, lying logs and stumps made the fallen stand more protective against rockfall than the forest before it \citep{costa_biological_2021}. By contrast, burnt stems and rootstocks can fall and carry rock with them, as observed at a single Austrian site \citep{melzner_brief_2019}, and most damaged stems in fire-injured beech stands collapse within about two decades \citep{maringer_temporal_2016}. Post-fire rockfall itself remains little studied, and as recently as 2019 no work on it had been published for the eastern Alps \citep{melzner_brief_2019}. Imported evidence suggests that vegetation loss matters most for small blocks \citep{asteriou_assessing_2025}, while a review of three Spanish cases notes that rockfall model coefficients have not been standardised for post-fire conditions \citep{sarro_effects_2021}, so the parameters needed to model a burnt slope appear to be those least constrained by measurement.

Warming adds a slower threat to the same function. Three independent simulation studies project that warming will reshape the composition or biomass of Alpine forests, in one of them more strongly than management will \citep{schumacher_relative_2006, moos_climate_2021, hillebrand_contrasting_2023}. The two that evaluated protection find it eroding at low and montane elevations while subalpine stands hold. Above Martigny in the Valais, \citet{moos_climate_2021} placed annual rockfall risk roughly five times higher without the protection forest than with it. This risk rises under climate scenarios as composition shifts from beech to pubescent oak (monetary values in Section~\ref{sec:economics}). Along an elevation gradient in South Tyrol, \citet{hillebrand_contrasting_2023} showed that under severe warming montane forests decline drastically in avalanche protection, whereas subalpine stands remain stable or improve.

Ecological consequences are mixed, and two independent Alpine studies show why abundance and composition should be kept apart. At two burnt sites in the inner-Alpine Valais, \citet{rey_effects_2019} found that high-severity fire roughly halved bird abundance without significantly changing species richness, while red-listed and priority species were more common in burnt stands. Similarly, the burnt subalpine slopes in the Northern Limestone Alps carried higher vascular plant richness than unburnt controls, including alpine specialists, even though tree regeneration failed \citep{degiuli_arrested_2024}. At European scale, a single continental assessment ranks temperate conifer forest second among biomes in ecological vulnerability, driven by recovery time rather than by resistance \citep{arrogantefunes_mapping_2024}. However, a systematic assessment imported from the Colorado Front Range found that mountain wildfire mostly reduces ecosystem services, while noting a possible bias towards negative framing in the research questions \citep{vukomanovic_systematic_2019}. Because we found no Alpine estimate of faunal mortality, we treat faunal losses as unmeasured rather than small.

We read these findings as showing that Alpine losses have several components that behave differently. Protection is spatially concentrated above assets and slow to recover, and the ecological value lost during recovery is high for the dominant Alpine forest type, whereas fire is negative for abundance and closed-forest specialists but positive for open-habitat species. Combining these components into a single measure invites treating fire either as uniformly destructive or as ecologically benign, and we therefore keep them apart in the risk framing of this review.
\subsection{Managing for protection: silviculture, species and fuel treatment}

Independent sources agree that protection rather than timber is the management objective across much of the Alps, where stands are kept in a structure that can stop rockfall, anchor the snowpack and hold soil on steep ground \citep{dinca_timber_2025, jandl_management_2013, accastello_asforesee_2019}. As reviewed by \citet{dinca_timber_2025}, harvesting costs in difficult terrain are often not covered by timber revenue, and \citet{accastello_asforesee_2019} found that protective thinning often has a negative stumpage value on steep slopes. For the Alpine Space programme MANFRED, \citet{jandl_management_2013} described protection as by far the most important ecosystem service of mountain forests. Both Alpine valuations of this service, by replacement cost \citep{accastello_asforesee_2019} and by avoided risk \citep{moos_climate_2021}, find it substantial (Section~\ref{sec:economics}), and it is this value that fire puts at risk.

Maintaining protective structure is becoming harder at low and mid elevations, where climate is projected to change forest composition \citep{schumacher_relative_2006} and to erode protection while subalpine stands hold \citep{hillebrand_contrasting_2023, moos_climate_2021}, as described earlier in this section. Silvicultural effort should therefore go where protection is most exposed rather than where the forest is most productive. Fire also sharpens a conflict between objectives. In scenario modelling of Austrian forests, \citet{jandl_strategies_2018} found stem biomass rising by 34\% over the century under a reduced-harvest strategy, which maximises stored carbon but, in our reading, also maximises standing fuel. As a single study, it shows the direction of the trade-off rather than its size. We found no Alpine study that optimises species selection jointly for protection, carbon, timber and fire resistance.

For grazing, two reviews and one national analysis indicate that grazing animals and active agro-pastoral land use can lower fire impact where abandonment has led to shrub and tree encroachment, although most of this evidence is imported \citep{rouetleduc_effects_2021, xu_how_2026, spadoni_active_2023}. As reviewed by \citet{xu_how_2026}, Mediterranean studies report burnt-area reductions between 25.9\% and 60.9\%, although the effect reverses under heavy use, which can leave a fuel bed more flammable than the one it replaced, at a threshold not yet located for any Alpine pasture. However, the Alpine evidence is weaker and less consistent. In the Valais, \citet{zumbrunnen_human_2012} found livestock density in forests associated with fewer fires before the late 1950s but with more fires after 1956, a reversal we attribute to dryness because those densities lay in the driest part of the canton. In the Australian Alps, published evidence suggests that alpine grazing has no significant effect on fire risk (Section~\ref{sec:governance}). We therefore treat grazing as a plausible but unverified Alpine fuel treatment.

Whether treatment works better when embedded in agricultural land use has been quantified by only one modelling group, in Iberian landscapes. \citet{pais_optimizing_2023} found that treatment avoided one hectare of wildfire per hectare treated only when combined with restoration of high nature value farmland and fire-smart forest conversion, and \citet{lecinadiaz_incorporating_2023} found that fire-smart forestry alone raised suppression costs. Across 602 Italian agro-forest districts, Alpine ones included, \citet{spadoni_active_2023} found smaller fire impacts where land governance (represented by proxies such as extensive agro-pastoral activity) was more active, although the effect was moderate compared with climate and flammability. Agricultural policy may therefore be a more effective lever than forest policy alone, but because the modelling comes from one group and the Italian evidence is correlational, we hold this as a hypothesis for the Alps.

Mechanical thinning has a justification independent of fire. Reviewing drought-induced Scots pine mortality in inner Alpine valleys, \citet{giuggiola_droughtinduced_2010} suggest that thinning and shrub removal could ease drought stress, although whether it does so in the most sensitive areas remains unclear. Where it does, the same operations remove surface and ladder fuel, so the case for thinning does not rest on a fire projection. Two independent studies also indicate that treatment achieves most under moderate fire weather, since its benefit for tree survival declined under extreme conditions in a Californian experiment \citep{brodie_forest_2024}, and the mitigating effect of land governance weakened similarly across Italian districts \citep{spadoni_active_2023}. Because Alpine fire danger is projected to rise from low towards moderate levels \citep{gerberding_mapping_2025, miller_climate_2024}, the window in which treatment pays best is likely to be opening now.

However, thinning also trades against the function it is meant to protect. Even a treatment that retains the canopy removes standing stems that slow falling blocks \citep{moos_climate_2021} and lying material that adds a barrier effect \citep{costa_biological_2021}. Because protective capacity can be valued by its replacement cost \citep{accastello_asforesee_2019}, the acceptable degree of thinning is an empirical question with a monetary answer that no study has yet computed. We regard it as the most tractable unanswered question in Alpine fuel management, namely how much understorey and stem removal a protection forest can absorb before its protective function declines faster than its fire hazard.

\subsection{Fuel breaks, prescribed fire and post-fire restoration}
\label{sec:fuelbreaks}

Linear firebreaks and fuelbreaks are weak barriers on their own, and although the evidence is imported, it comes from independent groups \citep{zong_effectiveness_2026, ortega_modeling_2024, ascoli_firebreak_2018}. As reviewed by \citet{zong_effectiveness_2026}, firebreaks stopped only 28\% of large fires in southern California, and some 73\% of wildfires in western Arnhem Land (northern Australia) breached them fully or partially, mainly through firebrand transport. Where fuelbreaks do work, they appear to act as platforms for ground suppression, since in Andalusia \citet{ortega_modeling_2024} found containment rising from 6.66\% without suppression to 76.74\% with ground crews on the line. In Alpine terrain, however, the slope and access conditions that motivate a fuelbreak also prevent crews from holding it, and a break on the contour meets an upslope fire run \citep{conedera_linking_2024} at the perpendicular angle at which unsupported breaks held least often \citep{ortega_modeling_2024}.

Following \citet{ascoli_firebreak_2018}, fuelbreaks that retain vegetation (including shaded ones, which keep a thinned tree cover) are considered better suited than cleared firebreaks to steep terrain because they limit soil erosion. We found no design guidance calibrated to Alpine fuels and slopes, and the thinning trade-off described above still applies. Large linear networks also suit small private parcels poorly \citep{ascoli_firebreak_2018}, a description that fits much of the Alpine arc and that Catalan subsidy data bear out (Section~\ref{sec:economics}). In contrast, a cleared margin next to a building serves as defensible space rather than as a containment line, so its high expert ranking (Section~\ref{sec:risk}) does not, in our view, contradict the weak landscape-scale record.

Prescribed burning is little used or prohibited in most Alpine countries. As \citet{muller_forest_2020} report, it is applied at scale in France, where 1{,}000 to 3{,}000\,ha are treated annually in the Alpes-Maritimes, but outside the Alpine region proper. The structural objections are real, because the dominant species rank low in fire tolerance (as described earlier in this section) and the spring burn window coincides with the peak of human-caused fires \citep{muller_forest_2020}. In Austria the obstacle is also legal, since the Forestry Act prohibits lighting fires in forest \citep{neumann_fuel_2022}. Both sources come from one Vienna-based group. Against them stands a single Alpine trial, in which \citet{gamba_prescribed_2025} found that burning roughly halved fuel load in a Scots pine stand with minimal effect on tree water status and soil. Because the trial covers one stand of a fire-tolerant species over one year and comes from one Turin group, we read it as showing that the question can be answered empirically in the Alps rather than that it has been.

The nearest operational precedent is imported from the Pyrenees, where the Aran valley adopted in 2022 a fire management plan through a community participation process, with a target of burning 0.66\% of natural land cover annually \citep{oliveres_path_2025}. Its winter burning windows and transhumance history resemble Alpine conditions, but its authors acknowledge that economic sustainability is unassessed and that managed fire can escape. As a single case, it suggests that adoption may depend as much on a participatory process as on ecological evidence.

Taken together, Alpine fuel management is likely to be mainly area-based rather than linear, with linear elements confined to ridgelines, road corridors and the interface, and to rest on grazing of abandoned slopes \citep{rouetleduc_effects_2021}, thinning that removes fuel and may ease drought stress \citep{giuggiola_droughtinduced_2010}, prescribed burning where the stand tolerates it (on the strength of one trial) \citep{gamba_prescribed_2025} and prioritisation of the terrain positions from which large fires develop \citep{conedera_linking_2024}.

Post-fire management determines how long the period of reduced protection described earlier in this section lasts, yet independent sources agree that it is the least developed phase of the fire management cycle in research, institutions and Alpine practice \citep{goncalves_fostering_2025, vieira_postfire_2026, mantero_short-term_2023}. In a bibliometric analysis, \citet{goncalves_fostering_2025} found the recovery phase around 46\% less studied than the pre-fire phase. Across the European Union, \citet{vieira_postfire_2026} locate the obstacle in a gap between science and operational practice rather than in a lack of tools, drawing on Greek, Portuguese and Spanish experience. After a large fire in the Western Alps, \citet{mantero_short-term_2023} concluded that the restoration techniques most often applied are poorly matched to the ecological context. We expect the Alpine case to be the harder one, because intervention must prevent gravitational rather than agronomic consequences, and salvage after fire is still decided on evidence from windthrow, which is itself less settled than often assumed \citep{seben_impact_2024} and concerns a barrier legacy that burnt stems do not provide.

\section{Risk, Exposure and Vulnerability}
\label{sec:risk}

\subsection{From hazard to risk: the missing terms}

Methods that separate hazard, exposure and vulnerability have been developed and applied in several European settings \citep{papathomakohle_towards_2025, papathomakohle_wildfire_2022, riviere_mapping_2023, arrogantefunes_mapping_2024, lecinadiaz_assessing_2021, pede_mountain_2022}. Where damage records are absent, indicators are weighted by expert judgement \citep{papathomakohle_towards_2025}, whereas where they exist, indices are calibrated on surveyed damage \citep{papathomakohle_wildfire_2022}, and the ecological component can be mapped as value lost over the recovery period \citep{arrogantefunes_mapping_2024}. However, the Alpine applications cover one settlement \citep{papathomakohle_towards_2025} or one climate-risk zone \citep{pede_mountain_2022}, and we found no study that joins any of these methods to a wildfire hazard surface for the Alpine arc. Alpine assessments therefore end at a hazard surface (Section~\ref{sec:hazard}) that is then read as if it were a risk map. Susceptibility, the static probability that a place experiences fire given the intrinsic characteristics of the territory \citep{trucchia_defining_2022}, is a legitimate quantity, but in mountains it stands unusually far from risk, because the exposed asset usually lies outside the burn perimeter but beneath it, so that we expect burnt area alone to be a poor guide to consequence (an inference from where exposure lies, since no Alpine study has related damage to burnt area). We regard this missing join as the gap that most directly separates the Alpine literature from a usable risk assessment, and closing it would mean combining layers that already exist (Section~\ref{sec:governance}).

\subsection{Exposure: habitats, assets, people and seasonality}

Exposure in mountains is dynamic, because human presence follows tourism, recreation and the wildland--urban interface more closely than resident population does \citep{gelabert_assessing_2025, pede_mountain_2022, muller_forest_2020, hoang_stakeholders_2026}. Across five European pilot sites, distance to the wildland--urban interface was the most relevant human predictor of ignition, although the models treat it as static and their authors recommend covariates that vary with commuting and seasonal recreation \citep{gelabert_assessing_2025}. Alpine practitioner and stakeholder surveys, which come from one research group, name recreation and tourism among the leading socio-economic drivers \citep{muller_forest_2020, hoang_stakeholders_2026}. For one Piedmont zone, \citet{pede_mountain_2022} expect shorter high-altitude snow cover to draw summer visitors, while local fire danger rises mainly in spring, so the overlap between visitors and fire season remains open. Institutions may also shape exposure, since a transboundary simulation in the Saxon--Bohemian borderland, so far available only as a preprint, suggests that modelled burn probability and flame length at settlements can differ across a national border \citep{avouris_exposure_2025}.

The ecological component is the one part measured at continental scale. In 2025, 39\% of the area burnt in the European Union lay inside Natura 2000 sites, most of it in Spain, Portugal and Romania, and the four fires mapped in Austria lay almost entirely inside such sites \citep{jrc_advance_2026}. Because these four fires are a small fraction of the Austrian fire count (Section~\ref{sec:data}), we read the coincidence as established on the detected tail rather than on the regime. Since much of what is exposed lies beyond the burnt area, exposure analysis should, in our view, follow the cascade rather than the fire. In south-eastern France, for example, a participative approach that separated exposure, sensitivity and adaptive capacity identified secondary vulnerability hotspots and places not yet affected where prevention was warranted \citep{riviere_mapping_2023}.

Compound structure enters at the preconditions \citep{miller_compounding_2025}, in disturbances that load the fuel bed \citep{bebi_changes_2017} (Section~\ref{sec:forest}) and in the post-fire cascade (Section~\ref{sec:water}). Exposure of infrastructure to that cascade has been quantified only in California, where the likelihood that natural gas transmission faces intense rainfall over burnt ground is projected nearly to double \citep{moftakhari_increasing_2019}. The method is likely to transfer, yet we found no Alpine equivalent for roads, rail and pipelines beneath burnt slopes.

\subsection{Vulnerability of the built environment}

For the Alps, building vulnerability has been constructed only from expert judgement, because no Austrian damage record exists from which to derive weights. \citet{papathomakohle_towards_2025} weighted indicators of building environment, exterior and characteristics through a national and an international expert panel and applied the index to one Carinthian settlement. Both panels ranked protective strips or firebreaks first among the environment indicators, crown distance first among the exterior indicators and roof material first among the building indicators.

Where a damage record does exist, the weights differ. Calibrating an index on buildings surveyed after a fire in Attica, \citet{papathomakohle_wildfire_2022} ranked roof material first and terrain slope third, whereas both Austrian panels gave slope a low weight. The two indices come from one group but rest on independent data, and they agree on roof material for a known reason, since most buildings are lost to embers and surface fire, which enter through roofing, vents and eaves, rather than to a flame front \citep{naser_vulnerability_2025}. Roof material is therefore the indicator most readily adopted without local calibration. Slope is where transfer is weakest, because the two indices disagree on the variable that most distinguishes the Alpine setting from the Mediterranean one in which the damage was recorded.

\section{Governance, Policy and Economics}
\label{sec:governance}

\subsection{Suppression, preparedness and the practitioner constraint}

Terrain and access, rather than fire size, are the binding constraint on mountain suppression, and large fires develop where this constraint meets continuous fuel \citep{muller_forest_2020, conedera_linking_2024, cardil_assessing_2025}, although the quantitative evidence on containment is imported from California \citep{cardil_assessing_2025}. Most large Swiss fires have spread along long, continuous forested upslope runs where they proved hard to suppress \citep{conedera_linking_2024}. The constraint also changes what counts as extreme. In the Alps a fire of a few hectares is operationally extreme if it removes the protective forest above a settlement or corridor \citep{muller_forest_2020}, so escalation rules calibrated on burnt area are likely to miss the events that matter most.

Alpine suppression is thus organised around water logistics and vertical distance \citep{muller_forest_2020}. Road density is not the main limitation, since it is high compared with Mediterranean or Scandinavian Europe, although road practicability repeatedly causes problems \citep{muller_forest_2020}. What limits access is the slope and the vertical separation between road and fire, which corrects an assumption easily imported from Mediterranean practice. Organisation differs as well. In Austria and Switzerland, dense volunteer brigades and reliable smoke reporting mean that most fires are attacked soon after ignition \citep{muller_forest_2020}. We suggest that this may partly explain a size distribution dominated by sub-hectare fires, although the inference is untested and no Alpine hazard model contains a term for response density.

A quantitative framework for the accessibility constraint exists, but not yet in the Alps. Calibrated on Californian initial attack, the terrain difficulty index of \citet{cardil_assessing_2025} combines accessibility, penetrability, line construction, slope and road proximity, and in its highest class the odds of successful initial attack are 90\% lower than in the lowest. Because it has been validated outside its region of origin, the Alpine gap is one of application rather than method. Since suppression cannot be fast everywhere, the places where it must be fast can be identified in advance from terrain and fuel connectivity, which is the case for pre-suppression infrastructure such as water points for aerial firefighting \citep{conedera_linking_2024}.

How suppression and fuel treatment interact is, for the Alps, a matter of inference. In imported evidence, treatment benefit declines under the most severe fire weather \citep{brodie_forest_2024}, whereas initial-attack success falls with terrain difficulty \citep{cardil_assessing_2025}. On accessible ground under moderate danger we would therefore expect the two to act as substitutes. In contrast, on steep slopes with long upslope runs \citep{conedera_linking_2024}, treatment is the only lever that acts before the fire, yet mechanised treatment costs most there \citep{dinca_timber_2025}, which is why the pastoral and silvicultural instruments of Section~\ref{sec:forest} matter most there.

No Alpine study gives the marginal effectiveness of a euro spent on suppression against one spent on treatment, conditioned on terrain. Beyond allocation, one continental modelling study suggests that sustained investment in capacity offsets most but not all of the climate-driven increase in burnt area, with one of its two models still projecting rises of 200 to 300\% along the Alpine foothills under high emissions \citep{billing_future_2026}. On this single projection, capacity appears to be the largest lever within the region's control but unlikely to suffice alone in the foothills.

\subsection{Prevention: placement, early warning and integrated fire management}

Prevention effort is not reaching the places where it is needed. Four independent studies point this way through different mechanisms, namely adaptation measures placed where they are institutionally feasible \citep{dubo_nature-based_2023}, subsidies that reach whichever owners apply \citep{gorrizmifsud_current_2026}, owners who ignore spillovers to neighbours \citep{bayham_economics_2022} and an emergency default that persists despite commitments to prevention \citep{kirschner_governance_2024}. We therefore read the evidence as convergent on the direction of the problem rather than on any one mechanism, and only the first study is wholly Alpine. Mapping 97 nature-based solutions across the Alps, \citet{dubo_nature-based_2023} found them generally located neither in hotspots of the hazards they addressed nor in hotspots of the ecosystem contributions they aimed to secure.

A low-cost correction is available, because the two layers needed to rank treatment sites by expected avoided loss already exist. Large-fire likelihood can be mapped from slope length and fuel connectivity using a terrain model and a forest map \citep{conedera_linking_2024}, and protective function can be valued per hectare by replacement cost \citep{accastello_asforesee_2019}. In our assessment, what is missing is the institutional mandate to combine them, because the hazard layer sits with fire and forest services and the value layer with the authorities responsible for the assets below. A transferable ranking method exists in Catalonia, where \citet{krsnik_spatial_2024} included accessibility among their criteria. Expert weighting there designated 20.69\% of the territory as high priority and equal weighting 7.98\%, which suggests that the participatory step shapes the result as much as the data do.

The most tractable near-term gain, in our view, lies in extending early warning systems that already exist, since Alpine torrent and avalanche services lack a fire term. The one concrete Alpine proposal is to adjust runoff parameters automatically for burnt catchments, so that debris-flow warning lowers its rainfall thresholds without waiting for a field survey \citep{tiranti_wildfires_2021}, and the Bussoleno event (Section~\ref{sec:water}) illustrates why this is needed. Such outputs would be most useful as decision thresholds that build in the asymmetry between missed detections and false alarms, as Alpine danger and ignition models have begun to do \citep{muller_towards_2020, moreno_spacetime_2025}, the latter so far as a preprint.

Integrated fire management likewise needs indicators that different institutions can observe and act on. \citet{melzner_wildfire-induced_2025} propose post-fire indicators of burn severity and geohydrological response, framed for policymakers, and \citet{baselt_compound_2026} call for the multi-institutional monitoring networks that would supply them. Each component has been proposed separately, and what is missing, as we see it, is their assembly. Targets are also largely missing from European wildfire risk management, and where fire has been rare they would have to rest on local assessments of fuel, exposure and vulnerability rather than on fire statistics \citep{berchtold_towards_2025}.

\subsection{Fragmentation, harmonisation and cross-border coordination}

Wildfire governance is fragmented across agencies, between Alpine countries and within the European policy framework \citep{kirschner_governance_2024, hoang_stakeholders_2026, plana_framing_2024, bardsley_wildfire_2021}. The cascade crosses an institutional boundary at every link, from forest administrations and fire services to geological surveys, infrastructure operators and planning authorities, so that the sequence of Section~\ref{sec:water} passes through all of them. Drivers also differ between countries, with rural abandonment dominating in France and Italy and tourism in Austria, Germany and Switzerland \citep{muller_forest_2020}, so prevention would work through agricultural policy in the west and south but through visitor management in the north and east.

Fragmentation extends to basic facts. Surveying three stakeholder groups across seven Alpine countries, \citet{hoang_stakeholders_2026} found significant differences in perceptions of fire numbers, burnt area and trend, although the groups agreed on the dominant drivers. Across seven Italian regions (three of them Alpine), experts described a fragmented structure in which emergency response persists as the default, and 58\% of the opportunities they identified concerned prevention \citep{kirschner_governance_2024}, which we take as evidence that the constraint is institutional rather than technical. One level up, integrated fire management is named only once, in passing, in an EU law or policy text \citep{plana_framing_2024}. Thus the coordination problem lies not only among eight countries but also in a supranational framework that barely names the practice they would align to. A single survey in Ticino found that residents who strongly support firefighting still see urban expansion as the main driver of risk \citep{bardsley_wildfire_2021}, so that support for suppression is not evidence of support for land-use restraint.

Harmonisation has been driven by projects. The first harmonised Alpine fire database came from a transnational project \citep{valese_wildfires_2011}, later reused in national danger assessment \citep{muller_towards_2020}, and a later EUSALP project produced a costed Alpine white paper \citep{muller_forest_2020}, but no standing infrastructure exists at Alpine scale. The one documented standing body for a whole range is imported, in the Australian Alps, where it has aligned agencies but did not prevent a cattle-grazing trial for fire-risk reduction despite published evidence suggesting that alpine grazing has no significant effect on fire risk \citep{lockwood_biodiversity_2014}, which suggests that standing coordination does not by itself settle land-use disputes.

\subsection{The economics of Alpine wildfire}
\label{sec:economics}

Spending on Alpine wildfire is dominated by response, although four independent analyses judge prevention to be the more efficient use of money \citep{muller_forest_2020, michetti_forest_2019, lecinadiaz_incorporating_2023, kirschner_governance_2024}. Direct firefighting and post-fire management across the arc are estimated at around €75 million a year, against roughly €10 million a year for a proposed integrated fire management framework \citep{muller_forest_2020}. These are costed estimates rather than measured outcomes, but we take the ratio to frame prevention as inexpensive relative to the response it would displace. \citet{michetti_forest_2019} reached the same recommendation from Italian regional econometrics, and in an Iberian scenario analysis the suppression-cost saving came from reversing agricultural abandonment rather than from fire-smart forestry \citep{lecinadiaz_incorporating_2023}. However, none of these studies measures the marginal return on either kind of expenditure.

What the forest protects is also worth more than what it produces. By replacement cost, \citet{accastello_asforesee_2019} value an Alpine Scots pine protection stand at €950 per hectare per year, and the avoided-risk valuation of a Valais protection forest by \citet{moos_climate_2021} places annual rockfall risk at 19{,}100\,CHF with the forest and 95{,}500\,CHF without it, a risk reduction of about 1{,}350\,CHF per hectare per year in this one modelled case and a protective effect that weakens under climate scenarios (Section~\ref{sec:forest}). Where extraction is technically demanding and economically marginal \citep{dinca_timber_2025}, the timber value is frequently the smaller number. Socio-economic conditions may also act as fire management variables, since in northern Italy a one-percentage-point rise in tertiary education was associated with 11\% fewer fire events \citep{michetti_forest_2019}.

Delivery is a further problem, because treatment that must be contiguous is often funded through instruments that allocate to whoever applies, as the Catalan subsidies of Section~\ref{sec:fuelbreaks} illustrate \citep{gorrizmifsud_current_2026}. As reviewed by \citet{bayham_economics_2022}, a private owner weighs private costs and benefits while ignoring spillovers such as fire crossing parcel boundaries, and liability rules and insurance shape these incentives by jurisdiction. With small parcels and eight legal systems, we expect these to be first-order determinants of whether Alpine fuel treatment happens, although no Alpine study has examined them.

Pastoral systems sit on both sides of the Alpine ledger, and neither side has been priced. Grazing has the broadest policy reach among fuel treatments, although its effect sizes come from outside the Alps (Section~\ref{sec:forest}) and evidence reported from the Australian Alps suggests no significant effect \citep{lockwood_biodiversity_2014}. As reviewed by \citet{xu_how_2026}, it is cost-effective relative to mechanical clearance, and in our reading its marginal cost to a fire budget is largely that of sustaining a contracting pastoral economy. Alpine pasture and stock are also exposed, at their highest elevation during the summer fire season. Even so, we found no Alpine study that values either side.

What the economics cannot yet resolve is distribution. Valley communities bear the costs of both hazard and prevention, while much of the protective benefit accrues to through-traffic, downstream water users and regions outside the mountains. As reviewed by \citet{vukomanovic_systematic_2019}, such services propagate across scales, and frameworks that treat adaptive capacity as part of vulnerability appear closest to representing this asymmetry \citep{riviere_mapping_2023, lecinadiaz_assessing_2021}. We found no Alpine study that quantifies who bears the costs and who receives the benefits.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Read across Sections~\ref{sec:hazard} to~\ref{sec:governance}, several findings recur in the work of independent groups, and we treat them as the most secure results of this review. Most Alpine fires burn less than a hectare and fall below the mapping thresholds of the continental burnt-area products on which European regime analyses rest. Change in the Alpine regime is therefore likely to be under-observed \citep{valese_wildfires_2011, muller_forest_2020, beloiu_climatedriven_2026, forrest_understanding_2024}, although the two Alpine inventories among these sources share one consortium lineage and count once. Fuel continuity is growing, yet it originates in pastoral abandonment and in infilling below the treeline rather than in fire exclusion. It thereby lengthens the continuous forested upslope runs from which large Alpine fires develop \citep{garbarino_contrasting_2020, frei_evidence_2023, conedera_linking_2024, muller_forest_2020}. At the same time, the climatic envelope of fire is expanding upslope across European mountains \citep{beloiu_climatedriven_2026}, snow-cover duration has shortened strongly below 2{,}000\,m across the Alps \citep{pepin_climate_2022}, and fire danger is projected to rise most, in relative terms, in the northern and eastern Alps, where operational fire experience is least \citep{gerberding_mapping_2025, miller_climate_2024}. That emphasis rests on two studies, one of them co-authored at Eurac Research. The climatic evidence as a whole converges from different facets (observed fire elevation, snow cover and projected danger) rather than replicating a single measurement.

These trajectories converge on the same steep, forested slopes above settlements and transport corridors, so we expect their consequences to depend more on the protective function at stake than on burnt area, although no Alpine study has yet related damage to burnt area directly. At the sites studied, that function was lost for years to decades after moderate and high-severity fire, and recovery was slow and at the upper forest margin sometimes arrested \citep{maringer_temporal_2016, gehring_shallow_2019, stritih_alternative_2023, degiuli_arrested_2024, melzner_wildfire-induced_2025}, although the two beech studies among these come from one research group, and the estimate of canopy recovery covers all disturbances rather than fire alone. The tools brought in to assess this risk also lose skill in Alpine terrain. Danger indices require recalibration and pooled ignition models lose skill at regional level. Spread models that reproduce perimeters can diverge on fireline intensity, spectral severity can decouple from soil damage, and continental models are neither calibrated on small fires nor evaluated in mountains separately \citep{de_angelis_modelling_2015, zacharakis_environmental_2023, gelabert_assessing_2025, malanchini_modeling_2025, negri_wildfire_2026, forrest_understanding_2024, hayajneh_wind_2026, or_review_2023}. Each of these components rests on one or two sources. However, the pattern recurs across unrelated groups and in two multi-study reviews, so the finding holds across tools rather than tool by tool. Read in this way, the mismatches collected in Table~\ref{tab:contrast} follow from the Alpine trends above rather than forming a list of separate technical failures.

These mismatches also compound one another, because detection thresholds that omit most Alpine fires starve local danger and spread models of calibration data, and this scarcity in turn encourages practitioners to borrow indices, severity proxies and vulnerability weights calibrated elsewhere. Several rows of Table~\ref{tab:contrast} go further and reverse the sign of an operational relationship rather than merely rescaling it. Thinning a protection forest to reduce fuel can degrade the rockfall barrier it is meant to defend (Section~\ref{sec:forest}), ground-crew containment is weakest on the long, steep forested runs where large-fire propensity is highest (Section~\ref{sec:governance}), and after fire a steep catchment can fail under a common rainstorm rather than an extreme one (Section~\ref{sec:water}).

Other quantities on which the argument draws rest on one study, one site or one event, and they remain open. Sub-threshold triggering of a post-fire debris flow has been analysed numerically in one Alpine catchment \citep{laporta_postwildfire_2025} (Section~\ref{sec:water}). The Alpine post-fire rockfall record rests on one fire \citep{melzner_brief_2019}, and both the forty-year persistence of shallow-landslide disposition in beech and the arrested regeneration on one subalpine massif come from single sites (Section~\ref{sec:forest}). Similarly, the divergence of spread models on fireline intensity rests on one Alpine comparison \citep{malanchini_modeling_2025} (Section~\ref{sec:hazard}), and the decoupling of spectral severity from soil damage on one Alpine fire \citep{negri_wildfire_2026} (Section~\ref{sec:water}). The upslope rate of roughly 72\,m per decade comes from one pan-European analysis \citep{beloiu_climatedriven_2026} (Section~\ref{sec:intro}). Prescribed burning has been tried in one stand of a fire-tolerant species (Section~\ref{sec:fuelbreaks}), and protective function has been monetised in two stands (Section~\ref{sec:economics}). These magnitudes are observed values rather than estimates of Alpine parameters, because a single case can show that a mechanism operates in the Alps and indicate its order of magnitude, but it cannot show how often the mechanism operates.

None of this implies that fire already dominates Alpine forest loss. In their arc-wide regime classification, \citet{conedera_characterizing_2018} concluded that wildfire does not currently rival windstorms and bark beetles for overall forest damage in the Alps, and as that remains the only arc-wide comparison we know of, our claim concerns the trajectory of systemic risk and the blind spots of burnt-area metrics, not the present ranking of disturbances. The hydrogeomorphic cascade receives detailed attention here because existing fire-hazard assessments omit it and because it breaks the return-period assumptions of Alpine engineering, not because it accounts for most of the loss.

If this reading is right, it points to a different orientation for management. Writing from experience outside the Alps, \citet{moritz_learning_2014} argued that a command-and-control response neglects the role of fire regimes in sustaining biodiversity and ecosystem services, and that coexistence through place-based approaches linked to land-use planning may serve better than exclusion. This is a perspective rather than a result tested in the Alps. Even so, it fits a range that is building its fire management now rather than reforming an entrenched system, and that could therefore, in principle, adopt an adaptation-led approach without first passing through a century of suppression. Several adaptation and mitigation policies (among them carbon sequestration, reduced management intensity and protective forest expansion) add standing biomass and fuel continuity (Section~\ref{sec:forest}), and expanding forest cover enlarges the area that can burn \citep{gerberding_mapping_2025}. This is not an argument against those policies, but fire hazard should enter their appraisal; the placement of nature-based solutions and prevention mandates is discussed in Section~\ref{sec:governance}. Because no single body can manage Alpine wildfire risk alone, we propose dividing responsibility over time. Forest and fire authorities would own prevention and suppression, a post-fire susceptibility phase lasting years would be owned jointly with torrent, avalanche and infrastructure authorities, and the fire itself would trigger the handover between them, which at present has no institutional form.

Research needs follow from the same diagnosis, and each discipline holds part of them. In hazard modelling, spread simulators would be more useful if evaluated on fireline intensity and surface thermal boundary conditions rather than on perimeter, since that is where the simulators compared on Alpine fires disagreed \citep{malanchini_modeling_2025} and existing spread frameworks omit the soil-heating terms that post-fire models require \citep{or_review_2023}. Climate and meteorology could test whether correcting interpolated fields for foehn, valley-wind and channelling regimes improves danger prediction, and could follow the fire, snow and glacier coupling that no Alpine study has yet measured (Section~\ref{sec:water}). In hydrology and geomorphology, the efficient path is probably to make existing debris-flow warning systems fire-aware, as \citet{tiranti_wildfires_2021} proposed, alongside long-term post-fire monitoring of the kind the western United States already has \citep{melzner_wildfire-induced_2025}. For forest ecology and silviculture, the most tractable question is probably the degree of thinning at which a protection forest loses protective capacity faster than fire hazard falls, ahead of prescribed burning in fire-intolerant stands and post-fire salvage trials (Section~\ref{sec:fuelbreaks}). Risk and exposure assessment needs finer detection of small fires and an arc-wide exposure and vulnerability register validated against Alpine damage, without which hazard surfaces cannot be converted into risk (Section~\ref{sec:risk}). Governance and economics could estimate the marginal return on suppression capability compared with fuel treatment with a terrain-conditioned framework developed for California \citep{cardil_assessing_2025}, and could value grazing and the distribution of costs and benefits across valley communities (Section~\ref{sec:economics}).

\label{sec:limitations}
These findings should be read within the limits of the evidence base. We built the corpus from two starting documents, a keyword search and one iteration of citation snowballing, and such a corpus inherits the blind spots of its seeds. Forward chaining also under-represents grey literature, national reports and publications in languages other than English (Supporting Information (Text~S1)). We consider this gap material, because a substantial part of Alpine fire knowledge exists in Italian, German, French and Slovenian technical reports, and the harmonisation of Alpine fire statistics has itself been driven by individual projects \citep{valese_wildfires_2011}. Because the studies cannot be pooled quantitatively (Section~\ref{sec:scope}), the numbers they report serve as illustrations of magnitude. Several mechanisms examined here are also established mainly outside the Alps, including terrain effects on surface winds, convective wind--slope coupling, post-fire channel sediment supply, fire--snowpack interactions and terrain-conditioned containment difficulty \citep{sharples_overview_2009, hayajneh_wind_2026, morell_sediment_2021, koshkin_wildfire_2022, cardil_assessing_2025}. Such studies serve here to show that a mechanism exists and what imported tools omit, never to assign an Alpine coefficient.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

In this interdisciplinary review we set the emerging wildfire evidence from the European Alps against the tools, monitoring products and management doctrines routinely imported from Mediterranean and continental fire science. Our principal conclusion is that Alpine wildfire is not a smaller version of the Mediterranean problem but a structurally different risk system. Imported assumptions break down along the chain from weather observation to post-fire governance.

The findings that carry this conclusion are each supported by several independent studies. Alpine fire is bimodal, combining a spring season of human-caused surface fires with a summer season in which lightning becomes an important ignition source (Section~\ref{sec:hazard}). Most fires burn less than a hectare \citep{muller_forest_2020}, below continental satellite mapping thresholds \citep{forrest_understanding_2024}. Pastoral abandonment and sub-treeline infilling, rather than fire exclusion, have assembled continuous fuel on the long, steep slopes from which large fires develop \citep{garbarino_contrasting_2020, frei_evidence_2023, conedera_linking_2024}. Meanwhile, the climatic envelope of fire is moving upslope, and danger is projected to rise most in the less experienced northern and eastern sectors \citep{beloiu_climatedriven_2026, gerberding_mapping_2025, miller_climate_2024}. Much of the forest belt is managed to arrest rockfall, snow avalanches and shallow landslides above fixed settlements and transport corridors. We therefore argue that burnt area is a poor guide to consequence. At the few sites studied, moderate and high-severity fire reduced that protection for one to several decades \citep{maringer_temporal_2016, gehring_shallow_2019, degiuli_arrested_2024}, although this evidence comes from one research group's beech stands and one subalpine massif (Section~\ref{sec:forest}). Across disciplines, gridded danger indices, separable wind--slope spread formulations, coarse burnt-area products and spectral severity maps lose skill or misrepresent intensity and soil conditions in steep Alpine valleys.

What remains to be measured is mostly the size and frequency of these effects. Sub-threshold debris-flow triggering has been analysed in one Alpine catchment \citep{laporta_postwildfire_2025}. Imported evidence from Colorado indicates that debris-flow thresholds recover as vegetation regrows \citep{rengers_evaluating_2024}, yet no Alpine monitoring network tracks that recovery window. We therefore see progress in selective transfer, that is, in importing physically grounded model structures while refitting coefficients locally. It also lies in coupling existing Alpine warning and protection-forest tools to fire, and in joining exposure and vulnerability registers to hazard surfaces so that risk can be estimated directly.

Managing Alpine wildfire is therefore an interdisciplinary problem spanning forestry, agriculture, torrent engineering and cross-border governance. In one costed estimate, prevention amounts to a small fraction of current firefighting and post-fire expenditure \citep{muller_forest_2020}. Yet protective benefits flow largely to valley corridors and downstream users, whereas management costs fall on small, fragmented mountain communities. Much of the Alpine arc is still designing its wildfire institutions and, in our view, could build them from the outset around adaptation, area-based fuel stewardship and formal post-fire handovers between fire, forest and geological authorities.
