# Proposal for a review article in *Reviews of Geophysics*

## 1. Proposed title

**Wildfires in the European Alps: An Interdisciplinary Review**

## 2. Potential authors

Alexandre Dunant (lead and corresponding author), Paulina Bartkowiak, Alice Crespi, Christian Hoffmann, Marco Mina, Massimiliano Pittore, Uta Schirpke, Stefano Terzi and Marc Zebisch, all at Eurac Research, Bolzano, Italy.

The team spans four Eurac Research units. The Center for Climate Change and Transformation covers climate and disaster risk, climatology and water-related multi-risk. The Institute for Earth Observation covers remote sensing of vegetation and the land-surface water balance. The Institute for Alpine Environment covers forest ecology, forest modelling and ecosystem services, and the Institute for Regional Development covers agricultural and forest economics.

## 3. Scientific questions

Wildfire in Europe has been studied mainly where it burns most, in the Mediterranean. The Alps are now drawing fire into steep, densely settled terrain that this body of science was not built for. The review asks four questions.

1. **How is the Alpine fire regime structured, and how is it changing?** How do warming, snow-cover loss and the regrowth of forest on abandoned pasture combine to change when, where and how often fires occur?
2. **Do the standard tools of fire science work in steep mountain terrain?** We ask how far fire-danger indices, spread models and satellite fire products, developed for large fires in gentler landscapes, can be trusted for small fires in complex relief.
3. **Why does the damage from Alpine fires not scale with the area burnt?** Much Alpine forest protects settlements and roads from rockfall, avalanches and landslides. We trace how a small burn can remove that protection for decades and lower the rainfall needed to trigger debris flows.
4. **What is needed to turn Alpine fire-hazard maps into risk assessments that can guide management and policy?** This covers exposure, building vulnerability, fuel management in protection forests, and coordination across eight countries.

## 4. Abstract

European wildfire science relies heavily on tools, satellite products and management doctrines built for large summer fires in Mediterranean landscapes, and has consequently left the European Alps at its margin. That position is no longer tenable. Large fires in European mountains have shifted upslope by roughly 72 m per decade since 2000, fire danger is projected to rise across the Alpine arc, and land abandonment has created continuous fuel on steep former pasture. In this review we synthesise the Alpine evidence across hazard, climate, water, forest, risk and governance, and set imported fire tools and doctrines against Alpine reality. Roughly 80% of Alpine fires burn less than a hectare, below continental satellite thresholds, yet they act on fire-intolerant stands managed to hold rock, snow and sediment above settlements and transport corridors. Consequence is therefore decoupled from burnt area. Loss instead runs simultaneously through degraded protective structure, through ecological shifts of opposite sign across guilds and through a hydrogeomorphic cascade that lowers rainfall thresholds for debris flow, landslide and rockfall from several years to many decades. Because the primary asset at risk is a downslope protective service rather than timber, forest and agricultural policy may matter as much as climate in shaping outcomes. We show why valley-interpolated weather indices, separable spread formulations, linear fuel breaks, windthrow salvage conventions and hazard maps without exposure or vulnerability terms misfire on fragmented Alpine slopes. We then outline an interdisciplinary research agenda scaled to what the sparse Alpine observational record can realistically support.

## 5. Outline

The review is built from a structured search of 3,228 records, of which 136 sources are cited. Its organising rule is evidence grading. Each section opens with the trends that at least three independent studies support. Findings resting on one or two studies are reported with their case count and stated cautiously. Evidence from outside the Alps is used only to show that a mechanism exists, and is marked as such.

1. **Introduction.** The Alps as an emerging fire frontier, and why tools and doctrines imported from other fire regions can misread Alpine risk. *Figure 1* maps the chain of Alpine fire processes against the points where imported tools fail.
2. **Review design and scope.** Contrasting fire regimes within the Alps (*Table 1*), the search and screening procedure (*Figure 2*), and the evidence-grading rule.
3. **Wildfire as a natural hazard.** The Alpine fire season peaks twice, with human-caused spring fires and lightning-driven summer fires. The section covers ignition and spread on steep slopes, danger indices and their need for local calibration (*Table 2*), and the gap between ground records and satellite products.
4. **Climate, weather and projections.** The section covers drought and fuel moisture, snow-cover loss, and foehn winds and valley circulations. It also covers lightning, and the processes that climate projections leave out.
5. **Water, drought and water use.** The section covers drought stress in trees, soil damage after fire, debris flows triggered by ordinary storms, and effects on water supply, snow and glaciers.
6. **Forest change, ecology and management.** The section covers forest expansion on abandoned pasture, the decades-long recovery of protective forest, management for protection rather than timber, fuel breaks, prescribed fire and restoration after fire.
7. **Risk, exposure and vulnerability.** The missing exposure and vulnerability data, seasonal tourism, and the vulnerability of buildings.
8. **Governance, policy and economics.** Limits on firefighting in steep terrain, prevention and early warning, fragmentation across countries, and the economic value of protective forest.
9. **Discussion.** The five findings that recur across independent studies, the mismatch between imported tools and Alpine conditions (*Table 3*), what still rests on single studies, a research agenda with one priority per discipline, and the limitations of the corpus.
10. **Conclusions.**

*Supporting Information:* the search protocol and screening register, and a glossary of cross-disciplinary terms.

## 6. Analysis of recent similar review articles

We found no peer-reviewed synthesis that follows wildfire in steep mountain terrain across the atmosphere, the land surface, hydrology, forest ecology and risk governance. We also found none that tests standard European fire tools against the detailed ground records available for the Alps. Three recent *Reviews of Geophysics* articles each cover part of this ground, and the review connects them.

- **Jones et al. (2022), *Rev. Geophys.* 60, e2020RG000726.** Global and regional fire trends and their drivers. Steep mountain fire regimes are not treated separately.
- **Pepin et al. (2022), *Rev. Geophys.* 60, e2020RG000730.** Elevation-dependent climate change and snow loss in mountains. It does not address wildfire.
- **Mani et al. (2023), *Rev. Geophys.* 61, e2022RG000791.** Chains of geomorphic processes in high mountains, centred on glacier and permafrost hazards. Fire is not a trigger it considers.

Other recent reviews each address a single strand.

- **Or et al. (2023), *Earth-Sci. Rev.* 245, 104569**, and **McGuire et al. (2024), *Nat. Rev. Earth Environ.* 5, 486–503.** Wildfire effects on land surfaces and geomorphic processes in general. They do not cover protection forests or Alpine multi-hazard chains.
- **Melzner et al. (2025), *Landslides* 22, 3989–4011.** An inventory of fire-related geohydrological hazards in the Alps. It does not cover fire weather, remote sensing, forest management, exposure or governance.
- **Moos et al. (2023), *Front. For. Glob. Change* 6, 1223934.** Global-change impacts on protective forests. Fire is treated briefly, alongside wind and bark beetles.
- **Sharples (2009), *Int. J. Wildland Fire* 18, 737–754**, **Werth et al. (2016), USDA PNW-GTR-891**, and **Hayajneh et al. (2026), *Fire* 9, 100.** Mountain fire meteorology and the effects of wind and slope on fire spread. They are not linked to satellite observation or to what happens after the fire.
- **Chuvieco et al. (2019), *Remote Sens. Environ.* 225, 45–64**, and **Wooster et al. (2021), *Remote Sens. Environ.* 267, 112694.** Satellite mapping of burnt area and active fires worldwide. Neither has a dedicated treatment of steep valleys, where terrain shadow, snow and small fire size limit detection.

The most direct predecessor is the 2020 policy white paper of the EU Strategy for the Alpine Region (EUSALP) on forest fires in the Alps (Müller et al., 2020). Because it is not peer reviewed, we take it as our starting point and test its positions against the empirical work published since.

## 7. Benefit to the readership of *Reviews of Geophysics*

The review is relevant across several disciplines.

- **Atmospheric scientists and climate modellers** will see why gridded weather data and fire-danger indices miss foehn winds and the warm layers above valley inversions, and what this means for projections in complex terrain.
- **Hydrologists and geomorphologists** will find fire treated as a trigger of multi-hazard chains, from debris flows set off by ordinary storms to effects on snow, glaciers and water supply.
- **Remote-sensing scientists** will see where satellite fire products lose detection in mountains: most Alpine fires are smaller than the products can register, and burn-severity indices fail to track soil damage.
- **Ecologists and forest scientists** will see how forest regrowth on abandoned land, protective function and fire interact over decades.
- **Hazard and risk researchers** will find the missing steps from hazard maps to risk assessment, together with their governance implications.

Because each section separates what several independent studies agree on from what rests on a single case, the review also gives readers an explicit map of what is established in Alpine fire science and what remains open. That distinction is useful well beyond the Alps, since many mountain ranges share the same conditions: small fires, steep terrain and settlements protected by forest.

**Accessibility.** The review is written for geoscientists outside fire science. Technical terms from meteorology, fire behaviour, forestry and risk assessment are defined at first use and collected in a glossary. Figure 1 and Table 3 show at a glance how physical and institutional links connect across disciplines. A Plain Language Summary and three Key Points are provided.
