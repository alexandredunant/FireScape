## 1. Proposed title

**Wildfires in the European Alps: An Interdisciplinary Review**

## 2. Potential authors

Alexandre Dunant (lead and corresponding author), Paulina Bartkowiak, Alice Crespi, Christian Hoffmann, Marco Mina, Massimiliano Pittore, Uta Schirpke, Stefano Terzi and Marc Zebisch, all at Eurac Research, Bolzano, Italy.

The team spans four Eurac Research units. The Center for Climate Change and Transformation covers climate and disaster risk, climatology and water-related multi-risk. The Institute for Earth Observation covers remote sensing of vegetation and the land-surface water balance. The Institute for Alpine Environment covers forest ecology, forest modelling and ecosystem services, and the Institute for Regional Development covers agricultural and forest economics.

## 3. Scientific questions

Most of Europe's burnt area lies in the Mediterranean, and much of European fire statistics, danger rating and prevention practice has developed there. Large fires in European mountains are now moving upslope, and fire hazard is projected to rise across the Alps, where terrain is steep and valleys are densely settled. Fire science has not yet fully adapted to these conditions. The review asks four questions.

1. **How is the Alpine fire regime structured, and how is it changing?** How do warming, snow-cover loss and the regrowth of forest on abandoned pasture combine to change when, where and how often fires occur?
2. **Do the standard tools of fire science work in steep mountain terrain?** We ask how far fire-danger indices, spread models and satellite fire products, developed largely for larger fires outside mountain terrain, can be translated to complex relief.
3. **Why might area burnt be a poor guide to the damage an Alpine fire causes?** Much Alpine forest protects settlements and roads from rockfall, avalanches and landslides, so where a fire burns may matter more than how large it is. We examine the evidence, so far limited to a few Alpine sites, that fire can reduce this protection for decades, and the finding, documented at two Alpine sites and well established elsewhere, that burnt slopes can produce debris flows after only moderate rainfall.
4. **What is needed to turn Alpine fire-hazard maps into risk assessments that can guide management and policy?** This covers exposure, building vulnerability, fuel management in protection forests, and coordination across eight countries.

## 4. Abstract

European wildfire science relies heavily on tools, satellite products and management doctrines developed for large fires outside the Alps, many of them in Mediterranean landscapes, leaving the Alps at its margin. Large fires in European mountains have moved upslope, most markedly after 2015, fire hazard is projected to rise across the Alpine arc, and land abandonment has created continuous fuel on steep former pasture. We synthesise the Alpine evidence across hazard, climate, water, forest, risk and governance, testing imported tools and doctrines against Alpine conditions. Roughly 80% of Alpine fires burn less than a hectare, below continental satellite thresholds. Many Alpine stands are fire-sensitive and managed to hold rock, snow and sediment above settlements and transport corridors, so we argue that where a fire burns may matter more for its consequences than how large it is. At the few Alpine sites studied, moderate and high-severity fire reduced protection against rockfall and shallow landslides for decades, and at two sites burnt slopes produced debris flows after brief, moderate rainfall. Because land use governs fuel continuity, and one continental model finds management capacity strongly modulates projected burnt area, forest and agricultural policy may matter as much as climate in shaping outcomes. We examine evidence that valley-interpolated weather indices, separable spread formulations, linear fuel breaks, windthrow salvage conventions and hazard maps lacking exposure or vulnerability can misfire on steep slopes. We separate trends supported by several independent studies from single-case findings, and outline a research agenda scaled to the sparse Alpine record.

## 5. Outline

The review is built from a structured search of 3,228 records, of which 136 sources are cited. Its organising rule is evidence grading. Each section opens with the trends that at least three independent studies support. Findings resting on one or two studies are reported with their case count and stated cautiously. Evidence from outside the Alps is used only to show that a mechanism exists, and is marked as such.

1. **Introduction.** The Alps as an emerging fire frontier, and why tools and doctrines imported from other fire regions can misread Alpine risk. *Figure 1* maps the chain of Alpine fire processes against the points where imported tools fail.
2. **Review design and scope.** Contrasting fire regimes within the Alps (*Table 1*), the search and screening procedure (*Figure 2*), and the evidence-grading rule.
3. **Wildfire as a natural hazard.** The Alpine fire season peaks twice: human-caused fires in late winter and spring, and a summer season in which lightning becomes an important cause. The section covers ignition and spread on steep slopes, danger indices and their need for local calibration (*Table 2*), and the gap between ground records and satellite products.
4. **Climate, weather and projections.** The section covers drought and fuel moisture, snow-cover loss, and foehn winds and valley circulations. It also covers lightning, and the processes that climate projections leave out.
5. **Water, drought and water use.** The section covers drought stress in trees, soil damage after fire, debris flows after moderate rainfall on burnt slopes, and effects on water supply, snow and glaciers.
6. **Forest change, ecology and management.** The section covers forest expansion on abandoned pasture, the slow recovery of protective forest after fire (decades at the few sites studied), management for protection rather than timber, fuel breaks, prescribed fire and restoration after fire.
7. **Risk, exposure and vulnerability.** The missing exposure and vulnerability data, seasonal tourism, and the vulnerability of buildings.
8. **Governance, policy and economics.** Limits on firefighting in steep terrain, prevention and early warning, fragmentation across countries, and the economic value of protective forest.
9. **Discussion.** The findings that recur across independent studies (small fires missed by continental products, fuel continuity from land abandonment, the climatic envelope of fire moving upslope, imported tools losing skill), the multi-decadal loss of protective forest documented at fewer sites, the mismatch between imported tools and Alpine conditions (*Table 3*), what still rests on single studies, a research agenda with one priority per discipline, and the limitations of the corpus.
10. **Conclusions.**

*Supporting Information:* the search protocol and screening register, and a glossary of cross-disciplinary terms.

## 6. Analysis of recent similar review articles

We found no peer-reviewed synthesis that follows wildfire in steep mountain terrain across the atmosphere, the land surface, hydrology, forest ecology and risk governance. We also found none that tests standard European fire tools against the detailed ground records available for the Alps. Three recent *Reviews of Geophysics* articles each cover part of this ground, and the review connects them.

- **Jones et al. (2022), *Rev. Geophys.* 60, e2020RG000726.** Global and regional fire trends and their drivers. Steep mountain fire regimes are not treated separately.
- **Pepin et al. (2022), *Rev. Geophys.* 60, e2020RG000730.** Elevation-dependent climate change and snow loss in mountains. Fire is mentioned only in passing.
- **Mani et al. (2023), *Rev. Geophys.* 61, e2022RG000791.** A classification of natural-hazard process chains in high mountains, centred on chains associated with glacier retreat and permafrost degradation.

Other recent reviews each address a single strand.

- **Or et al. (2023), *Earth-Sci. Rev.* 245, 104569**, and **McGuire et al. (2024), *Nat. Rev. Earth Environ.* 5, 486–503.** General reviews of wildfire effects on land surfaces and on geomorphic processes. Neither is centred on mountain protection forests or on the Alps.
- **Melzner et al. (2025), *Landslides* 22, 3989–4011.** Wildfire-induced geohydrological risk in the Alps, built on four case studies, a catalogue of burn-severity indicators (including satellite indices) and protection-forest considerations. It is centred on post-fire geohydrological hazards rather than on fire regimes, fire danger, spread or exposure across the Alpine arc.
- **Moos et al. (2023), *Front. For. Glob. Change* 6, 1223934.** A systematic review of 72 publications on how global change affects protective forests, covering natural disturbances as a group rather than focusing on fire.
- **Sharples (2009), *Int. J. Wildland Fire* 18, 737–754**, **Werth et al. (2016), USDA PNW-GTR-891**, and **Hayajneh et al. (2026), *Fire* 9, 100.** Mountain fire meteorology, extreme fire behaviour, and the effects of wind and slope on fire spread. Their focus is fire behaviour, not satellite observation or post-fire consequences.
- **Chuvieco et al. (2019), *Remote Sens. Environ.* 225, 45–64**, and **Wooster et al. (2021), *Remote Sens. Environ.* 267, 112694.** Global reviews of satellite burnt-area and active-fire mapping. Detection problems specific to steep mountain terrain are not their focus.

The most direct predecessor is the 2020 policy white paper of the EU Strategy for the Alpine Region (EUSALP) on forest fires in the Alps (Müller et al., 2020). Because it is not peer reviewed, we take it as our starting point and test its positions against the empirical work published since.

## 7. Benefit to the readership of *Reviews of Geophysics*

The review is relevant across several disciplines.

- **Atmospheric scientists and climate modellers** will see why gridded weather data and fire-danger indices can miss foehn winds and the warm layers above valley inversions, and what this means for projections in complex terrain.
- **Hydrologists and geomorphologists** will find fire treated as a trigger of multi-hazard chains, from debris flows after moderate rainfall on burnt slopes to effects on snow, glaciers and water supply.
- **Remote-sensing scientists** will see where satellite fire products lose detection in mountains: most Alpine fires are smaller than the products can register, and at the one Alpine fire where this was tested, burn-severity indices did not track soil damage.
- **Ecologists and forest scientists** will see how forest regrowth on abandoned land, protective function and fire interact over decades.
- **Hazard and risk researchers** will find the missing steps from hazard maps to risk assessment, together with their governance implications.

Because each section separates what several independent studies agree on from what rests on a single case, the review also gives readers an explicit map of what is established in Alpine fire science and what remains open. The same distinction may be useful in other mountain ranges where small fires, steep terrain and forest-protected settlements coincide.

**Accessibility.** The review is written for geoscientists outside fire science. Technical terms from meteorology, fire behaviour, forestry and risk assessment are defined at first use and collected in a glossary. Figure 1 and Table 3 show at a glance how physical and institutional links connect across disciplines. A Plain Language Summary and three Key Points are provided.
