## Working title

**Why mountain wildfire is different: terrain, fire regimes and consequence, from global pyrogeography to the European Alps**

## Potential authors

Alexandre Dunant (lead), Alice Crespi, Michael de Rachewiltz, Christian Hoffmann, Marco Mina, Massimiliano Pittore, Uta Schirpke, Stefano Terzi, Jenny Ufer, Marc Zebisch (Eurac Research, Bolzano, Italy).

*Note for the author team, not for the editors:* we recommend inviting two to four co-authors with primary expertise in the comparative systems: Himalayan / Hindu Kush Himalaya fire, Western North American Cordilleran post-fire hazards, and south-east Australian alpine fire. The editors will read the author list as evidence that the review can deliver its global scope.

## Scientific questions

1. **Mountain fire in global regime space.** Global classifications describe fire regimes by size, frequency, intensity, season and extent (pyromes), or by anthropogenic control (wild, tamed and domesticated macroregimes). How does complex terrain modify each of these dimensions? Do mountain regimes form a distinct region of that space, or are they unresolved by the satellite products on which the classifications rest?
2. **Consequence.** Why is consequence decoupled from burnt area in mountains, and what process chains replace area as the measure of loss? These include the loss of protective forest against rockfall, avalanches and debris flow; the post-fire geomorphic cascade, in which fire lowers rainfall thresholds for erosion and debris flow; and hydrological and snow responses.
3. **Terrain meteorology.** How do slope–wind coupling, valley circulation, foehn, the thermal belt and convective lightning control ignition and spread? How well are they represented in fire-danger and projection products derived from synoptic and reanalysis fields?
4. **Regime transition and the hazard-to-risk gap.** Fire regimes worldwide have changed far more often in the last two to three centuries than earlier in the Holocene. Where are mountain regimes heading, and what prevents current assessment from converting mountain fire hazard into risk?

## Abstract (draft, ~260 words)

Wildfire is becoming more frequent in mountain environments where it has historically been rare, yet fire science has been built mainly on lowland, Mediterranean and boreal regimes, and global classifications of fire regimes barely resolve mountains. We argue that mountain wildfire is not a scaled-down lowland regime but a structurally different one, and we review the evidence process by process, from ignition to consequence.

The European Alps are the anchor case. They hold the densest record of small fires, protective-forest management and post-fire hazard observations of any mountain system. Each finding is tested against mountain systems chosen to span global fire-regime types:
- **Himalaya and Hengduan:** human-set, dry-season fires.
- **North American Cordillera and south-east Australian Alps:** rare, intense, large crown fires.

Our rule is that mechanisms transfer between ranges but coefficients do not.

Three features define the mountain case:
- **Fire-size distribution.** It is heavy-tailed, with around 80% of Alpine fires under one hectare.
- **Upslope migration.** Across eight European mountain regions, the median elevation of fires larger than 30 ha rose by about 72 m per decade between 2000 and 2025. The shift is led by the Balkans, Iberia and Turkey and is still weak in the Alps.
- **Consequence.** It is decoupled from burnt area, because fire acts on forests that hold rock, snow and sediment above settlements. Damage therefore runs through a geomorphic cascade in which fire lowers the rainfall threshold for erosion, debris flow and rockfall.

Land abandonment is moving such regimes away from tight human control towards larger and more variable fires. We show why assessment that produces hazard without exposure and vulnerability cannot yet produce risk in mountains. We close by discussing what the evidence implies for fire management and governance in steep terrain, and how the community could detect and prepare for a regime shift.

## Framing: mountains in global fire-regime space

The review is organised around two global classifications.

**Pyromes (Archibald et al., 2013, *PNAS* 110:6442–6447).** These define five fire-regime syndromes from satellite-derived fire size, return interval, intensity, season length and burnt area. Two of them, rare–cool–small and intermediate–cool–small, dominate the biomes that make up the Alps:
- temperate mixed forests: 41% and 45% respectively
- montane grasslands: 31% and 36% respectively

The authors note that burnt area is barely detectable in these two pyromes.

**Fire macroregimes (Pereira et al., 2022, *Ecol. Appl.* 32:e2588).** These separate wild, tamed and domesticated regimes by the degree of anthropogenic control. Europe, India and China fall in the domesticated macroregime; the western United States and the boreal zone in the wild one. The paper reads land abandonment in southern Europe as a regime "turning feral": more year-to-year variability in burnt area and more very large fires, aggravated by suppression-centred policy.

**Sayedi et al. (2024, *Fire Ecology* 20:18)** add the time axis. In an assessment by 99 fire researchers, fire-regime change over the last ~250 years is judged about ten times more frequent than in the rest of the Holocene, and forest regimes are expected to intensify under all warming scenarios. Its biome scheme has no mountain category. **Jain et al. (2020, *Environ. Rev.* 28:478–505)** structure the methods dimension: their six problem domains of machine learning in wildfire science, and the limits on transferring data-driven models between regimes.

The review uses these frames in three ways:
- **Section structure:** each regime dimension (size, frequency, intensity, season, extent) is examined for how terrain modifies it.
- **Choice of comparative ranges:** one or two mountain systems for each regime type relevant to the Alpine trajectory.
- **The argument that ties them together:** the Alps are a domesticated, small-fire regime moving under abandonment and warming towards the conditions of wilder regimes, so mountain systems already in those regimes show what that transition implies.

## Comparative mountain systems

The placement of each range is our expectation from the published global maps and biome tables. Neither classification resolves individual ranges, and verifying the placements is part of the review.

| Regime type | Mountain systems | Role in the argument |
|---|---|---|
| Rare/intermediate–cool–small; domesticated | **European Alps (anchor)**; Pyrenees, Carpathians, Apennines | The reference case: small, mostly human-caused fires, a bimodal season, protective forests. |
| Intermediate–cool–small; human-set dry-season fire | **Himalaya / Hindu Kush Himalaya** (Nepal, Indian Himalaya); **Hengduan** margin of the Tibetan Plateau | A frequent, human-ignited counterpart of the Alpine spring fire season; deposition of fire-emitted black carbon on snow and ice; post-fire debris flows in the Hengduan; mountains as water towers. |
| Rare–intense–large; wild | **North American Cordillera** (Rockies, Sierra Nevada, Cascades, Canadian Rockies); **south-east Australian Alps** | Where the Alpine transition could lead: crown-fire regimes on steep terrain, the most developed post-fire debris-flow and fire–snow science, alpine ecology after large fires, and cross-jurisdiction management. |

Japan, Taiwan and New Zealand are included only where the search finds post-fire hazard evidence comparable to the systems above.

**Tropical montane systems are out of scope.** In the East African mountains and the Andean páramo and puna, frequent grass-fuelled fire maintains open vegetation and depresses the treeline. That is a different regime and process chain, and treating it properly would need a review of its own. We state this exclusion in the scope section.

## Outline

1. **Introduction.** Mountains as an emerging fire frontier. Mountains in global fire-regime classifications. Relation to previous reviews.
2. **Review design and scope.** The anchor case and the comparative systems chosen by regime type. Seed publications with backward and forward citation chaining, and a documented screening register. The transfer rule (mechanisms transfer, coefficients do not).
3. **Mountain fire regimes in global regime space.** Fire size, frequency, intensity, season and extent, each as modified by terrain. The detection limits of the satellite products behind global classifications. Regime transition under abandonment and warming.
4. **Ignition, spread and hazard modelling in complex terrain.** Ignition regimes. Slope–wind coupling. Danger indices. Data-driven hazard models and their transferability.
5. **Climate and mountain meteorology.** Valley and slope winds, foehn and the thermal belt. Lightning and convection. Elevation-dependent warming and what fire projections omit.
6. **Water and the post-fire geomorphic cascade.** The drought and fuel-moisture chain. Soil alteration. Rainfall thresholds for debris flow and rockfall. Snow, ice and black carbon.
7. **Forest change and protective function.** Fuel continuity after land abandonment. The protective-forest recovery window. Silviculture, fuel treatment and prescribed fire in steep terrain.
8. **From hazard to risk.** Exposure and vulnerability in mountain settings.
9. **Synthesis across regimes.** A matrix of mountain systems against processes, showing what is general to mountains, what is specific to a regime type, and where evidence is missing.
10. **Discussion: preparing for a shift in mountain fire.** What the evidence implies for fire management and governance in steep terrain (access-limited suppression, prevention, cross-border coordination). What observations and monitoring would detect a regime shift early. A research agenda scaled to the observational record.
11. **Conclusions.**

Supporting information: the search protocol and screening register, structured evidence notes for every cited source, and a glossary. The manuscript will include Key Points and a Plain Language Summary, with roughly 8–10 figures and tables: the mountain systems placed in pyrome and macroregime space, a process-chain schematic, the cross-system matrix, and a map of the systems covered.

## Comparison with recent similar reviews

No published review makes the cross-regime, process-based case that mountain wildfire is structurally different.

**The global frames we build on:**
- **Archibald et al. (2013)** and **Pereira et al. (2022)** classify global fire regimes from satellite data. Neither resolves mountains, and both note that small-fire regimes are poorly captured by burnt-area products.
- **Sayedi et al. (2024)** assess fire-regime change by biome and have no mountain category.
- **Jain et al. (2020)** review machine learning in wildfire science, where mountain applications are scattered case studies.

**The closest reviews each cover one strand:**
- **Jones et al. (2022, *Rev. Geophys.* 60, e2020RG000726):** global and regional fire trends and drivers, with no separate treatment of mountains.
- **Pepin et al. (2022, *Rev. Geophys.* 60, e2020RG000730):** elevation-dependent climate change in mountains, without fire.
- **Mani et al. (2023, *Rev. Geophys.* 61, e2022RG000791):** geomorphic process chains in high mountains, centred on glacier and permafrost hazards.
- **McGuire et al. (2024, *Nat. Rev. Earth Environ.* 5, 486–503):** fire effects on geomorphic processes globally, not mountain-specific.
- **Melzner et al. (2025, *Landslides* 22, 3989–4011):** wildfire-induced geohydrological risk in the Alps, with a first Alpine inventory.
- **Moos et al. (2023, *Front. For. Glob. Change* 6, 1223934):** global-change impacts on protective forests, with fire one disturbance among several.
- **Vukomanovic & Steelman (2019, *Landsc. Ecol.* 34, 1179–1194):** mountain wildfire and ecosystem services, mainly in the Colorado Front Range.
- **Sharples (2009, *Int. J. Wildland Fire* 18, 737–754)** and **Clements (2016, USDA Forest Service PNW-GTR-891, ch. 2):** mountain meteorological effects on fire behaviour (foehn and downslope winds, slope and valley flows, inversions, wind modelling), without links to detection or post-fire consequence.
- **Chuvieco et al. (2019, *Remote Sens. Environ.* 225, 45–64)** and **Wooster et al. (2021, *Remote Sens. Environ.* 267, 112694):** satellite burned-area and active-fire mapping globally; terrain effects on detection are documented only in case studies.

Existing reviews treat mountain fire meteorology (Sharples, 2009; Clements, 2016) and satellite fire mapping (Chuvieco et al., 2019; Wooster et al., 2021) separately, and none that is known to us examines how steep terrain jointly shapes fire weather, detectability and consequence. The review also gives a peer-reviewed, indexed update of the 2020 EUSALP consensus statement on forest fires in the Alps (Müller et al., 2020), which is grey literature.

## Benefit to the readership of *Reviews of Geophysics*

The review places mountain wildfire within the satellite-based description of global fire regimes and treats it as a coupled geophysical process chain: atmospheric dynamics in complex terrain, land-surface and snow hydrology, and post-fire geomorphology. It connects three recent *Reviews of Geophysics* syntheses (fire trends, elevation-dependent climate change, and mountain process chains) through a hazard none of them centres on. It shows where global fire-regime products under-represent mountains and what observations would close the gap. That serves researchers working on fire-weather prediction, remote sensing, Earth-system fire modelling and hazard cascades.

## Accessibility

The review is written for geoscientists outside fire science. Each section opens from a process familiar to an adjacent discipline:
- boundary-layer and valley meteorology
- snow and catchment hydrology
- hillslope geomorphology
- remote sensing of the land surface

Each then shows what fire changes in it. Fire-specific terms (fire regime, pyrome, burn severity, fire danger indices) are defined at first use and collected in a glossary. A single process-chain figure links ignition, spread, the geomorphic and hydrological response, and loss.

## Why now

Mountain fire regimes may be at the start of a shift that recent fire seasons have only begun to reveal. A synthesis now could help the geoscience and hazard communities prepare for coming events rather than learn from them afterwards. It could also serve as a common reference for a community of practice across mountain regions, linking researchers and practitioners who currently work on the same processes in isolation.
