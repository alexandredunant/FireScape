Dear Editor-in-Chief, dear members of the Editorial Board,

We would like to propose an interdisciplinary review article for *Reviews of Geophysics* titled *Wildfires in the European Alps: An Interdisciplinary Review*. The attached proposal gives the title, the potential authors, the scientific questions, the abstract and outline, an analysis of recent similar reviews, and the benefit to the journal's readership.

Fire danger has risen in the Alps (Wastl et al., 2012) and is projected to rise further (Gerberding & Schirpke, 2025), large fires in European mountains have become more frequent and moved upslope since 2015 (Beloiu et al., 2026), and decades of pastoral abandonment have created continuous forest fuel on steep slopes. Together these make a shift towards more frequent large fires plausible. In neighbouring south-eastern France, the number of fires larger than 100 ha is projected to be the fastest-growing fire metric (Pimont et al., 2023), but no comparable projection yet exists for the Alps. Yet operational fire danger rating, satellite monitoring and prevention practice in Europe were developed mostly for larger fires outside the Alps, many of them in Mediterranean landscapes. Taking the European Alps as an intensively monitored sentinel system—where national inventories record fires well below one hectare that continental satellite products cannot detect (Müller et al., 2020) alongside formal protection-forest and torrent networks—we test imported fire-science tools and doctrines against Alpine reality across six domains: hazard, climate and weather, water, forest, risk, and governance. The synthesis is evidence-graded: each section opens with the trends that several independent studies support, while findings that rest on a single Alpine case are reported as such, so the review shows what is established and what remains open.

We argue that Alpine wildfire is not a scaled-down Mediterranean problem. Much Alpine forest is managed to hold rock, snow and sediment above valley settlements and transport corridors, so we argue that where a fire burns may matter as much for its consequences as how large it is; no Alpine study has yet related damage to burnt area directly. At the few Alpine sites studied, moderate and high-severity fire reduced protection against rockfall and shallow landslides for decades, and at two sites burnt slopes produced debris flows after brief, moderate rainfall. The review also examines where standard tools lose reliability: valley-interpolated weather indices can miss foehn winds and mid-slope thermal belts, coarse burnt-area products omit roughly four fifths of recorded fires, and, at the single Alpine sites where this has been tested, spread simulators diverge on fireline intensity and spectral severity indices do not track soil water repellency.

We believe the review suits the readership of *Reviews of Geophysics* because three recent articles in the journal approach mountain wildfire from different sides without addressing it directly:
- Jones et al. (2022) synthesise global fire trends and drivers and call for more work on how topography affects fire spread;
- Pepin et al. (2022) synthesise elevation-dependent warming in mountains and note that it may push forest fire lines upslope;
- Mani et al. (2023) classify high-mountain hazard process chains, none of which is triggered by fire.

Our review takes mountain wildfire as its subject, and links these strands to recent reviews of wildfire effects on land surfaces and geomorphic processes (Or et al., 2023; McGuire et al., 2024).

Existing reviews treat mountain fire meteorology (Sharples, 2009; Werth et al., 2016) and satellite fire detection (Chuvieco et al., 2019; Wooster et al., 2021) separately; we found none that examines how complex relief jointly governs atmospheric forcing, remote-sensing observability, protective-forest loss and post-fire hydrogeomorphic cascades. The text is written to be accessible to geoscientists across disciplines, defining fire- and forestry-specific terms at first use.

Because the author team at Eurac Research (Bolzano, Italy)—spanning climate and disaster risk assessment, mountain climatology, Earth observation of vegetation and land-surface water balance, water-related multi-risk, forest ecology and modelling, ecosystem services, and agricultural and forest economics—has already completed the 3,228-record screening and a complete draft (about 13,700 words, 138 cited sources), a polished manuscript can be submitted well within three months of an invitation.

Thank you for considering this proposal. We would be glad to adjust its emphasis or framing in light of the Board's advice.
