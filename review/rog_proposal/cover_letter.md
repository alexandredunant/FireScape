Dear Editor-in-Chief, dear members of the Editorial Board,

We would like to propose an interdisciplinary review article for *Reviews of Geophysics* titled *Wildfires in the European Alps: An Interdisciplinary Review*. The full proposal—containing the working title, author team, scientific questions, abstract, outline and comparison with recent reviews—is attached.

Wildfire danger is rising across European mountains and large fires have shifted upslope since 2000 (Beloiu et al., 2026; Gerberding & Schirpke, 2025), while a century of pastoral abandonment has created continuous forest fuel on steep slopes. Yet operational fire danger rating, satellite monitoring and prevention doctrines in Europe remain calibrated on large summer fires in Mediterranean landscapes. Taking the European Alps as an intensively monitored sentinel system—where eight national inventories record even sub-hectare fires invisible to continental satellites (Müller et al., 2020) alongside formal protection-forest and torrent networks—we test imported fire-science tools and doctrines against Alpine reality across six domains: hazard, climate and weather, water, forest, risk, and governance. The synthesis is evidence-graded: each section opens with the trends that several independent studies support, while findings that rest on a single Alpine case are reported as such, so the review shows what is established and what remains open.

We show that Alpine wildfire is not a scaled-down Mediterranean problem but a coupled Earth-system and socio-ecological cascade in which consequence decouples from burnt area. Sub-hectare and moderate fires act on thin-barked forests managed to arrest rockfall, avalanches and shallow landslides above dense valley settlements and trans-Alpine transport corridors, while lowering catchment rainfall thresholds so that ordinary storms can trigger debris flows over multi-year to multi-decadal recovery windows. Along that chain, valley-interpolated weather indices miss foehn and mid-slope thermal belts, empirical spread simulators diverge on fireline intensity, coarse burned-area products omit roughly four fifths of recorded events, and paired-date spectral severity indices decouple from soil water repellency.

We believe the review suits the readership of *Reviews of Geophysics* because it bridges four recent *Reviews of Geophysics* syntheses that do not yet intersect on steep-terrain fire risk:
- global wildfire trends and climate drivers (Jones et al., 2022)
- elevation-dependent warming and snowline retreat in mountains (Pepin et al., 2022)
- wildfire effects on land surfaces and soil hydrology (Or et al., 2023)
- geomorphic process chains in high-mountain environments (Mani et al., 2023)

Existing reviews treat mountain fire meteorology (Sharples, 2009; Werth et al., 2016) and satellite fire detection (Chuvieco et al., 2019; Wooster et al., 2021) separately; none examines how complex relief jointly governs atmospheric forcing, remote-sensing observability, protective-forest loss and post-fire hydrogeomorphic cascades. The text is written to be accessible to geoscientists across disciplines, defining fire- and forestry-specific terms at first use.

Because the author team at Eurac Research (Bolzano, Italy)—spanning climate and disaster risk assessment, mountain climatology, Earth observation of vegetation and land-surface water balance, water-related multi-risk, forest ecology and modelling, ecosystem services, and agricultural and forest economics—has already completed the 3,228-record screening and a complete draft (about 13,500 words, 136 cited sources), a polished manuscript can be submitted well within three months of an invitation.

Thank you for considering this proposal. We would be glad to adjust its emphasis or framing in light of the Board's advice.
