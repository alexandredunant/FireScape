Dear Editor-in-Chief, dear members of the Editorial Board,

We would like to propose a review article for *Reviews of Geophysics* on wildfire in mountain terrain. The proposal is attached. It contains the working title, potential authors, scientific questions, abstract, outline and an analysis of recent similar reviews.

Fire is moving into mountain environments in which it played little part during the last century. Across eight European mountain regions, the elevation of large fires has risen by about 72 m per decade since 2000. Land abandonment is also building continuous fuel on steep slopes that were pasture a few generations ago. Yet mountains barely register in the satellite-based classifications of global fire regimes. Their fires are mostly small, and the products on which those classifications rest miss many small fires. Global syntheses of fire-regime change also have no mountain category.

The review argues that mountain wildfire is not a scaled-down lowland regime but a structurally different one, and it examines the evidence process by process:
- how complex terrain modifies fire size, frequency, intensity, season and extent
- valley winds, foehn and convective lightning
- the post-fire geomorphic cascade, in which fire lowers the rainfall threshold for debris flow and rockfall
- the loss of protective forest above settlements and infrastructure

The European Alps serve as the anchor case, because they hold the densest record of small fires, protective-forest management and post-fire hazard observations. Each finding is tested against mountain systems chosen to span global fire-regime types: the Himalaya and the Hengduan margin of the Tibetan Plateau, the North American Cordillera, and the south-east Australian Alps.

We believe the topic suits the journal's readership because mountain wildfire is a coupled geophysical problem, linking atmospheric dynamics in complex terrain, snow and catchment hydrology, hillslope geomorphology and land-surface remote sensing. The review connects three recent *Reviews of Geophysics* syntheses, on global fire trends, elevation-dependent climate change and mountain process chains, through a hazard that none of them centres on. We have found no review of mountain fire meteorology, nor of satellite detection limits in steep terrain. We will write for geoscientists outside fire science, defining fire-specific terms at first use. As supporting dynamic content, we propose an interactive map placing each mountain system in global fire-regime space and a browsable evidence matrix.

The proposal builds on an existing manuscript of about 30,000 words and 150 sources. Every citation has been checked against its source document. If the proposal is accepted, we would extend the comparative systems, restructure the manuscript for the journal and submit within three months of the invitation.

The author team is at Eurac Research, Bolzano, Italy, and covers climate, forest ecology, natural hazards and risk, ecosystem services and governance. We intend to invite co-authors with expertise in the comparative mountain systems.

Thank you for considering this proposal. We would be glad to adjust its scope or emphasis in light of the Board's advice.

With kind regards,
