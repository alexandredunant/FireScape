Dear Editor-in-Chief, dear members of the Editorial Board,

We would like to propose a review article for *Reviews of Geophysics* on wildfire in mountain terrain. The proposal is attached. It contains the working title, potential authors, scientific questions, abstract, outline and an analysis of recent similar reviews.

Wildfire is becoming more frequent in mountain environments where it has historically been rare, yet fire science has been built mainly on lowland, Mediterranean and boreal regimes. Mountains have been treated as smaller versions of those problems. We argue that mountain wildfire is structurally different. Terrain changes how fires ignite, spread and are observed. Above all, it changes their consequences, because fire on steep slopes can remove the forests that hold back rockfall, avalanches and debris flows, and can set off a cascade of hazards long after the flames are out.

The review takes the European Alps as its anchor case, because they hold the densest record of small fires, protective-forest management and post-fire hazards of any mountain system. To separate what is general to mountains from what is specific to the Alps, each finding is tested against mountain systems chosen for two reasons: their fire regimes differ from the Alpine one, and the processes the review examines have been measured there.
- **The Himalaya and the Hengduan margin of the Tibetan Plateau** share the Alps' human-set, dry-season fires, but at far higher frequency, and add the link between fire, snow and ice in a major water-supply region.
- **The North American Cordillera** shows the large, intense fires towards which the Alpine regime may be moving, and has the most advanced research on post-fire debris flows.
- **The south-east Australian Alps** show what large fires do to alpine and subalpine ecosystems managed across several jurisdictions.

We believe the topic suits the journal's readership because mountain wildfire is a coupled geophysical problem. It links atmospheric dynamics in complex terrain, snow and catchment hydrology, hillslope geomorphology and remote sensing of the land surface. It also connects three recent *Reviews of Geophysics* syntheses, none of which centres on fire in mountains:
- the global review of fire trends and drivers under climate change (Jones et al., 2022)
- the review of elevation-dependent climate change in mountains (Pepin et al., 2022)
- the review of geomorphic process chains in high-mountain regions (Mani et al., 2023)

Existing reviews treat mountain fire meteorology (Sharples, 2009; Clements, 2016) and satellite fire mapping (Chuvieco et al., 2019; Wooster et al., 2021) separately, and none that is known to us examines how steep terrain jointly shapes fire weather, detectability and consequence. We will write for geoscientists outside fire science, defining fire-specific terms at first use.

The review is also timely. Mountain fire regimes may be at the start of a shift that recent seasons have only begun to reveal. A synthesis now could help the geoscience and hazard communities prepare for the events ahead rather than learn from them afterwards, and it could serve as a common reference for a community of practice across mountain regions.

The proposal builds on an extensive synthesis of the Alpine evidence that the author team has already compiled, so the manuscript can be completed within three months of an invitation. The authors are at Eurac Research, Bolzano, Italy, and work on natural hazards and risk, climate, forest ecology and management, ecosystem services and environmental governance, the last of which frames the review's discussion of fire management in steep terrain. 

Thank you for considering this proposal. We would be glad to adjust its scope or emphasis in light of the Board's advice.

With kind regards,

Alexandre Dunant, on behalf of the author team
