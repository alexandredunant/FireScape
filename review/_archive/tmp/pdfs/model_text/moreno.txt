5                                                Abstract
6    Wildfires are complex hazards occurring worldwide, leading to substantial economic
7    losses, fatalities, and carbon emissions. The interplay of climate change, land use alter-
8    ations, and socioeconomic pressures is expected to further increase the frequency and
9    intensity of wildfires. In this context, developing reliable, dynamic prediction tools is es-
10   sential for risk mitigation. This work presents a spatiotemporal wildfire prediction model
11   for the Trentino-South Tyrol region (13,600 km2 ) in the northeastern Italian Alps. Leverag-
12   ing generalized additive models, we integrate multitemporal wildfire records (2000–2023)
13   with static and dynamic environmental controls (e.g., topography, land cover, daily pre-
14   cipitation, and temperature). The resulting model predictions change dynamically over
15   space and time in response to static features, seasonal trends, and evolving meteorolog-
16   ical conditions. Model outputs were evaluated using established performance metrics,
17   enabling the derivation of dynamic spatial wildfire probability thresholds. These thresh-
18   olds are illustrated for varying amounts of precipitation, temperature, and different com-
19   binations of static factors. Validation through multiple perspectives yielded performance
20   scores generally exceeding 0.8, confirming the model strong generalization and transfer-
21   ability. To demonstrate the practical application, the model was used to hindcast past
22   wildfire initiation between 1–15 July 2022–a period marked by elevated wildfire activity.
23   By integrating static and dynamic environmental controls, this research advances the spa-
24   tiotemporal prediction of wildfires in complex alpine regions, supporting the develop-
25   ment of early warning systems.

28   1    Introduction
29   Wildfires are frequent hazards posing significant threats to society and the environment.
30   On average, an estimated 350 Mha burns globally each year (Giglio et al., 2013). The
31   economic toll of wildfires is severe, with global wildfire-related losses amounting to ap-
32   proximately USD$142 billion since 2000, as reported by the international disaster database
33   (EM-DAT; Jones et al., 2022). Some of the most devastating wildfires in recent history in-
34   clude the events in Australia, the United States, and Chile (Filkov et al., 2020; Maranghides
35   et al., 2023; Guerrero et al., 2024). Beyond their immediate economic and societal con-
36   sequences, wildfires are a major source of carbon emissions, releasing an estimated 2–3
37   billion tons of CO2 annually, at times surpassing 50% of emissions from fossil fuel com-
38   bustion (Jolly et al., 2015; van der Werf et al., 2017). Their impacts manifest across multiple
39   scales as locally, wildfires disrupt ecosystems, threaten human settlements, and damage
40   infrastructure (Driscoll et al., 2024); regionally, they accelerate soil erosion, reducing agri-
41   cultural productivity (Keeley et al., 2017); and globally, their carbon emissions contribute
42   to climate change, potentially amplifying future wildfire risk (Zscheischler et al., 2018).
43   Given the severe impacts of wildfires, there is an increasing need to minimize their harm
44   and, as such, the ability to ensure operational preparedness. In this context, reliable wild-
45   fire predictions and developing wildfire early warning systems (WEWS) are essential for
46   reducing wildfire impacts and improving response efforts.
47       Wildfires occur when an ignition source interacts with available fuel under suitable
48   weather conditions, with subsequent behavior often influenced by terrain. Like other nat-
49   ural hazards, wildfires are governed by multiple controls across various spatiotemporal
50   scales. At the smallest scale, fire behavior is commonly described by the “fire triangle”
51   concept (Countryman, 1972; Pyne et al., 1996), identifying heat, fuel, and oxygen as the
52   three fundamental components necessary for combustion. At the scale of a single wildfire,
53   its occurrence and propagation are driven by a complex interplay of static and dynamic
54   environmental controls (Moritz et al., 2005). Static factors such as the topography, hydro-
55   climatic predisposition, and vegetation (fuel type) can determine whether an area is prone
56   to wildfires or the “where” (Faivre et al., 2014). In contrast, the timing, or the “when”, is
57   governed by dynamic controls such as air temperature, precipitation, fuel moisture, and
58   anthropogenic influences—including arson or accidental ignitions—which directly affect
59   ignition likelihood and fire spread (Bessie et al., 1995; Ganteaume et al., 2013). Therefore,
60   assessing “where” and “when” wildfires may occur requires a comprehensive evaluation
61   of static and dynamic factors influencing wildfire behavior (Finney, 2005; Koutsias et al.,
62   2015).
63       In this context, data-driven models are widely used to assess wildfire occurrence over
64   large areas (Martínez et al., 2009; Cao et al., 2013; Ghorbanzadeh et al., 2019; Tonini et al.,
65   2020; Bjånes et al., 2021; Trucchia et al., 2022b; Yue et al., 2023). Despite its widespread use
66   in fire prediction, the concept of susceptibility has been defined in various ways (Verde
67   et al., 2010; Cao et al., 2017; Leuenberger et al., 2018). In this contribution, we adopt a
68   definition analogous to that used for landslides and in agreement with Leuenberger et al.
69   (2018), describing wildfire susceptibility as the likelihood of a fire occurring within a given
70   spatial unit (e.g., grid cells, catchments, administrative units) based on local predispos-
71   ing factors. In other words, it predicts “where” wildfires will likely occur (Brabb, 1984;

                                                    2
72    Guzzetti et al., 2005). In a purely spatial context, data-driven models derive statistical re-
73    lationships between historical records of wildfire occurrence, i.e., wildfire inventories, as
74    static environmental factors to estimate wildfire susceptibility (Arndt et al., 2013; Oliveira
75    et al., 2012; Trucchia et al., 2022a; Zhang et al., 2021). Placing the spotlight on the spa-
76    tial domain, wildfire susceptibility assessments provide valuable insights to support fire
77    management, whether as standalone analyses or as part of broader risk assessment frame-
78    works and spatial planning strategies (Oliveira et al., 2021; Jappiot et al., 2009; Chuvieco
79    et al., 2010; Sebastián-López et al., 2008). Nevertheless, these approaches often disregard
80    the temporal component, excluding the assessment of critical meteorological conditions
81    that influence wildfire occurrence.
82        The temporal component of wildfire prediction is usually assessed through dynamic
83    factors, primarily focusing on daily variations in meteorological conditions, which form
84    the foundation of traditional WEWS (Chuvieco et al., 2010) and serve as tools for defin-
85    ing local fuel treatments, surveillance, and suppression activities (Ager et al., 2010; Scott
86    et al., 2012; Oliveira et al., 2021). Numerous largely empirical wildfire danger indices,
87    also called fire danger rating systems, are used worldwide. A comprehensive literature
88    review of these index-based rating systems is beyond the scope of this contribution and
89    can be found in Viegas et al. (1999); Chuvieco et al. (2003); Arpaci et al. (2013); Giuseppe
90    et al. (2016); Pérez-Sánchez et al. (2017); Sirca et al. (2018); Pagnon Eriksson et al. (2023).
91    Wildfire danger indices empirically quantify the relative wildfire danger in a given area
92    based on meteorological conditions. Notable examples include the Canadian fire weather
93    index (FWI; Wagner et al., 1987), which relies on meteorological input data—wind speed,
94    air temperature, relative humidity, and precipitation—to assess daily wildfire danger. Al-
95    though initially developed for pine fuel types prevalent in Canadian forests, the FWI has
96    been extensively tested worldwide (Kudláčková et al., 2024). Similarly, the McArthur’s
97    forest danger index (FDI; Noble et al., 1980; Hollis et al., 2024) was developed to evaluate
98    wildfire danger and behavior in Australian eucalyptus forests, incorporating fuel avail-
99    ability implicitly through atmospheric conditions. Another example is the Italian index
100   IREPI (Bovio et al., 1984), designed to assess wildfire danger during the winter–spring
101   season in northwestern Italy, which accounts for soil water loss due to evapotranspiration
102   derived from the multiple meteorological input data. Despite the utility of this fire danger
103   system and its spatially explicit features, these rating systems often overlook factors such
104   as intrinsic terrain characteristics, seasonality, and human influences, both of which are
105   critical in wildfire ignition and propagation (Chuvieco et al., 2010).
106       The integration of the spatial and temporal components for wildfire prediction in large
107   areas remains challenging. However, existing studies underscore its considerable poten-
108   tial. For instance, de Santana et al. (2021) modeled the occurrence of wildfires over a 19-
109   year period by integrating static and dynamic predictors aggregated on an annual scale,
110   generating independent susceptibility maps for each year. In Verde et al. (2010); Parente
111   et al. (2016); Oliveira et al. (2021), the wildfire hazard was estimated by combining the
112   static wildfire susceptibility with a probabilistic assessment of wildfire recurrence, based
113   on projected future burned areas within each mapping unit. Bergonse et al. (2021) in-
114   tegrated wildfire susceptibility with a meteorological index representing spring condi-
115   tions, enabling predictions for any given year before the critical fire season. Conversely,
116   Deng et al. (2023) used a deep learning approach to predict daily wildfire ignition scores

                                                    3
117   by integrating dynamic weather predictors—such as temperature, rainfall, humidity, and
118   wind—with static factors. Richards et al. (2023) leveraged a spatiotemporal interpretable
119   neural network to model both wildfire ignition and spread, incorporating both static and
120   dynamic factors at a monthly resolution over the period 2001–2020. Woolford et al. (2011)
121   employed generalized additive models (GAMs) to predict wildfire occurrence during the
122   active fire season over a 24-year period, integrating both static and dynamic factors. Their
123   study emphasized the high interpretability of the chosen approach and the influence of
124   anthropogenic predictors, such as population density and proximity to infrastructure.
125       In the Alpine context, wildfires pose a significant threat to mountain forests and their
126   protective function against other natural hazards such as rockfalls, avalanches, mudflows,
127   increased run-off, and soil erosion (Maringer et al., 2016; Martin et al., 2001; Robichaud
128   et al., 2006). The region is characterized by two main fire seasons: an early spring peak in
129   March or April, driven by frost-induced dryness, and a summer peak in July and August
130   due to high temperatures (Müller et al., 2020a). To support wildfire assessment and forest
131   protection, multiple WEWS have been developed across different Alpine countries. For in-
132   stance, the European forest fire information system (EFFIS; Giuseppe et al., 2016) provides
133   large-scale fire danger assessments at a spatial resolution of 8×8 km, but its applicability
134   in complex Alpine terrain is limited. National-level models, such as the one in Austria,
135   achieve resolutions of 1×1 km using meteorological inputs from weather forecasting sys-
136   tems like INCA (Haiden et al., 2011), although a more recent prototype was developed,
137   achieving a spatial resolution of 100×100 m (Müller et al., 2020b). Despite these efforts,
138   wildfires in the Alps remain a significant challenge. In Italy, the average Alpine wildfire
139   covers about one hectare, but 10% of the fires exceed 10 ha, accounting for 85% of the total
140   burned area (Müller et al., 2020a). Human activities are the primary cause of ignitions,
141   responsible for about 90% of ignitions, though lightning-induced fires can contribute up
142   to 50% of ignitions in some areas during the summer season (Müller et al., 2013). Ad-
143   vancements in WEWS have brought improvements, yet the spatial resolution of existing
144   models might be insufficient to capture the complex interactions between wildfire behav-
145   ior and the heterogeneous Alpine topography, including narrow valleys, mountain peaks,
146   high-altitude plateaus, and their effects on weather controls (Carrega, 1995; Schunk et al.,
147   2013; Müller et al., 2020b). As wildfire frequency and severity are expected to rise, there
148   is a growing need for high-resolution fire prediction models that integrate both static and
149   dynamic factors to enhance preparedness and mitigation strategies (Chuvieco et al., 2010;
150   Wastl et al., 2012).
151       This study investigates space-time wildfire initiation modeling by adapting a previ-
152   ously developed framework devised for the assessment of mass movement hazards in an
153   Alpine region of northern Italy (Steger et al., 2023; Moreno et al., 2024; Steger et al., 2024;
154   Moreno et al., 2025). Following a similar modeling approach, we aim to integrate static
155   and dynamic wildfire controls using data-driven techniques to estimate wildfire initiation
156   probabilities in space and time. Furthermore, we aim to enhance the applicability of the
157   model by linking the predicted probabilities to quantitative thresholds. These analyses
158   are performed for the region of Trentino–South Tyrol (Italy), covering a period of 24 years
159   (2000–2023).
160       The remainder of this paper is structured as follows: Section 2 describes the study area
161   and its environmental characteristics. Section 3 introduces the wildfire historical records

                                                    4
162   and the different static and dynamic factors used in the modeling. Section 4 details the
163   methods, including the sampling procedure, data extraction, modeling with GAMs, vali-
164   dation strategies, and the thresholding approach. Section 5 presents the key results, em-
165   phasizing data sampling, model interpretation, and application. Ultimately, Sections 6
166   and 7 discuss the findings and conclude with an outlook on future research directions.


167   2    Study area
168   Our study area (shown in Figure 1) corresponds to the upper Adige River Basin, encom-
169   passing the region of Trentino-South Tyrol in the northeastern Italian Alps, covering an
170   area of about 13,600 km2 . Its diversified landscape and climatic conditions characterize
171   the region. The mountainous topography is dominated by pronounced variations in eleva-
172   tion, ranging between ∼65 m a.s.l. (above sea level) in the southernmost part of the region,
173   close to Lake Garda to ∼3900 m a.s.l. at the Ortles peak in the northwest. Due to phys-
174   iographical features, Trentino-South Tyrol is highly prone to various mountain-related
175   natural hazards, including avalanches, rockfalls, and mudflows. Due to the proximity of
176   human settlements to the steep Alpine terrain, a significant portion of the infrastructure
177   is exposed to these processes.
178       The interaction of humid air masses from the northwest Atlantic, warm Mediterranean
179   influences, and dry continental air from the east shapes the regional climate. This intersec-
180   tion gives rise to a pronounced seasonal cycle characterized by warm and humid summers
181   and cold and dry winters (Norbiato et al., 2009; Adler et al., 2015). The complex interplay
182   of topography and diverse atmospheric conditions of the region determines the strong
183   seasonality and spatial variations in precipitation and temperature. The annual precipita-
184   tion sum varies significantly across the region, ranging from ∼500 mm in the northwestern
185   rain-shaded inner valleys to over 1,700 mm in the southeastern parts, maintaining a spa-
186   tial average over the study area of about 1000 mm. Similarly, mean annual temperature
187   varies substantially, from approximately +14∘ C in the Garda Valley to around −11∘ C at
188   the Ortles peak, with an overall regional average of about +5∘ C. The annual cycles of pre-
189   cipitation and temperature are characterized by the warmest and wettest conditions dur-
190   ing summer (July–August) and the coldest and driest conditions towards the late winter
191   (January–February). The most significant warming and precipitation increase occurs to-
192   wards spring (April–May; Crespi et al., 2021).
193       The land cover in the region is predominantly forest (∼45%), primarily consisting of
194   coniferous trees located on hillsides. Herbaceous vegetation and heathland account for
195   ∼35%, while cultivated land, mainly concentrated in the valley bottoms, represents ∼8%.
196   The remaining land cover includes mostly rocky outcrops, water bodies, permanent snow-
197   covered surfaces, and urban areas.

199   3.1    Wildfire database
200   The wildfire inventory for the region was obtained from the Forestry Service Department
201   (Abteilung Forstdienst/Ripartizione Servizio Forestale) of South Tyrol and the Forestry
202   and Wildlife Service of Trentino (Servizio Foreste e Fauna). The databases were accessed
203   in early 2024; the former was directly provided by the office in charge, and the latter can
204   be accessed at https://siat.provincia.tn.it/geonetwork/srv/ita/catalog.

205       The wildfire inventory of South Tyrol is divided into two periods: records from 1999
206   to 2017 and those from 2017 onward. This distinction reflects changes in mapping tech-
207   niques, as polygonal representations of burned areas were introduced only after 2017. Be-
208   fore this, fire locations were recorded as points, presumably indicating a location within
209   the affected area. The dataset includes 608 wildfire events with attributes such as date and
210   time of occurrence, duration, total burned area, and causes.
211       Similarly, the wildfire inventory of Trentino is structured into two periods: records
212   from 1966 to 1983 and those from 1984 onward. The earlier dataset comprises 1,062 events,
213   where only the burned area polygons were documented, without associated timestamps
214   and other relevant attributes. From 1984 onward, the dataset includes 3,134 wildfire
215   events, with temporal resolution improving over time. While timestamps were recorded
216   at the yearly level until 2007, only 507 events after 2007 include precise day-of-occurrence
217   information, leaving 2,627 with year-only time stamps. The recorded attributes in this
218   dataset include the occurrence time (with varying temporal resolution), total burned area,
219   and forested burned area.
220       Since the main objective of this study is to predict wildfire occurrence in space and
221   time, the analyses focus on wildfire events for which occurrence dates are available. Only
222   these events were extracted from the inventories to ensure the desired temporal resolution
223   in the modeling. Further details on this methodical step are provided in Section 4.1.

224   3.2   Environmental factors
225   Static factors
226   Identifying wildfire-prone areas using data-driven approaches relies on analyzing spatial
227   environmental factors observed at locations with and without wildfire occurrences. The
228   choice of predictors was guided by the numerous contributions that have explored the
229   influence of different static factors and their role in wildfire modeling (Ganteaume et al.,
230   2013; Jain et al., 2020). For this case study, we prioritized predictors that offer meaningful
231   insights into wildfire occurrence.
232       We used the NASADEM (NASA JPL, 2020) to derive standard morphological factors
233   such as slope steepness, which may facilitate fire spread (Chuvieco et al., 2010), and as-
234   pect—decomposed into the components eastness and northness—to capture variations in
235   solar exposure and moisture retention (Müller et al., 2020b). A proxy for vegetation and
236   fuel types was represented using data extracted from the CORINE land cover and high-
237   resolution map of Europe 2017 (Malinowski et al., 2020). The data were subsequently
238   reclassified into eight categories: (i) artificial surfaces, (ii) agricultural land, (iii) broadleaf
239   tree cover, (iv) coniferous tree cover, (v) herbaceous vegetation and heathland, (vi) marshes and
240   peatbogs, (vii) natural material surfaces, and (viii) permanent snow-covered surfaces and water
241   bodies. To further characterize vegetation within forested areas and measure forest struc-
242   ture, we utilized the tree cover density dataset extracted from European Union’s Copernicus
243   Land Monitoring Service information (2020). Climatic predisposition predictors, includ-
244   ing total annual precipitation and mean annual temperature, averaged over a 30-year period
245   from 1981 to 2010, were also tested to characterize the regional climate and its influence on
246   wildfire occurrence (Crespi et al., 2020). Finally, to account for the anthropogenic compo-
247   nent and infrastructure, buildings and roads were extracted from OpenStreetMap (OSM;

                                                       7
248   OpenStreetMap contributors, 2017). The Euclidean distance to buildings and roads were
249   then computed to incorporate their effects on wildfire modeling. All the environmental
250   factors were resampled to a 50 m spatial resolution for modeling purposes.

251   Dynamic factors–gridded precipitation and temperature fields
252   We used the high-resolution gridded daily temperature and precipitation dataset for
253   Trentino–South Tyrol provided in Crespi et al. (2021). The dataset was built upon a dense
254   network of over 200 meteorological stations, ensuring comprehensive coverage across the
255   study area. Initially, the data processing involved multiple methodical steps, including
256   quality and consistency checks, homogeneity tests, and gap-filling techniques to maxi-
257   mize data completeness. Using the processed database, an anomaly-based interpolation
258   scheme was applied to project the daily fields of precipitation and mean temperature onto
259   a grid of 250 m resolution. The accuracy, estimated via leave-one-out cross-validation,
260   yielded a mean absolute error (MAE) of 1.1 mm for precipitation and 1.5∘ C for mean tem-
261   perature, averaged across all stations and months. For each day, the dataset provides the
262   total precipitation accumulated over 24 hours, from 08:00 UTC of the previous day to 08:00
263   UTC of the observation day, and the daily mean temperature is defined as the average of
264   the daily maximum and minimum temperature.


265   4    Method
266   The method implemented in this research extends a data-driven framework initially de-
267   vised for the assessment of mass movement hazards (Steger et al., 2023; Moreno et al., 2024;
268   Steger et al., 2024; Moreno et al., 2025), adapting it to integrate static and dynamic wildfire
269   controls for estimating wildfire occurrence probabilities in space and time. The workflow
270   comprises five main steps: (i) filtering and sampling rulesets. Since our model is based on
271   binary data, we first need to define our target variable: the areas with wildfire occurrence
272   or wildfire presences and those without or wildfire absences. First, we defined the wild-
273   fire presences by combining the different wildfire databases and filtering the individual
274   records based on the inventory attributes. This is followed by the definition of wildfire ab-
275   sences, where we applied a strategic sampling procedure to define wildfire absences, con-
276   sidering both spatial and temporal aspects. The subsequent (ii) data extraction–precipitation
277   and temperature time windows phase retrieves static and dynamic predictors, with dynamic
278   predictors grouped into time windows. In (iii) modeling and grid search, we implement a
279   binomial GAM to perform a grid search and select the optimal combination of cumulative
280   precipitation and temperature to describe wildfire occurrence, which is then integrated
281   with static predictors to fit the final spatiotemporal model. (iv) Model evaluation follows,
282   incorporating plausibility checks and cross-validation routines from multiple perspectives
283   to assess predictive performance. Finally, the predictions are linked to quantitative met-
284   rics, facilitating (v) visualization and thresholding, with a hindcasting example provided to
285   demonstrate model applicability.

286   4.1   Filtering and sampling rulesets
287   Wildfire presences
288   The four wildfire inventories—two from South Tyrol and two from Trentino—were pre-
289   processed before being integrated into a single dataset. For South Tyrol, we used only the
290   point-based wildfire records. These records initially spanned the period from 1999 to 2017
291   but remained consistently available through 2023. Although polygon-based records were
292   available after 2017, they were excluded while point-based records were retained to ensure
293   spatial consistency, as comparisons between points and polygons did not always follow a
294   clear pattern. Since our analysis period starts in the year 2000, any events occurring before
295   this year were removed, resulting in the filtered wildfire dataset for South Tyrol.
296       Since our model aims to predict wildfire occurrence on a daily scale, only wildfire
297   records with an associated daily timestamp were considered. In the case of Trentino, this
298   requirement led to the selection of the inventory covering the period from 1984 onward,
299   while records from earlier periods, without any timestamp, were excluded. Addition-
300   ally, wildfire events lacking a recorded day of occurrence (i.e., those before 2007) were re-
301   moved. To ensure a uniform spatial representation across the inventories in South Tyrol
302   and Trentino, wildfire occurrences in Trentino were represented as point centroids of their
303   respective burned area polygons.
304       The two inventories were merged into a single dataset, forming the preliminary wild-
305   fire sample. Events with a burned area smaller than 10 m2 were filtered out to ensure data
306   reliability, resulting in a final dataset for the subsequent analysis. Finally, for data extrac-
307   tion, we consider a buffer of 50 m around each point to enhance the spatial representative-
308   ness of each wildfire point.

309   Wildfire absences
310   Selecting wildfire absence data is just as crucial as the wildfire presence filtering. However,
311   defining the absences poses a greater challenge, as it requires identifying not only loca-
312   tion but time periods where wildfires are unlikely to have occurred. The sampling strategy
313   is essential in shaping the model structure and is directly reflected in the modeling out-
314   comes. Therefore, ensuring a well-balanced representation across spatial and temporal
315   domains is essential to prevent wrongly estimated model relationships and potentially
316   biased predictions. To this aim, we considered several rules, including (i) masking out
317   trivial terrains, (ii) implementing temporal buffers, (iii) balancing samples across years
318   and months, and (iv) masking out trivial periods that are detailed below.
319       Trivial terrains are locations where wildfire initiation is not expected. In our analy-
320   sis, we defined these areas based on the available fuel that can be accounted for using
321   the land cover map. In this way, the classes referring to artificial surfaces, natural material
322   surfaces (e.g., bare rock, sand), and permanent snow-covered areas and water bodies were ex-
323   cluded from the spatial domain as there is no fuel to be potentially burned. This resulted
324   in a mask being used to sample space wildfire absence locations. Notably, no recorded
325   wildfire events were located within trivial terrains.
326       Wildfire absences were sampled under two different schemes, including the sampling
327   of wildfire absences at presence locations and the sampling of wildfire absences at absence
328   locations. For the first scheme, wildfire absences at presence locations were generated by

                                                     9
329   replicating records from the filtered wildfire inventory and assigning a random observa-
330   tion date within the period of analysis, i.e., 01-01-2000 to 31-12-2023. To ensure that the
331   selected dates were temporally meaningful, a temporal buffer was applied to avoid select-
332   ing dates within five years of the respective wildfire occurrence. This step aimed to allow
333   sufficient time for vegetation (fuel) recovery before it may be subject to wildfire occur-
334   rence, as supported in Sass et al. (2017), Bowd et al. (2021), and Mantero et al. (2023). A
335   30-day temporal buffer was also introduced to avoid overlapping meteorological informa-
336   tion when extracting the dynamic predictors, as explained in Section 4.2.
337        For the second scheme, wildfire absences at absence locations were generated by ran-
338   domly sampling absence locations, excluding the trivial terrains and the areas that had
339   been burned before, according to the filtered inventory. Once absence locations were iden-
340   tified, they were replicated and assigned a random observation date for the specified anal-
341   ysis period (01-01-2000 to 31-12-2023). Similar to the first scheme, the date selection was
342   constrained by a 30-day temporal buffer to avoid overlapping meteorological data.
343        The preliminary wildfire absence subset was formed by combining the wildfire ab-
344   sences at presence locations and those at absence locations. Subsequently, the absence
345   subset was further refined by balancing the observation dates across the different years
346   and months to achieve a uniform temporal representation. The results of this step were
347   combined with the wildfire presences to obtain the modeling sample, which was subjected
348   to a final filtering step to account for trivial periods, as described in Section 4.2.

349   4.2   Data extraction–precipitation and temperature time windows
350   The static predictors were extracted to the modeling sample using the datasets described in
351   Section 3. Additionally, predictors regarding the occurrence date, specifically year, month,
352   and day of the year (doy) were derived from the assigned observed date in the modeling
353   sample. Data extraction was performed for the meteorological predictors such as precip-
354   itation and temperature by linking each sample location and date to the high-resolution
355   gridded dataset from Crespi et al. (2021). Both predictors were retrieved up to 30 days
356   before the assigned observation date. Similar to trivial terrains, we defined trivial periods
357   based on a precipitation threshold so that observations with precipitation amounts ≥1.1
358   mm on the day of observation were excluded from the analysis. This filtering step was
359   necessary because including large amounts of “wet” days—presumably with no wildfire
360   occurrence—into the binary classification model would result in the model simply learn-
361   ing the difference between “dry” and “wet” conditions. Hence, we applied the precipi-
362   tation threshold to explicitly keep the modeling target to predict wildfire occurrence in
363   “dry” periods.
364       Finally, the precipitation and temperature predictors were structured into multiple
365   time windows for analysis. The predictor P represents the cumulative precipitation within
366   a given time window, while Te corresponds to the mean daily temperature over the same
367   period. As an analogy to what was described in Steger et al. (2023), P aims to represent
368   the medium-term preparatory precipitation, which can be seen as a proxy for the surface
369   moisture conditions, whereas Te can be seen as the short-term temperature. This approach
370   enabled a systematic evaluation of how the combination of different antecedent precipi-
371   tation and temperature influences wildfire occurrence.

                                                   10
372   4.3   Modeling using GAMs and grid search
373   Theoretical background
374   Generalized additive models are semi-parametric regression models that extend general-
375   ized linear models (GLMs) by incorporating additive smooth functions to capture nonlin-
376   ear relationships between predictor variables and the response (Wood, 2006; Bolker et al.,
377   2009; Zuur et al., 2009). In this study, the binary response variable, representing wildfire
378   presence or absence, follows a Bernoulli probability distribution and can be generically
379   expressed as:

                             logit(𝑝𝑖 ) = 𝛽0 + 𝑓1 (𝑥1𝑖 ) + 𝑓2 (𝑥2𝑖 ) + ⋯ + 𝑓𝑛 (𝑥𝑛𝑖 )                 (1)
380       Where 𝑓1 , 𝑓2 and 𝑓𝑛 are smooth functions to model the nonlinear effects on each of the
381   predictors 𝑥1𝑖 , 𝑥2𝑖 , and 𝑥3𝑖 . A logistic link function is applied to relate 𝑝𝑖 to the effects of
382   the predictor variables. Due to their high interpretability and flexibility—often described
383   in the literature as white-box models—GAMs have been extensively used across various
384   disciplines, including natural hazard modeling (Woolford et al., 2011; Pourtaghi et al.,
385   2016; Ríos-Pena et al., 2017; Lombardo et al., 2020; Eskandari et al., 2021; Opitz et al., 2022;
386   Moreno et al., 2023; Alvioli et al., 2024).

387   Grid search and model fit
388   In this contribution, GAMs were applied in two distinct phases: (i) to identify the optimal
389   time windows P and Te that best describe wildfire initiation, and (ii) to integrate static
390   factors with the identified best P and Te windows to estimate wildfire probabilities in space
391   and time. The analyses were conducted using the comprehensive R package mgcv (version
392   1.9-1; Wood, 2017).
393       The selection of optimal time windows for P and Te was conducted through a system-
394   atic grid search. This procedure involved a pairwise comparison of different P and Te time
395   window combinations using a binomial GAM. This model was fitted to the previously de-
396   fined modeling sample, as explained in 4.1, incorporating iteratively the different combi-
397   nations of P and Te, and the doy as predictors. The grid search evaluated 30 time windows
398   for P and 10 for Te, resulting in 300 unique combinations. Each combination underwent a
399   10-fold random cross-validation with 10 repetitions to ensure robustness, yielding 300,000
400   iterations. The performance of the model was recorded for each iteration—based on well-
401   known performance metrics addressed in Section 4.4—allowing for a systematic compar-
402   ison to identify the optimal pair of time windows that best describe wildfire initiation.
403       The selected optimal time windows P and Te were integrated with the static predictors
404   to develop the spatiotemporal wildfire initiation model. The model fitting process was it-
405   erative, starting with a simple baseline configuration and progressively incorporating ad-
406   ditional predictors. Each newly introduced predictor was assessed by visually inspecting
407   partial effect plots and formally inspecting p-values, confidence intervals, and the overall
408   model fitting performance. Once the final model fit was obtained, the relative contribution
409   of each predictor was evaluated using a permutation-based variable importance analysis.
410   This assessment indicates how much each predictor contributes to the model by randomly
411   permuting its values and measuring the resulting change in the model prediction error.


                                                       11
412   Specifically, the analysis was based on the deviance explained—a widely used measure
413   of goodness of fit derived from the likelihood function. The importance of each predictor
414   was quantified by comparing the deviance explained by the final model fit against a series
415   of models, each with a single predictor permuted. These permutations disrupt the origi-
416   nal relationship between the predictor and the response; therefore, a greater reduction in
417   the deviance explained indicates a more substantial contribution of each permuted predic-
418   tor (Schlögl et al., 2025). In this study, permutation feature importance was summarized
419   using the mean and standard deviation to quantify both the average effect and the associ-
420   ated uncertainty. For the interpretation and plausibility of the model relationships, partial
421   effect plots were generated, illustrating how individual predictors influence wildfire oc-
422   currence probabilities while holding all other predictors constant at their average values.
423   Additionally, interaction effects between predictors were visualized using contour plots,
424   providing further insights into the complex relationships governing wildfire dynamics.

425   4.4   Model evaluation
426   For a comprehensive evaluation of the model performance, we leveraged multiple vali-
427   dation strategies using the R package sperrorest (version 3.0.5; Brenning, 2012). The as-
428   sessment included k-fold random cross-validation (RCV), k-fold spatial cross-validation
429   (SCV), temporal cross-validation (TCV) based on years and months, and leave-one-factor-
430   out cross-validation (FCV) using the land cover data. These approaches ensured a robust
431   model generalization and transferability assessment across different spatial, temporal, and
432   environmental conditions.
433       Random cross-validation was implemented by partitioning the dataset into indepen-
434   dent training and testing sets. We applied a ten-fold RCV with ten repetitions, result-
435   ing in 100 iterations (Brenning, 2012). While RCV provides a general measure of model
436   predictive performance, it may overlook spatial heterogeneity, potentially leading to over-
437   optimistic results if the model performs inconsistently across different subregions. To ad-
438   dress this, we incorporated SCV, which estimates the spatial transferability of the model
439   by evaluating its performance in spatially distinct partitions. The spatial partitioning was
440   achieved using a k-means clustering approach, ensuring alignment with the ten-fold, ten-
441   repetition setup used in RCV.
442       To further assess model generalization and transferability, we applied TCV and FCV.
443   The former was conducted by iteratively excluding wildfire observations from either
444   one month (leave-one-month-out) or one year (leave-one-year-out) and then evaluating
445   model predictions on the omitted data. This approach allowed us to test how well the
446   model performs when transferred to unseen time periods at the scale of years and months.
447   Similarly, FCV was performed using the five nontrivial land cover classes—agricultural
448   land, broadleaf tree cover, coniferous tree cover, herbaceous vegetation, and heathland, and marshes
449   and peatbogs— to define independent training and testing datasets, ensuring that the model
450   performance was not overly dependent on specific land cover conditions.
451       The model performance for each validation approach was quantified using the area
452   under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) computed for independent
453   testing sets. The ROC curve represents the classifier performance across varying cut-off
454   thresholds, with AUROCS values ranging from 0.5, i.e., random classification to 1, i.e.,

                                                      12
455   perfect discrimination (Hosmer et al., 2013). Higher AUROC values indicated stronger
456   performance, providing an objective measure to compare different validation strategies
457   and assess the model robustness across spatial, temporal, and land cover variations.


458   4.5   Thresholding and visualization
459   In this research, we adapted an extension of empirical rainfall thresholds–—traditionally
460   employed in landslide early warning systems(Brunetti et al., 2010)—–to wildfire predic-
461   tion. Conventional landslides thresholds operate on the premise that slope failures be-
462   come probable when specific rainfall parameters exceed critical values. Recent advance-
463   ments in Steger et al. (2024) showed that spatial dynamic landslide probability thresholds
464   can be derived by incorporating both dynamic meteorological controls and static terrain
465   conditions. Building upon this methodological framework, we developed analogous spa-
466   tially explicit thresholds for wildfire prediction, enabling the reclassification of dynamic
467   probability maps to directly visualize threshold exceedance patterns across varying spa-
468   tiotemporal contexts.
469       In our wildfire analysis, we employ heatmap visualizations to represent wildfire proba-
470   bility scores for increasing amounts of the meteorological predictors P and Te. To enhance
471   the practical application of this results, we established three distinct threshold derived
472   from the ROC curve, transforming the continuous probability scores (0–1) into meaning-
473   ful discrete classes. These thresholds represent specific combinations between correctly
474   classified wildfires (true positive rate or sensitivity; TPR) and wrongly classified absences
475   (false alarms or false positive rate; FPR), thereby providing a more nuanced categoriza-
476   tions of wildfire likelihood. The TPR95 threshold establishes a high sensitivity benchmark,
477   ensuring that 95% of observed wildfires occur in areas and periods exceeding this prob-
478   ability value. While this threshold effectively captures most wildfire-prone conditions, it
479   comes at the expense of generating a high proportion of false alarms. On the other hand,
480   the TPR35 threshold implements a more stringent criterion, identifying only 35% of wild-
481   fire occurrences. This higher-probability threshold substantially reduces false alarms but
482   excludes 65% of actual wildfire events, capturing only the most extreme wildfire-prone
483   conditions.
484       Positioned between these two extremes, we implemented an optimal OPT threshold
485   based on the Youden index, which maximizes the sum of correctly classified wildfire and
486   non-wildfire locations and periods. This threshold corresponds to the point on the ROC
487   curve with maximum vertical distance from the diagonal line representing a random clas-
488   sification (Fluss et al., 2005; Ruopp et al., 2008). The OPT threshold provides the most
489   balanced compromise between the classification of wildfire and non-wildfire conditions.
490   Utilizing these three thresholds, our final dynamic probability map delineates four dis-
491   tinct wildfire likelihood classes, with OPT as the central reference point.
492       The resulting dynamic maps directly visualize wildfire threshold exceedance across
493   the study region. To further evaluate the practical utility of the methodical approach, we
494   conducted a comprehensive hindcasting analysis. This exercise allowed us to reconstruct
495   and examine the spatiotemporal evolution of wildfire probability thresholds during the
496   period of 1–15 July 2022, when several wildfire events affected the study area.

686   6     Discussion
687   In this study, we implemented an interpretable spatiotemporal modeling framework that
688   integrates static environmental and dynamic meteorological factors to predict wildfire oc-
689   currence. The model demonstrates strong predictive capabilities, with AUROC values
690   generally exceeding 0.80, highlighting its effectiveness in capturing key drivers of wild-
691   fire initiation. These included static landscape features, daily temperature and precipita-
692   tion fields, and a seasonal proxy. The following discussion addresses critical aspects of
693   the study, including reflections on model interpretability and flexibility, considerations
694   related to wildfire processes and drivers, and the applicability of the model in early warn-
695   ing.


696   6.1   Model flexibility and interpretability
697   The sampling strategy played a key role in constructing the modeling dataset. As the
698   quality and representativeness of the input data heavily influenced the model outcomes,
699   careful design of the sampling process is as relevant as the modeling phase itself. While
700   many data-driven studies emphasize comparing algorithms, evaluating data preparation
701   and sampling strategies is often underappreciated. Generating the modeling sample in-
702   volves filtering the wildfire presences and sampling the absence observations due to the
703   large disproportion between the two. The selection of wildfire presences—conditioned on
704   the availability of historical records—enables event filtering based on the attributes in the
705   wildfire inventory, e.g., date of occurrence, and burned area. In contrast, sampling absence
706   observations, especially in the spatiotemporal domain, offers broader flexibility. In this re-
707   search, the absence sampling was guided by set of structured rules: as detailed in Section
708   4.1, we (a) masked out trivial terrain based on land cover data, (b) implemented tempo-
709   ral buffers in locations that experienced wildfires before, (c) ensured temporal balance by


                                                   23
710   sampling across different years and months, and (d) excluded trivial periods. These steps
711   collectively aimed to reduce sampling biases and improve the model generalization.
712       The modeling approach considers the integration of static and dynamic factors to ac-
713   count for the complex interplay of wildfire occurrence. An advantage of this framework is
714   the interpretability of the model outcomes, which we briefly highlight in the following dis-
715   cussion. The optimal time window analysis identified that the combination yielding the
716   highest predictive performance includes Te0 , the mean daily temperature on the obser-
717   vation day, and P28 , the cumulative precipitation over the preceding 29 days (see Figure
718   4). While this combination achieved the highest median AUROC, it is noteworthy that
719   performance differences among alternative time window configurations were relatively
720   small, with AUROC values ranging from 0.77 to above 0.81. This reinforces the impor-
721   tance of precipitation and temperature as dynamic predictors, while also indicating that
722   the model results are relatively stable and not highly sensitive to changes in the selected
723   time windows.
724       Regarding the modeled relationships illustrated in Figure 5 Aspect depicts high prob-
725   abilities in slopes facing south (135∘ –225∘ ). In the northern hemisphere, south-facing
726   slopes receive more solar radiation during the day than others, and this translates to lower
727   average moisture levels and thus increasing wildfire probabilities, as several studies indi-
728   cate (Alexander et al., 2006; Oliveira et al., 2013; Oliveras et al., 2009). The tree density cover
729   reveals that areas with very sparse tree cover (<20%), may lack sufficient fuel to sustain
730   wildfires, while densely forested areas (>70%) appear to be more resilient to wildfire ini-
731   tiation due to forest microclimate effects—tendency to be cooler, more humid and with
732   less sun exposure. In contrast, regions with intermediate and fragmented tree density
733   cover (20–70%) exhibit the highest wildfire probabilities, likely due to a combination of
734   fuel availability and increased edge effects (Song et al., 2017; Rotbarth et al., 2025).
735       The total annual precipitation was used as a proxy to represent the spatial variability
736   of long-term precipitation regimes across the study area. The results indicate that drier
737   regions are more susceptible to wildfire occurrence, whereas wetter areas are associated
738   with lower wildfire probabilities. Similarly, the mean annual temperature captures broader
739   spatial thermal gradients, revealing that relatively warmer zones are generally more prone
740   to wildfires than colder ones. These drier and warmer areas tend to exceed the criti-
741   cal probability thresholds earlier under specific temperature and precipitation conditions,
742   compared to their wetter and cooler counterparts.
743       Although the wildfire inventory lacks the specific data on the fire causes for our study
744   area, previous research in comparable alpine regions has identified human activity as a
745   dominant ignition source (Arpaci et al., 2014; Müller et al., 2020b; Arndt et al., 2013; Müller
746   et al., 2020a). In our analysis, distance to buildings was used as a proxy to account for an-
747   thropogenic influence. The results indicate that areas closer to infrastructure—and thus
748   more accessible to human activity—are associated with higher wildfire probability. Ini-
749   tially, distance to roads was also considered an additional proxy; however, including both
750   predictors in the model fit introduced redundancy, leading to the loss of statistical signif-
751   icance in one of them. Therefore, we retained only distance to buildings, given its broader
752   spatial representation of human influence.
753       The land cover, identified as the second most influential predictor, served as a proxy for
754   fuel availability. Compared to the reference category agricultural land, all other classes ex-

                                                      24
755   hibited association with higher wildfire probability. Overall, agricultural land appeared to
756   be the least prone to wildfire occurrence. Among the remaining classes, marshes and peat-
757   bogs were slightly less preferred regarding wildfire occurrence. Both coniferous tree cover
758   and broadleaf tree cover showed nearly identical regression coefficients, suggesting a com-
759   parable influence on wildfire probability—consistent with the findings reported in Müller
760   et al. (2020a). The herbaceous vegetation and heathland class displayed a slightly higher coef-
761   ficient, pointing to increased fire likelihood. This supports the conclusions in Oliveira et al.
762   (2013), where it was reported that shrublands, grasslands, and sparsely vegetated areas
763   are more prone to ignition due to their intrinsic flammability and ease of combustion.
764       The predictor doy, used as a proxy for seasonal effects, is expected to capture the tempo-
765   ral distribution of wildfire occurrences throughout the year. However, since the model al-
766   ready incorporates two dynamic predictors, P28 and Te0 , which inherently exhibit seasonal
767   variability, we interpret the effect of doy as capturing residual seasonality not explained by
768   these two meteorological predictors. Notably, the model reveals elevated wildfire prob-
769   ability in early spring, a period typically associated with rapid warming and snowmelt.
770   This suggests that the selected meteorological predictors may be less effective in fully cap-
771   turing the seasonal dynamics influencing fire occurrence during this transitional period.
772   Conversely, doy indicates comparatively lower fire probabilities during summer, which
773   we interpret as evidence that P28 and Te0 already account for much of the seasonal vari-
774   ation during that time. In practical terms, this implies that the wildfire probability may
775   be higher in early spring than in late summer for a given location and under the same
776   precipitation and temperature conditions.
777       These results are a sample of the complex interplay between dynamic meteorological
778   and static environmental factors in determining wildfire probability, providing quantita-
779   tive insights into the spatiotemporal dynamics of wildfire occurrence across the landscape.
780   Also, the analysis shows that identical meteorological conditions can produce markedly
781   different wildfire probabilities depending on the underlying environmental context, high-
782   lighting the importance of incorporating dynamic and static predictors in wildfire assess-
783   ment.

784   6.2   Limitations and future perspectives
785   Among all the predictors, the 29-day cumulative precipitation P28 and the 1-day mean tem-
786   perature Te0 emerged as the most influential ones in the model, as shown in Figure 6. Both
787   P28 and Te0 act as proxies for fuel moisture, so that drier fuels, resulting from prolonged
788   dry and warm periods, are more prone to wildfire ignition than moist fuels. Hence, lower
789   cumulative precipitation and high temperature levels reflect higher wildfire occurrence
790   probabilities. However, limitations arise from the use of these two predictors. The use
791   of absolute values in precipitation and temperature may mislead the results, since equal
792   levels of these two meteorological predictors can have different impacts depending on
793   the local climatologies. For instance, an area experiencing usually high temperatures and
794   anomalously low precipitation—relative to the long-term averages—may exhibit height-
795   ened wildfire probabilities compared to an area with equal static settings for which such
796   dynamic conditions are typical. In our model, we included total annual precipitation and
797   mean annual temperature averaged over 30 years to partly account for these climatologies.

                                                    25
798   Still, the variable importance assessment indicated they were among the least influential
799   predictors. We believe that the use of anomalies in the precipitation and temperature,
800   rather than absolute values, could provide the means to directly account for the clima-
801   tologies and improve the overall interpretability of the model. Future perspectives of the
802   model could benefit from integrating indices such as temperature anomalies (e.g., daily
803   deviations from the climatological means) or counts of days above-below specific temper-
804   ature thresholds. In terms of precipitation widely recognized drought indicators such as
805   the standardized precipitation index (SPI; McKee et al., 1993) or the standardized pre-
806   cipitation evapotranspiration index (SPEI; Vicente-Serrano et al., 2010) could be imple-
807   mented. These indices offer standardized, temporally dynamic representations of hydro-
808   logical stress and have proven valuable in capturing preconditions favorable for wildfire
809   occurrence (Turco et al., 2017, 2018; Smith et al., 2023).
810        In a similar context, one of the most relevant, yet underrepresented aspects in this
811   model is the role of compound events, particularly droughts and heatwaves. Compound—
812   simultaneous occurrence—hot and dry extreme events can trigger and exacerbate cascad-
813   ing processes such as wildfires (Richardson et al., 2022). Atmospheric heatwaves, defined
814   as prolonged and consecutive periods of anomalously high temperatures, have been con-
815   sistently linked to increased wildfire activity (Barriopedro et al., 2023). Similarly, “hot
816   drought” conditions (characterized by warm temperatures and simultaneous meteoro-
817   logical drought) represent elevated fire-weather types. Recent studies have identified two
818   heat-induced fire-weather types, “heatwave” and “hot drought”, which collectively ac-
819   count for nearly 50% of wildfires in the Mediterranean region (Ruffault et al., 2020; Santos
820   et al., 2024). These events are marked by significantly elevated temperatures, low humid-
821   ity, and dry fuels, often with anomalous values compared to surrounding days. Including
822   compound drought and heatwave indices in such a model would allow a more realistic
823   characterization of wildfire scenarios (Lemus-Canovas et al., 2025), especially in light of
824   expected increases in their frequency under global warming (Felsche et al., 2024).
825        As is the case for the meteorological predictors, further model improvements could
826   involve expanding some static predictors into dynamic representations. For example, land
827   cover, used as a proxy for fuel type, could be replaced or complemented by dynamic fuel
828   maps that reflect seasonal changes in vegetation structure and flammability. Similarly, the
829   anthropogenic influence—currently represented by static proximity to infrastructure—
830   could be much further elaborated through time-varying proxies such as mobility data,
831   population fluxes, or seasonal tourism patterns (Pittore et al., 2023; Renner et al., 2018).
832   Another limitation pertains to uncertainty in the wildfire inventory. In South Tyrol, the
833   dataset is point-based, yet the meaning of these points, whether they represent ignition lo-
834   cations or random points within burned polygons, is unclear. In Trentino, wildfire points
835   were generated from polygon centroids, assuming that ignition occurs at the spatial center
836   of the fire. This spatial uncertainty could affect the accuracy of predictor extraction, par-
837   ticularly for sensitive predictors. Furthermore, another point of improvement could be
838   adding the potential wildfire spread in addition to the occurrence probability. Incorpo-
839   rating fire spread in a second-stage model would require a potentially different modeling
840   framework and additional predictors (e.g., wind speed and direction, slope steepness;
841   Povak et al., 2018; Linn et al., 2007; Jellouli et al., 2022). Such an extension could sig-
842   nificantly enhance the predictive utility of the framework, particularly for operational or

                                                   26
843   early-warning applications.
844       To better tailor the model toward dry conditions, we applied a threshold-based filter-
845   ing to exclude periods characterized by excessive precipitation. However, the downside of
846   this approach is that it removed a significant number of summer wildfire records, many
847   likely linked to convective storms and lightning activity. These events are particularly im-
848   portant in alpine regions, where afternoon thunderstorms are frequent and lightning is
849   a known natural ignition source. As a result, our model may underrepresent lightning-
850   induced fires, specifically those occurring during so-called “wet” periods. In future devel-
851   opments, we recommend including classified atmospheric circulation patterns (Lemus-
852   Canovas et al., 2019) as part of the filtering rule to better account for lightning-induced
853   events.
854       Wildfires are more complex than other natural hazards, which often have natural phys-
855   ical triggers. A wildfire can only occur if a source of ignition is present, such as lightning as
856   a natural source, or the much more frequent (in)direct human influence (Chuvieco et al.,
857   2003; Müller et al., 2020a). While our model includes a proxy for anthropogenic influence,
858   distance to buildings, it does not explicitly account for lightning or human influence as trig-
859   gers. As such, the model outputs should not be interpreted as a pure relative probability
860   of wildfire initiation, but rather as the probability of an area experiencing a wildfire given
861   that ignition occurs (human or lightning). This distinction is crucial, particularly when
862   using the model to inform practitioners. In this context, the spatial predictions reflect the
863   relative predisposition of the landscape to wildfires under specific static terrain and dy-
864   namic meteorological conditions. Future studies should explore more direct proxies of
865   human activity, such as forest trail density, proximity to power lines, proximity to cable
866   cars, mobility datasets, and investigate multi-scale dynamic factors influencing ignition
867   potential (Arpaci et al., 2014; Chuvieco et al., 2010).

868   6.3   Considerations on early warning
869   The most relevant model outcome, the wildfire prediction scores, can be illustrated as
870   continuous probability surfaces (e.g., ranging from 0 to 1) or discrete categorized classes.
871   While the continuous outputs retain the full granularity of the model, categorized maps
872   are often preferred in decision-making contexts for their intuitive representation of dif-
873   ferent danger levels. However, deriving classes from statistical properties of the data dis-
874   tribution, such as quantiles, geometry, and standard deviation, can obscure the practi-
875   cal meaning and limit the interpretation for end-users. To address this, we adopted the
876   classification strategy proposed in Steger et al. (2023), which categorizes raw probabili-
877   ties based on their association with key performance metrics such as the TPR and false
878   alarms, which hold particular relevance in early warning. Among the evaluated thresh-
879   olds, the optimal cut-off OPT provided a balanced trade-off, capturing 77% of observed
880   wildfires while limiting the false alarms to 25%. Alternative threshold such as the TPR95
881   (high sensitivity) and the TPR35 (low false alarms) allow the approach to be tailored to
882   different risk priorities. Such priorities can be seen in a context where a false alarm may
883   lead to unnecessary allocation of resources, whereas a missed alarm may have more severe
884   consequences—–such as failing to issue timely warnings and inadequate preparedness for
885   wildfire events. However, as our results show, prioritizing high sensitivity (95%) substan-

                                                     27
886   tially increases false alarms (59%), whereas minimizing false alarms (e.g., 5%) comes at
887   the cost of reduced wildfire classification rates (TPR = 35%).
888       Other approaches for threshold selection are particularly valuable when dealing with
889   imbalanced datasets—such as those with few wildfire observations and many absences—
890   where standard metrics like accuracy may become less informative (Saito et al., 2015). In
891   such contexts, the thresholding can be guided based on the precision–recall curve, where
892   the thresholds are chosen based on the recall (TPR, proportion of actual wildfires correctly
893   classified) and precision (the proportion of predicted wildfire locations that correspond
894   to actual wildfires). This can provide a more meaningful assessment of predictive perfor-
895   mance, especially for early warning applications targeting rare but high-impact hazards
896   like wildfires (Patton et al., 2023). In any case, the threshold selection should always be
897   done accordingly to the end-user needs, whether the concern may be the cost of the false
898   alarms or if the tolerance is low to accept almost any alarm.
899       The temporal analysis showcased in this study confirms the model capacity to reflect
900   dynamic changes in wildfire probabilities in response to evolving meteorological condi-
901   tions. While originally applied for hindcasting, the model can be extended to nowcasting
902   and forecasting applications, provided that the input levels for precipitation and tempera-
903   ture do not substantially deviate from the fitting dataset. Notably, the model also enables
904   exploratory what-if scenarios, a valuable asset in the context of climate risk prepared-
905   ness and adaptation planning. For example, users can assess the implications of a drop
906   in cumulative precipitation P28 under anomalously high temperatures Te0 for given static
907   settings and a given seasonal context. Additional scenario-building can explore the role of
908   fuel-related predictors such as changes in tree cover density or transitions between land cover
909   classes, to evaluate their influence on wildfire probability. This narrative-driven simula-
910   tions offer a flexible tool to assess potential wildfire outcomes under hypothetical future
911   climatic conditions and support more informed and forward-looking decision-making in
912   wildfire risk management.
913       Our model delivers wildfire predictions at a relatively high spatial resolution (50
914   m) and daily temporal resolution, offering significantly finer granularity than existing
915   global and European early warning systems, such as EFFIS (current resolution: 9 km;
916   Di Giuseppe et al., 2025) and the former global early warning system for wildland fire
917   (https://gfmc.online/gwfews/index-12.html; de Groot et al., 2006), suspended on 2021.
918   This high-resolution capability is particularly advantageous in complex Alpine environ-
919   ments like our study area, where wildfires tend to be small-scale and spatially heteroge-
920   neous. Nevertheless, benchmarking to better understand potential benefits and limita-
921   tions in model outcomes is still relevant for future perspectives.

922   7    Conclusion
923   In this research, we employed GAMs to predict wildfire initiation in space and time, ac-
924   counting for static terrain attributes and dynamic meteorological conditions in the region
925   of Trentino–South Tyrol, Italy. We captured the meteorological drivers of wildfire occur-
926   rence based on a combination of 29-day cumulative precipitation (P28 ), 1-day mean tem-
927   perature (Te0 ), and day of the year (doy) factor. These dynamic predictors were comple-
928   mented by static proxies representing fuel type and structure, meteorological predisposi-
929   tion, topographic features, and anthropogenic influence. Particular emphasis was placed
930   on implementing a representative sampling scheme and generating interpretable outputs,
931   including the visualization of the modeled relationships and variable importance. Model
932   predictions were further linked and classified using combinations of true positive rates
933   and false alarms to derive thresholds that support practical applications in the realm
934   of early warning. The model showed strong predictive performance, with AUROC val-
935   ues generally exceeding 0.80 under a rigorous multi-validation framework accounting for
936   spatial and temporal variability. Beyond predictive skill, we illustrated the model utility
937   by hindcasting a two-week period of elevated wildfire activity in July 2022. The flexible
938   framework can be adapted to other natural hazards governed by the interplay of static and
939   dynamic factors. We see our approach as a contribution to the broader development of
940   data-driven solutions that characterize the occurrence of natural hazards through the joint
941   assessment of static controls and time-varying triggers. Crucially, we emphasize that such
942   models should prioritize input data quality, carefully designed sampling strategies, and
943   the interpretability of results to ensure their value in scientific and operational decision-
944   making contexts.
