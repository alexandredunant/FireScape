Mountain regions in Europe increasingly face compound runoff-
related hazards under a changing climate. This article compares
postfire surface runoff (PFSR) and frozen-soil surface runoff
(FSSR), 2 processes typically studied separately but sharing key
meteorological triggers, as well as initiation and propagation
mechanisms. Both are triggered by short, high-intensity rainfall
acting on infiltration barriers: hydrophobic layers in burned soil
and pore ice in frozen soil. Despite distinct thermal and seasonal
contexts, PFSR and FSSR exhibit convergent process chains
characterized by reduced interception, limited infiltration, surface
ponding, and efficient sediment entrainment. Their occurrence
increasingly transcends expected seasonal regimes, reflecting
climate-driven shifts in fire activity, snow dynamics, and freeze–
thaw cycles. Building on these parallels, this study proposes a
research framework and outlines a future research agenda that
links mapping, monitoring, experimentation, modeling, and
governance. It advocates integrated observation networks
combining remote sensing and in situ techniques, controlled
experiments to quantify thermohydraulic and erosional processes
and changes in vegetation cover, and physical models capturing
preferential flow and transient infiltration barriers. For risk
management, the article stresses predictive early warning
systems that provide sufficient lead time to manage evacuations
and infrastructure protection. By identifying shared mechanisms
and converging research needs, this contribution supports a
future research agenda and cross-disciplinary strategies for
mitigating emerging compound hazards in alpine environments.
Keywords: postfire surface runoff; frozen soil surface runoff;
compound hazards; infiltration barriers; debris flows; hydrological
extremes; climate change; freeze–thaw processes; monitoring and
modeling; risk governance.

Introduction
Mountain regions are increasingly exposed to hydrological
extremes caused by climate change. These include water-

floods, and landslides, as well as dry extremes such as
droughts and wildfires (IPCC 2023). Two types of
compound events have gained prominence due to their
challenging nature, increasing relevance under climate
change, and comparatively limited investigation in
European mountain regions. (1) Postfire surface runoff
(PFSR) is triggered when intense rainfall hits fire-altered
soils with reduced water infiltration capacity and
increased erodibility, generating sediment-laden floods or
debris flows ( Santi and Rengers 2022 ; Gartner et al 2024 ;
McGuire et al 2024 ). (2) Frozen soil surface runoff (FSSR)
can occur when seasonally frozen or snow-covered soils
inhibit infiltration, leading to surface ponding and
overland flow ( Bardou and Delaloye 2004 ; Baselt and
Heinze 2021 ).
Although PFSR and FSSR are typically studied as
separate processes and may appear unrelated at first glance,
both are controlled by similar boundary conditions. In
general, they are not directly linked in space and time, and
typically occur in different seasonal contexts. However, they
may affect the same regions sequentially, for example, when
burned catchments are subsequently exposed to freezing
conditions. Their increasing importance highlights the
value of analyzing the mechanisms they share within an
integrated framework. Monitoring, modeling, and
management strategies rarely account for possible shared
features or synergies, leaving potential for knowledge
transfer and joint countermeasures unused. A critical
research gap is the absence of a synthesis that systematically
examines these commonalities, with the dual aim of
advancing process understanding and informing the
development of cross-applicable strategies for impact
assessment and risk mitigation.
This article highlights that PFSR and FSSR share
predisposing factors and triggers leading to comparable
process chains. These similarities call for common
experimental, modeling, and governance approaches. We
propose an interdisciplinary framework to identify shared
mechanisms and compound hazard scenarios in rainy
mountain regions characterized by steep and fire- or freeze-
prone landscapes, with specific reference to the European
context. Such compound scenarios may also include
situations where both processes affect the same regions or

The literature considered for this article is mainly derived

Wildfire, postfire surface runoff, and debris flow
Wildfires have increased globally in frequency and severity,
particularly in mountain regions (M€uller et al 2020; Liu et al
2024; Selander et al 2025). Europe ranks among the most fire-
prone areas worldwide (Gr€unig et al 2026), due to declining
agricultural activity and rising fuel loads (Moreira et al 2011),
more frequent droughts and heatwaves (Elia et al 2024), and a
sharp increase in fire season length (Wu et al 2015; Bowman
et al 2020; Dupuy et al 2020; Sayedi et al 2024). Mountain
regions thus present extensive landscapes prone to both
wildfires and postfire processes that depend, in turn, on a
variety of predisposing factors ( Melzner et al 2025 ). In
steep mountain environments, wildfire disturbances often
initiate a cascade of hydrological and geomorphic
responses. Vegetation loss, soil heating, and ash deposition
can reduce infiltration capacity and surface protection
cover, promoting rapid surface runoff during intense
rainfall. This runoff may subsequently mobilize sediment,
trigger landslides, and provide the material for torrential
debris flows. PFSR develops when rainfall impacts burned
hillslopes, initiating erosion and sometimes mass
movements ( Figure 1 ), even on moderate slopes or small
scars (eg Cannon and Gartner 2005 ; Cannon and DeGraff
2008; Abbate et al 2019 ; Ebel and Moody 2020 ; Raymond
et al 2020 ; Sarro et al 2021 ; Gartner et al 2024 ; Jacobson
et al 2024 ; Zhou et al 2025 ).
In Europe, intense postfire erosion has been
documented along burned hillslopes with reduced soil
cohesion, root strength, and hydraulic conductivity ( Peduto
et al 2022 ; La Porta et al 2025 ). Morphometric studies of
burned watersheds prone to postfire soil erosion and debris
flows show steep relief and short concentration times
(,10 min), explaining rapid sediment responses ( Esposito
et al 2019 ). Fire-prone mountain regions often coincide
with catchments that are already predisposed to rapid
runoff generation and debris flow initiation ( Ebel and
Moody 2020). Steep slopes, shallow soils, short
concentration times, and abundant loose sediment create
geomorphic conditions that favor rapid hydrological
response to intense rainfall. When a wildfire removes
vegetation and alters soil hydraulic properties, these
preexisting factors can significantly amplify postfire runoff
and debris flow hazards. Generally, the sediment cascade is
driven by wildfires that alter soil properties controlling
infiltration and runoff: hydrophobic layers ( /C245 cm) due to
ash or fine particle sealing ( Tang et al 2019
), hyperdry
conditions (Moody and Ebel 2012 ), and mineralogical
changes (Esposito et al 2017 ) may contribute to reduced
infiltration (Gorr et al 2024 ). Ash can either clog pores and
increase runoff ( Onda et al 2008 ) or, depending on its
properties, promote infiltration and reduce splash erosion
(Woods and Balfour 2008 ; Melzner et al 2019 ). When
hillslopes are impacted by intense rainfall shortly after or in
the months following wildfires, runoff-driven erosion
progressively entrains sediment, with flows often bulking
into hyperconcentrated flows or debris flows downstream
( Jordan 2016; Santi and Rengers 2022 ; Gorr et al 2024 ).
Laboratory tests simulating burned soil conditions show
that the combustion of organic matter can reduce the yield
stress of fire-burned debris mixtures by up to 100% and
viscosity by up to 80%, increasing debris flow mobility and
providing critical rheological parameters for hazard models
(Gao et al 2025 ). While triggering rainfall is usually short,
subhourly intensities are often decisive. In southern Italy,
the peak intensity of triggering bursts within 10 and 30 min
ranges between 30–138 mm/h and 19–105 mm/h,
respectively, with modest return periods (1–3 years) showing
that such events are not extreme ( Esposito et al 2023 ).
While postfire process cascades are well known and well
studied in the United States, the Mediterranean region,
and Australia, systematic documentation of postfire
geomorphic processes remains limited in the European
Alps ( Melzner et al 2025 ). Inventories of slope instabilities
vary in completeness and thematic focus, directly
influencing hazard and risk assessments ( Guzzetti et al
2012; Steger et al 2016 , 2017; Melzner et al 2020 , 2023). In
situ observations are equally challenging, as postfire debris
flows can develop within minutes, leaving only a narrow
time window for installing measurement systems after a
fire ( Kean et al 2011 )a n dh a m p e r i n gl o n g - t e r m
monitoring. Empirical studies have examined soil heating
and water repellency ( DeBano 2000 ), but temporal
dynamics of infiltration capacity, repellency, and sediment
availability remain poorly constrained, limiting model
calibration and validation ( Lopes et al 2021 ). Numerical
models range from simplified kinematic wave or Green–
Ampt schemes that simulate runoff generation and debris
flow timing with few parameters ( McGuire et al 2018 ;
Rengers et al 2019 ) to fully coupled morphodynamic
frameworks linking runoff, entrainment, and deposition
with probabilistic surrogate models for sensitivity and
uncertainty analysis ( Spiller et al 2025 ). However,
parameter transferability, spatial heterogeneity, and the
integration of long-term recovery trajectories into
predictive frameworks remain key challenges ( Lopes et al
2021).

Erosive surface runoff over frozen soils
When rain falls on frozen soil, infiltration is often sharply
reduced due to pore blockage by ice ( Burt and Williams
1976; Kane and Stein 1983 ; Iwata et al 2011 ). Consequently,
precipitation or snowmelt contributes directly to FSSR
(Figure 2), which can rapidly erode sediment and trigger
slope failure. These effects are particularly relevant during
transitional seasons, when freeze–thaw cycles and
intermittent snow cover alter surface hydrology ( Bardou
and Delaloye 2004 ; Ala-Aho et al 2021 ).
The dominant hydrological response in a catchment
depends on thermal-hydraulic conditions, antecedent soil
moisture, and structural soil properties such as porosity,
hydraulic conductivity, and pore connectivity (Baselt and
Heinze 2021). In seasonally frozen soils, the frost-affected
zone is typically limited to the upper 40 cm, where subzero
temperatures prevail, while deeper soil layers remain above
08C and gradually warm with depth to 8–108C( Iwata et al
2011; Ala-Aho et al 2021). This vertical temperature gradient,
governed by surface energy exchange and thermal inertia,
creates a transient freeze–thaw interface that controls
infiltration during winter and early spring precipitation
(Demand et al 2019; Stuurop et al 2022). Open macropores
such as cracks, root channels, or wormholes can disrupt this
interface, allowing air, vapor, and water to bypass the frozen
matrix (St€ahli and Stadler 1997; Beven and Germann 2013).
Vertical soil column experiments have demonstrated
that, under freezing conditions, infiltration is restricted to
preferential pathways along unfrozen macropores, while
matrix flow is significantly impeded ( Stadler et al 2000 ;
Weigert and Schmidt 2005 ; Watanabe and Kugisaki 2017 ;
Grant et al 2019 ; Holten et al 2019 ; Pittman et al 2020 ).

Field observations confirm the hydrological significance
of frozen soils. During rain events, infiltration occurs
rapidly and unevenly through preferential pathways
(Conway and Benedict 1994 ), while basal ice layers can
impede infiltration and trigger FSSR ( St€ahli et al 2004 ).
Snowpacks act as dynamic vadose zone extensions where
preferential flow governs infiltration timing ( Webb et al
2018). Although macropore-driven infiltration under frozen
conditions is documented ( St€ahli et al 1999 ; Webb et al
2018; Fang et al 2024 ), boundary conditions, such as
antecedent moisture, freezing depth, and rainfall intensity,
remain uncertain, and spatial macropore heterogeneity
complicates understanding of thermohydraulic processes.
Although coupled thermohydraulic processes are
hydrogeologically important, most physical models still
assume homogeneous pore structures and local thermal
equilibrium, overlooking preferential macropore flow.
Hence, their applicability to seasonally frozen soils and
thaw-induced slope failure is limited ( Heinze 2021). Existing
freeze–thaw and permafrost schemes rarely resolve
preferential flow under partially frozen conditions
(Mohammed et al 2018 ). Richards-based approaches further
assume matrix-dominated infiltration and thermal
equilibrium, causing errors under steep thermal gradients
or heterogeneous flow ( Hansson et al 2004 ; Kurylyk et al
2014; Grenier et al 2018 ). Recent dual-permeability and
local thermal nonequilibrium (LTNE) models ( Larsbo et al
2019; Heinze and Bl €ocher 2019; Heinze 2025; Khanahmadi
et al 2026 ) improve realism but remain weakly validated.
Process similarities and divergences
PFSR and FSSR occur in different thermal and seasonal
contexts and are not directly related. However, they share
key predisposing factors and mechanisms, 8 of which are
introduced below ( Figure 3).
1. Meteorological triggers exert strong control over the
often abrupt initiation of PFSR and FSSR. Short, high-
intensity rainfall or rapid transient thaw events can
initiate sudden runoﬀ. Limited storage capacity and short
concentration times promote ﬂow responses that may
lead to sudden erosion and sediment entrainment in
both systems (Haslinger et al 2025 ).
2. Interception is reduced in both systems. After wildﬁres,
canopy and litter removal expose the soil to
precipitation, while, in frozen soil environments,
deciduous canopies are seasonally absent. In both cases, a
larger fraction of rainfall reaches the ground, increasing
the kinetic energy imparted to the surface.
3. A near-surface, low-permeability horizon limits
inﬁltration. It is typically about 5–10 cm deep. The nature
of this inﬁltration barrier diﬀers between systems. In
burned catchments, hydrophobic layers may form or be
enhanced as chemically mediated sealing through
combustion-induced changes in organic or mineral
compounds (Doerr et al 2000 ). In frozen soil, ice within
pore spaces creates a low-permeability barrier.
4. Surface water ponding above these inﬁltration barriers ,
in combination with steep terrain and reduced surface
roughness, increases surface runoﬀ. Usually, these early

5. Soil and rock properties, and preferential pathways such
as cracks and other macropores inﬂuence inﬁltration and

variably aﬀect inﬁltration, depending on burn severity

clogging and surface crusting that inhibit inﬁltration
(Onda et al 2008 ) to temporary water retention by black
char, which can prevent sealing and reduce splash
erosion (Woods and Balfour 2008 ; Wittenberg 2013;
Shtober-Zisu et al 2018 ). Additionally, surface cracks
formed during burning may locally enhance percolation.
In frozen soils, inﬁltration is progressively restored
during thaw or along macropore networks that transmit
water and heat downward, locally accelerating thaw and
altering runoﬀ thresholds ( Watanabe and Kugisaki 2017 ;
Bauer et al 2026 ).
6. Sediment mobilization, sediment transport, and slope
failure are involved in both systems and can lead to
multiphase mass ﬂows, often evolving as cascading
sediment transfer processes across hillslopes and
channel networks (eg Murphy et al 2019 ). Burned
hillslopes supply abundant sediments of various grain
sizes through vegetation loss and ash redistribution,
often resulting in a wide spectrum of mobilizable
material from ﬁne ash to coarse debris. Through rapid
PFSR entrainment, these sediments may eventually
contribute to debris ﬂow formation ( Melzner et al 2025 ),
typically dominated by runoﬀ-driven erosion and
entrainment processes. In frozen soil environments,
shallow saturation over icy layers and thaw-weakening
increase pore pressure and reduce shear strength,
whereas thawing surface horizons may detach and move
downslope along frozen–unfrozen interfaces, promoting
shallow sliding ( Bardou and Delaloye 2004 )a n d
reﬂecting diﬀerent failure kinematics compared to
postﬁre conditions. In cases of larger-scale slope
failures, a similarly wide range of sediment grain sizes
may be mobilized.
7. Expected seasonal regimes increasingly deviate in both
processes, with timing and persistence diverging. PFSR
is often triggered by the ﬁrst high-intensity storms
following ﬁre and may occur year-round, depending on
burn severity, rainfall duration and intensity, and site
recovery ( Melzner et al 2022 ). Approximately 87% of
postﬁre debris ﬂow events occur within the ﬁrst 2 years
after a ﬁre. McGuire et al (2024) further show that more
than 80% of runoﬀ-generated postﬁre debris ﬂow
events occur in the ﬁrst year, whereas roughly 82% of
landslide-generated events occur more than 1 year after
a ﬁre. Wildﬁre, thus, can reduce protection for months
to years ( Melzner et al 2019 ; Esposito et al 2023 ). In
contrast, FSSR tends to cluster in transitional seasons
only, with event likelihood depending on snowpack,
antecedent moisture, and freeze–thaw cycles ( Pittman
et al 2020 ).
8. Climate change is expected to increase the magnitude
and frequency of PFSR and FSSR. Rising temperatures,
longer ﬁre seasons, more frequent heatwaves and
droughts, as well as altered snow regimes, shallower
seasonal freezing, and more frequent freeze–thaw cycles
collectively expand the windows of occurrence and
intensify triggering conditions (IPCC 2023).

Future research agenda
The proposed research agenda builds on the findings and
experiences gained from recent and ongoing research
projects addressing postfire and frozen soil hydrological
processes. These projects provided empirical insights and
conceptual advances that form the basis for identifying
shared mechanisms, methodological needs, and cross-
disciplinary opportunities. Building on this, the following
sections outline priority areas for future work in monitoring,
experimentation, modeling, and risk governance to improve
understanding and management of compound runoff-related
hazards in mountain regions.
Monitoring
Improving understanding of runoff-related hazards in
mountain environments requires monitoring strategies
capable of capturing the dynamics of surface runoff in both
postfire and frozen soil settings. Important steps in the
development of a monitoring strategy include defining a
catalog of field evidence for burn severity indicators related
to vegetation, soil, and rock ( Melzner et al 2025 ) and a
catalog of methods to determine burn severity. Remote
sensing, in situ instrumentation, and geophysical
approaches provide complementary perspectives, but each
comes with specific advantages, limitations, and quality
requirements. Airborne Laser Scanning (ALS), Terrestrial
Laser Scanning (TLS), Unmanned Aerial Vehicle Light
Detection and Ranging (UAV LiDAR), and
photogrammetric surveys are powerful tools to detect
sediment sources, deposition zones, and geomorphic and
vegetation changes. Their applicability has been
demonstrated in postfire contexts ( Rengers et al 2016 ), as
well as in frozen soil and snow settings ( Deschamps-Berger
et al 2025 ). In the wide spectrum of remote sensing
technologies, synthetic aperture radar (SAR) systems are
also useful for burn severity mapping ( Tanase et al 2015 )
and for soil moisture and surface roughness investigations
to assess postfire hydrological response or snow and frozen
ground conditions.
LiDAR penetrates vegetation and records multiple
attributes per pulse, whereas photogrammetry reconstructs
detailed 3D surfaces from overlapping UAV or terrestrial
images (Melzner and Schiller 2022). Technique selection
depends on terrain: TLS for steep faces, UAV LiDAR for
mixed slopes, and ALS for large-scale coverage. In frozen soil
contexts, these tools document snowpack, frost, and
microtopographic changes during freeze–thaw cycles (Jadoon
et al 2015; Nakata et al 2021; Cho et al 2025). Satellite
multispectral data from Landsat and Sentinel complement
such high-resolution datasets by providing consistent, long-
term regional observations, enabling analyses of burn severity,
vegetation recovery, soil moisture, and snow dynamics across
mountain ranges (Wulder et al 2022; Lewi/C19nska et al 2024;
Melzner et al 2025).
Continuous in situ monitoring is essential to observe
triggering conditions and event dynamics in real time. In
both burned and frozen environments, soil water content
(SWC), measured at different depths, will reveal infiltration
behavior during rainfall on burned slopes and during rain or
melt on frozen soils. In addition to traditional time domain
reflectometry (TDR) and frequency domain reflectometry
(FDR) probes and capacitance-based or, more generally,
dielectric soil moisture sensors (Watanabe and Kugisaki 2017;
Bauer et al 2026; Salmabadi et al 2026), novel methods such
as cosmic-ray neutron sensing extend the observation scale to
several hundred meters and depths up to /C2480 cm (Zreda et al
2008). Coupling SWC probes with tensiometers may provide
information on matric suction, critical for both shallow slope
failures in burned terrain and for thaw weakening in frozen
soils (Rengers et al 2020). Seismic monitoring has proven
effective in detecting debris flow surges (Porter et al 2023)
and could similarly be applied to identify erosive runoff
pulses in frozen soil settings. Optical and thermal cameras,
pressure transducers in channels, and stage gauges offer
further opportunities to characterize the timing, type, and
magnitude of runoff processes (Kean et al 2011; Dematteis
et al 2025).
Data quality must be defined through method-specific
criteria. For LiDAR, these include point density, coverage,
classification accuracy, and acquisition timing ( Melzner and
Schiller 2022). In photogrammetry, positional and shape
accuracy and image resolution determine the detection of
fine-scale features such as macroscopic soil sealing, ash
layers, or thaw-induced cracks ( Melzner et al 2019 ; Melzner
and Schiller 2020). High-resolution data enhance the
capture of small-scale variability in burned and frozen soils
but increase computational effort. Integrating multiscale
remote sensing with continuous in situ monitoring helps to
resolve spatial heterogeneity and temporal dynamics. The
main challenge is to develop efficient, scalable, and
affordable strategies that yield long-term, high-quality
datasets to advance physical models and early warning
systems for catchments affected by both fire and frost.
Experimental studies
Future advances in understanding runoff-related hazards will
depend on targeted and collaborative experimental
approaches, combining controlled laboratory studies with
advanced field measurement stations to capture process
dynamics across scales. Such investigations are essential for
disentangling coupled hydrological and thermal processes,
for systematically testing hypotheses of infiltration and
runoff generation, and for generating benchmark datasets to
calibrate and validate numerical models. In this context, plot-
to hillslope-scale rainfall simulation experiments (egLechner
et al 2026) provide a critical bridge between controlled
laboratory conditions and catchment-scale responses, as they
enable the quantification of scale-dependent runoff
generation processes under realistic boundary conditions. A
primary avenue of research concerns the hydrological
implications of water repellency and infiltration into
hydrophobic soil layers. Initial investigations may employ
simple 1-dimensional soil column experiments, extending
earlier foundational studies (DeBano et al 1979; DeBano
2000). Subsequent efforts should advance toward 2- and 3-
dimensional laboratory setups capable of lateral subsurface
flow, evolving heterogeneities, and preferential flow
pathways. Designing such systems is technically demanding,
as both boundary conditions and evolving variables,
including soil moisture, hydraulic conductivity, repellency
strength, and pore structure, must be measured with high
spatial and temporal resolution. Another promising
direction is surface runoff experiments with burned soils and

slope instability.
For frozen soil systems, new experimental research is
required to resolve key uncertainties in freezing front
propagation, macropore behavior, and infiltration–runoff
partitioning under freeze–thaw dynamics. The soil specificity
of freezing behavior—mediated by grain-size distribution, fine
particles, and organic matter—remains poorly constrained.
Future laboratory programs should therefore systematically
vary soil texture, organic content, and macropore structure to
quantify their role in governing freezing progression,
infiltration capacity, and runoff initiation. A particularly
important frontier is the role of macropores under freezing
and refreezing conditions. Existing results suggest that
macropores can initially enhance infiltration but subsequently
lose continuity once pore ice develops (Bauer et al 2026). The
dynamics of these transitions, and their sensitivity to initial
moisture content and freeze–thaw sequences, remain
insufficiently quantified. Experiments combining rainfall–
freeze–thaw cycles, snowmelt simulations, and advanced
imaging (eg nuclear magnetic resonance, electrical
capacitance tomography) could provide critical process-level
insights. Future experiments should also address compound
boundary conditions, including repeated rainfall events on
frozen soils, rain on snow scenarios, and the combined effects
of fire-induced hydrophobicity and subsequent frost. These
compound experiments are crucial to reproducing realistic
mountain settings where burned soil may later be exposed to
seasonal freezing, or where thawing coincides with rainfall.
Modeling integration
Across postfire and frozen soil settings, laboratory
experiments and field surveys provide essential testbeds for
next-generation physical models. Particularly promising are
dual-domain and local thermal nonequilibrium approaches,
which explicitly represent preferential pathways, heat
transfer, and evolving pore connectivity ( Heinze 2021;
Khanahmadi et al 2026 ). Beyond traditional single-domain
hydrological frameworks, recent advances in multiphase
flow modeling now incorporate thermal processes, ice–
water phase transitions, and the resulting feedback on
frictional resistance and mobility ( Pudasaini and
Krautblatter 2014; Feng et al 2023 ; Munch et al 2024 ;
Pudasaini et al 2024 ). These developments are critical for
hazard assessment, as they enable the simulation of complex
rheological behavior of flows enriched with snow, ice, or
ash. Nevertheless, benchmark datasets derived from
controlled experiments and field surveys are indispensable
for systematic model calibration, validation, and
parameterization (Lopes et al 2021 ). The Bussoleno case
study in the northwestern Italian Alps illustrates the added
value of coupling susceptibility-based triggering analysis
with physical runout models ( La Porta et al 2025 ). This
underlines that physical models need to account not only
for runout but also for temporally and spatially distributed
triggering conditions linked to vegetation loss, infiltration
reduction, and altered soil strength ( Gorr et al 2024 ).
Further improvements in physical models are required
to incorporate mechanical aspects relevant for hazard
planning, protection structures, and soft measures, such as
bioengineering. A central challenge lies in developing fully
coupled hydrological–geomorphic–hydraulic catchment
models that account for temporal and spatial changes in
land cover, porosity, permeability, and temperature to
realistically simulate infiltration, groundwater recharge, and
runoff. Findings from hydrogeology underline the necessity
of linking runoff, groundwater, and water quality to secure
sustainable drinking water supply in mountain regions.
Risk governance
Effective management of postfire and frost-related hazards
requires timely and coordinated measures to reduce risks
for downstream urban settlements. Population safety should
rely on multilayered warning systems that combine local
weather alerts with advanced forecasting and monitoring
tools. As demonstrated by fatal postfire debris flows in the
United States ( Kean et al 2019 ) and Italy ( Esposito and
Gariano 2025), or by rapid runoff events on frozen ground
in Switzerland and Japan ( Bardou and Delaloye 2004 ; Iwata
et al 2011 ), local weather alerts can be insufficient to warn
the population efficiently, and more action is thus required.
Early warning systems based solely on the exceedance of
rainfall thresholds can also be ineffective, since exceedance
may occur only minutes before slope or runoff processes are
triggered, providing insufficient lead time to manage
evacuations or road closures. Early warning protocols
should therefore be based not only on rainfall threshold
exceedance but also on predictive models that incorporate
lead time. In this context, impact-based warning (IbW)
approaches are increasingly promoted, shifting the focus
from forecasting hazard variables to communicating
expected impacts on people, infrastructure, and services.
Such frameworks aim to translate forecasts into actionable
information by linking hazard intensity with exposure and
vulnerability (Potter et al 2018 , 2021; R€ozer et al 2021 ).
However, most existing IbW systems are focused on single
hazards without considering cascading and compound
effects, highlighting the need for the development of
multihazard approaches in mountain environments
(Lumbroso et al 2025 ).
Weather radar should be systematically employed to
monitor rainfall conditions both upwind of vulnerable areas
and in locations not covered by rain gauges. Tracking and
forecasting the spatial and temporal evolution of convective
systems capable of producing intense rainfall must
therefore be prioritized. This applies equally to postfire
hillslopes with reduced infiltration capacity and to frozen
soil catchments where impermeable near-surface ice layers
favor rapid runoff generation. Rainfall forecasting models
should be coupled with radar and in situ observations to
improve nowcasting activities and, consequently, emergency
response preparation. Many national meteorological
services already operationalize key elements such as radar–
gauge merging and radar-based nowcasting. However, full

for anticipating compound events, which increasingly blur
traditional seasonal distinctions and challenge conventional
hazard management frameworks in alpine environments.
Implications and conclusions
This article highlights both the convergences and
divergences of runoff generation in postfire and frozen soil
environments. Both systems exhibit reduced interception,
constrained infiltration, rapid surface runoff, and efficient
sediment mobilization, underscoring the value of cross-
fertilization in monitoring, experimentation, and modeling.
At the same time, divergences in the origin of infiltration
barriers, seasonal timing, sediment grain size, and failure
kinematics highlight a need for caution in using simple
analogies and point to the need for process-specific
parameterizations. Beyond direct impacts on infrastructure
and human safety, these processes can also affect vegetation
cover and recovery, soil development, and sediment
dynamics in aquatic environments, thereby influencing
ecosystem functions in mountain regions.
Future progress will rely on systematically addressing the
open questions that arise from the convergences and
divergences identified in this article. Building on these shared
mechanisms and contrasting features, we have outlined a
comprehensive research agenda that spans monitoring,
experimental investigations, modeling integration, and risk
governance. This agenda highlights that advancing process
understanding and hazard management in postfire and frozen
soil systems in the high mountains of the Alps is possible only
through coordinated and interdisciplinary effort, as shared
physical mechanisms enable knowledge exchange and joint
methodological development across domains.
Advancing this agenda ultimately depends on
collaborative and interdisciplinary research frameworks.
Future experimental studies demand joint expertise from
cryosphere science, geomorphology, wildfire research, and
alpine natural hazard studies, combined with soil physics,
hydrology, and engineering to design large-scale,
multiprocess laboratory frameworks. Modeling progress
requires equally integrative approaches, where empirical
benchmark datasets are embedded in international
collaborations. Likewise, monitoring strategies must
combine remote sensing, in situ instrumentation, and
geophysical methods within multi-institutional networks to
ensure robust long-term datasets across mountain ranges.
Finally, risk governance calls for cooperation beyond
science, linking geoscientists, engineers, practitioners, and
policymakers to develop effective early warning systems and
to implement sustainable, nature-based protection
strategies. Taken together, these collaborative efforts are
not only critical for advancing process understanding but
also for aligning hazard research with the values of
sustainable mountain development.
