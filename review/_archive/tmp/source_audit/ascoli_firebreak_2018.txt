Deﬁnition
Firebreak and fuelbreak are fuel-managed areas
meant to stop or hamper ﬁre propagation, respec-
tively. Firebreaks are parcels of land, linear in
shape, where fuels are totally removed down to
mineral soil through a combination of physical
treatments (thinning, mechanical clearing, pre-
scribed burning, slashing, mastication, mowing,
plowing). Fuelbreaks are parcels of land, linear
or in blocks, covered by vegetation where fuels
are reduced in both volume and ﬂammability
by physical treatments and land management
practices such as grazing, game management, or
silvicultural treatments favoring less-ﬂammable
vegetation, so that ﬁres burning into them can
be controlled more effectively and safely. In the
event of ﬁre, both ﬁrebreaks and fuelbreaks may
serve as a control line from which to carry out
suppression operations.
Introduction
The mitigation of negative effects of wildﬁres
on ecosystem services and human assets can be
achieved by reducing the ﬁre rate of spread and
the intensity of the ﬁre front. As wildﬁres spread
through a landscape where vegetation is available
for combustion, a key strategy to reduce ﬁre
intensity, ﬁre perimeter growth, and mitigate the
ﬁre severity is to limit the biomass available for
combustion by the management of vegetation fu-
els in speciﬁc areas (Agee et al.2000). Firebreaks
and fuelbreaks are parcels of land, linear or in
blocks, where the ﬂammability of fuels is reduced
by total or partial removal through physical treat-
ments (thinning, mechanical clearing, prescribed
burning, slashing, mowing, etc.) or land manage-
ment practices (grazing, game management, use
of alternative crops or favoring less-ﬂammable

Firebreaks and fuelbreaks can be distinguished in

Linear Firebreaks and Fuelbreaks
Linear ﬁrebreaks, fuelbreaks, and shaded fuel-
breaks are terms referring to fuel treated areas
with different characteristics (Fig. 1). Linear ﬁre-
breaks are strips of land cleared to the mineral
soil, while fuelbreaks maintain the herbaceous
and shrub vegetation, and a shaded fuelbreak
maintains the tree cover. Linear fuelbreaks and
shaded fuelbreaks are more suited in mountain-
ous territories (i.e., steep slopes and rough mor-
phology) where the maintenance of the vegeta-
tion cover is advantageous to limit soil erosion.
Linear ﬁrebreaks and fuelbreaks are classiﬁed
according to their decreasing width in primary,
secondary, and tertiary (Fig. 2) with different
objectives, technical speciﬁcations, and manage-
ment approaches for each type. Recommenda-
tions regarding width are variable across geo-
graphical areas and related ﬁre management sys-
tem. For the primary ones, minimum width rec-
ommendations range from 30 to 100 m. Both sec-
ondary and tertiary networks are recommended
to range from 3 m to more than 50 m wide,
depending on the geographical areas and speciﬁc
situations. In territories where the wildland-urban
interface is widespread, the implementation of
large and long ﬁrebreaks and fuelbreaks is less
suited, as well as in areas where the forest prop-
erty is mainly private and cadastral land parcels
are small. The width can be deﬁned on local
expertise (Agee et al. 2000; Bahro et al. 2007),
by Forest Fire Management Plans or by a fuel
modeling system and a ﬁre behavior prediction
software (see the following section of modeling
tools). The width of a given fuel discontinuity
should at least prevent any material located at
the other side of the strip from being ignited due
to the heat received through radiation (Agee et
al. 2000). As it relates to shape, some of the
primary fuelbreaks are aimed at being established
as a network creating landscape wide treatments
of 500 to 10,000 ha, depending on the region.
The others are established as strips or as buffers
surrounding, e.g., valuable land or assets.
The primary and secondary network aims at
facilitating ﬁre suppression (i.e., making it safer
and more effective), promoting the passive pro-
tection of assets and fastening the suppression
of starting ﬁres, whereas the tertiary network
only aims at this latter objective. Other objec-
tives include (i) back-burning (burning out ﬁeld
ahead under safe conditions) to stop wildﬁres
and decreasing ﬁre rate of spread; (ii) modifying
ﬁre severity, minimize erosion, and water qual-
ity/ﬂow problems; and (iii) protecting valuable
assets.
Guidelines for the location of ﬁrebreaks and
fuelbreaks are based on a wide range of cri-
teria. Overall, the expected ﬁre environments,
i.e., fuels (land cover), terrain (topography), and
ﬁre weather, are taken into account for deﬁn-
ing locations. Rivers, croplands, and grasslands
and previously burned areas can be suitable to
connect both ﬁrebreaks and fuelbreaks. In very
steep terrain, ridgelines or valley bottoms are
preferred locations for implementation. Other cri-
teria for selecting location include maintenance
costs, accessibility (along roads), and ﬁre ﬁghter
safety. Knowledge of predominant winds and
history of past large ﬁres should also be taken into
account (Fig. 3). Indeed, the spatial design of low
combustibility linear ﬁrebreaks and fuelbreaks is
based on anticipating the type of large ﬁres that
may affect a speciﬁc landscape, by identifying
the location of the strategic management points
in which fuel management actions would limit
ﬁre propagation (Agee et al. 2000). Based on
the study of historical large ﬁres in the area and
the analysis of the synoptic weather conditions
associated with each of those ﬁres, the most
frequent ﬁre situations are grouped in typology
of ﬁres, i.e., of ﬁre propagation patterns (Du-
ane et al. 2015). Fire behavior modeling can
support ﬁrebreak and fuelbreak location (see the
following section of modeling tools). Standards
for design require continuous fuel management
between two anchor points of low ﬂammability

Area-Wide Fuelbreaks
Area-wide fuelbreaks consist in fuel management
taking place over large land areas. They can be
implemented using (a) preventive silviculture in
forested areas, (b) prescribed burning or graz-
ing, (c) implementing or maintaining annual and
permanent crops. The speciﬁcations regarding
fuelbreaks size are quite variable (1 to 5000 ha),
according to the geographical region. Area-wide
treatment objectives are decreasing ﬁre growth
and intensity, spotting probability and reducing
ignitions, and increasing forest resistance and
resilience to ﬁre (Fig. 4).
Preventive silviculture is usually integrated
within broadest forest management objectives
such as increasing soil protection and the me-
chanical stability of forests or changing even aged
forest stands, such as ﬂammable pine plantations,
to multispeciﬁc uneven aged forests by favoring
natural successions and biodiversity (Moreira
et al. 2011). Recommended locations include
areas with higher ﬁre risk derived from fuel load
maps, history of past ﬁres (mainly ﬁre paths
and predominant wind direction), topography,
climatic regions, proximity to ignition sources,
and simulation models of ﬁre spread (see the
following section of modeling tools). Usually,
area-wide treatments for stand self-protection
are restricted to forest areas of high patrimonial

As for the type of management, prescribed
burning is a common fuel treatment practice in
many geographical areas, although in the proxim-
ity of urban areas its implementation has higher
costs, an increased risk, and greater concern for
smoke. This is carried mostly through ground
and, in more remote areas, by aerial burning. In
some cases, the burning of silvicultural residues
following harvesting is implemented. Manual and
mechanical treatments are also common, particu-
larly in areas that have good accessibility. Treat-
ments include thinning, pruning, slash removal,
conversion from coppice to high forest stands,
and implementation of strips of vegetation that
have low combustibility, e.g., hardwood or de-
ciduous species. Often techniques are integrated
(Fig. 5) and mechanical treatments followed by
prescribed burning are considered the most ef-
fective in reducing forest ﬂammability (McIver
et al. 2012). Grazing management in fuelbreaks
is mostly restricted to areas adjacent to linear
fuelbreaks. The use of biomass for bioenergy is
increasingly used as an additional objective for
fuel removal.
Fuelbreaks at the Wildland-Urban
Interface
Fuelbreaks at the wildland-urban interface con-
sist in changes to fuel characteristics in parcels
adjacent to homes and communities so as to
reduce wildﬁre risk and create safe zones for
ﬁreﬁghting (Rigolot et al. 2004; Radeloff et al.
2005).

Modeling Tools to Assist the Design
of Firebreaks and Fuelbreaks
Fire propagation simulation software implement
“ﬁrebreak” and “fuelbreaks” to test the effect
of fuel reduction areas on the ﬁre growth and
intensity. These tools enable ﬁre behavior (rate
of spread, ﬂame length and ﬁreline intensity),
radiation and convection functioning, and
spot ﬁre occurrence estimation for speciﬁc
meteorological, fuel, and topographic conditions
(e.g., Finney 2006; Sullivan 2009; Vacchiano and
Ascoli 2015). The data obtained from these
scenarios (average and extremes) are used to
estimate the ﬁrebreak width needed to stop the
spread of ﬁre by radiation. In fuelbreaks and
shaded fuelbreaks, simulation tools are used
to estimate the minimum amount of surface
fuels allowed to reduce the ﬁre intensity to a
desired level for effective ﬁre suppression by ﬁre
brigades.

Practical evidence shows that software tools
can be successfully used in testing fuel treatments
(Stephens 1998), thus acting as decision support
systems, i.e., when evaluating the most effective
ﬁrebreaks (i.e., barrier) for a particular location
(Mansourian et al.2005). For example, a study by
Duguy et al. (2007) in the Spanish Mediterranean
using Farsite suggested that ﬁrebreaks could con-
tribute to reducing the ﬁre size. Fire behavior
modeling can support ﬁrebreaks and fuelbreaks
location. A ﬁre modeling-based approach allows
to generate scenarios of ﬁre growth (major ﬁre
paths the ﬁre front probably will follow) in a
given vegetation, meteorological, and topograph-
ical scenario. The data obtained from these simu-
lations can be used to locate the fuel discontinu-
ities that need to be introduced in the landscape
in order to minimize the propagation of potential
ﬁres. However, in many simulators, the position,
the length, and the depth of the ﬁrebreaks are
deﬁned by the user; thus they cannot be auto-
matically optimized. This represents a serious
limitation for the deployment of these systems in
designing and managing wildﬁres. Indeed, while
the simulation approach is very useful to decide
if a particular choice of the distribution of ﬁre-
breaks and fuelbreaks is effective or not, alone it
does not give any a priori information about what
is the best distribution which reduces the hazard
risk of forest ﬁre.
Recent studies focused on the mathematical
assessment of spatial distribution of ﬁrebreaks
and fuelbreaks in a landscape (e.g., selective
harvesting or prescribed burning) for the man-
agement of wildland ﬁres (Palma et al. 2007).
In particular, a number of optimization mod-
els have been proposed for spatial allocation
of fuel treatments (Hof et al. 2000; Wei et al.
2008; Rytwinski and Crowe 2010;W e i 2012).
Optimization modeling strategies, based on op-
erational research approach (Minas et al. 2012;
Minas et al. 2013), typically require thousands
of objective function calculations; thus the so-
lution of the optimization problem is in general
highly computationally demanding (Hillier and
Lieberman 1990). Moreover, the introduction of
oversimpliﬁcations to reduce the computational
cost poses strong doubts on realistic utility (Ry-
twinski and Crowe 2010). On the other hand,
simulation-optimization approaches (Minas and
Hearne 2016; Martell 2007) have been applied
for ﬁnding the best allocation of ﬁrebreaks and
fuelbreaks with, for example, the objective of
minimizing ﬁre-risk subject to a limit on the total
area to be used as fuelbreaks. Although the results
presented in Rytwinski and Crowe (2010) suggest

that simulation-optimization is a robust approach

Very recently, based on complex network
theory, it has been proposed a computational
methodology that addresses the spatial distribu-
tion of ﬁrebreaks for the inhibition of the spread
and size of wildland ﬁres on heterogeneous
landscapes (Evaggelidis et al. 2015; Russo
et al. 2015, 2016). This is a two-tier approach
where the dynamics of ﬁre spread are modeled as
a random Markov ﬁeld process on a directed
network whose edge weights are provided
by a state-of-the-art cellular automata model
(Alexandridis et al. 2008, 2011) that integrates
detailed GIS, landscape, and meteorological data.
Within this framework, the spatial distribution of
ﬁrebreaks is reduced to the problem of ﬁnding
the network nodes among which the ﬁre spreads
faster; thus their removal favors the inhibition of
the ﬁre propagation (see Fig. 6).
