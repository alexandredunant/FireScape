Abstract 
A survey of residents in the Locarnese region of Canton Ticino, Switzerland examined perceptions of 
both increases in exposure to changing levels of environmental risk  and the deliberative Swiss 
planning system. In the context of a growing risk of wildfires in the study region, residents in the peri-
urban residential areas  of the forested south -facing slopes of the Swiss Alps  were asked a series of 
questions regarding their relationships to the planning system, place and environment, and attitudes 
to risk. Descriptive and inferential statistics revealed high appreciation of lifestyle and amenity values 
and support for firefighting services . While survey respondents did not consider that thos e risks are 
increasing with environmental change, wildfires and landslides were recognised as p osing significant 
risk. Current levels of urban expansion were viewed as excessive, heightening planning challenges at 
the interface between forests and settleme nts. Although the deliberative governance arrangement 
offers citizens with the opportunity to be involved in decision-making through official channels, many 
respondents felt their views were inadequately reflected in decisions and that key issues are not being 
addressed by the planning system. In contrast to other respondents, those who are younger, of local 
background or less educated are more dissatisfied with their influence over planning, which could 
have important implications for hazard management as new risks emerge with environmental change. 
If expectations of involvement are not met, new challenges will be presented to the distinctive Swiss 
planning system.

1. Introduction 
 
Global climate change is shifting many of the dynamic relationships between the environment and 
society.  One of the major implications of that change is the increasing likelihood of wildfires, even in 
places that have not traditionally considered themselves at high wildfire risk, such as the Swiss Alps 
(Valese et al. 2014; Conedera et al. 2018). In the alpine landscape, wildfire risk is also being 
exacerbated by factors such as reafforestation and urban development. In the Swiss Italian Canton 
of Ticino, the forested area has more than doubled in the last 100 years, to cover 52 percent of the 
canton in 2018 (Galfetti 2020). At the beginning of the 20th century, the forest cover in Ticino was 
60,000 hectares (ha), but by 1950 had grown to 85,000 ha, and by the beginning of the 21st century 
had reached 140,000 ha (USTAT 2007). This increase in forest cover has been particularly evident at 
altitudes greater than 800 metres above sea level (Bettelini 2007), where previously managed 
agrarian and pastoral landscapes have transitioned to forest (Bauer et al. 2009; Mack et al. 2013; 
USTAT 2013; Loran et al. 2017). At lower altitudes, the forests have also expanded into agricultural 
lands and are increasingly in close contact with settlements (Galfetti 2020). The positive outcomes of 
that expanded forest area are significant improvements in erosion control (Rickli and Graf 2009; 
Malek et al. 2015), and, for many people, biodiversity conservation and amenity values through the 
rewilding of landscape (Pereira 2015; Prior and Brady 2017; Frick et al. 2018). Nevertheless, the 
change has also generated different forms of environmental risk. While forests have largely been 
perceived historically as a means of mitigating the risk of natural hazards (Mather and Fairbairn 
2000), as the forest has expanded in areas close to townships, greater risks of wildfires and tree fall 
have emerged in peri-urban areas (wildland-urban interface) (Von der Dunk et al. 2011; FOEN 2014; 
Conedera et al. 2015).  
 
Concurrently, increased population growth rates in the second half of the 20th century in Canton 
Ticino, and the associated decline in availability of residential land on valley floors, have changed the 
traditional urban settlement patterns that had dominated since the Middle Ages (OST-TI 2014). In 
the Locarnese region of Ticino, this expansion has been particularly evident since the 1970s, and has 
come at the expense of terraced vineyards and grazing land that acted as buffer zones between the 
forest and the village. Traditionally, a trading centre between Switzerland and northern Italy, high-
level industrial activities and sun-seeking tourists and retirees have supported growth in the 
residential population in the Locarnese region from 21,031 in 1950 to 38,398 in 2000 (USTAT 2020). 
Since 1985, agricultural areas have diminished by 20 percent across Ticino (Galfetti 2020), and the 
loss of traditional landscapes has become a sensitive cultural issue (Rodewald et al. 2014), as

suggested by the high level of cantonal support for a proposal seeking to curb urban sprawl in 
Switzerland during a plebiscite in February 2019 and the initiative to protect cultural landscapes 
(Geiser 2019; Iniziativa Paesaggio 2020). 
 
The key research aim is to examine how the highly deliberative Swiss planning system is managing 
changes in environmental risk. As the landscape of Canton Ticino changes and residential areas are 
brought into direct contact with the expanding forest (Figure 1), this paper analyses how residents of 
the steep peri-urban areas around Locarno perceive of the environment and changing natural 
hazards in association with opportunities for appropriate planned responses. The analysis is 
particularly relevant given that the highly deliberative Swiss political system aims to generate 
planning outcomes that reflect people’s perceptions of landscape and environmental risks. The 
paper appraises the potential for participatory planning strategies to provide the community with 
opportunities to express their attitudes about changing levels of environmental risk, and thereby 
influence effective planning in the Locarnese peri-urban area.

2. Deliberative planning for risk  
 
Risk is a complex term defined as a ‘combination of the probability of an event and its negative 
consequences’ (UNISDR 2007). New levels of environmental risk associated with climate change are 
driving societies accustomed to dealing with natural hazards to acknowledge the potential limits of 
current disaster risk governance and management capabilities (Kanonier and Rudolf-Miklau 2018; 
Bardsley and Knierim 2020). This situation reflects a structural crisis identified by Beck (1986), 
whereby established modern management frameworks no longer adequately represent the 
fundamental shift that has occurred in the parameters defining risk across society. Beck’s theory 


PAGE 6
5 
 
considers the significant complexity of the current social reality and notes that first modernity 
approaches to risk management have focussed on reducing the impacts of natural disasters through 
technological advancement. Yet, with increasing risk, boundaries or thresholds are reached after 
which consequences and likelihoods continue to escalate irrespective of substantial further 
investments in physical management interventions.  
 
Beck et al. (2003) evoke a new, second or reflexive modernity, in which governance must work to 
incorporate an understanding of risk into all contemporary policy and planning. Such a socialisation 
of risk builds upon Habermas’ (1989) reflections on the need for processes or ‘social spaces’ that 
allow citizens to engage and participate in decision-making (Parkins and Mitchell 2005). Together 
these conceptions inform the fundamental premise of this paper, namely that landscape planning 
must become more reflexive to respond to the unique situations that are presented locally to 
manage environmental risk (Godschalk et al. 2003; Morehouse et al. 2011; Von der Dunk et al. 2011; 
Bardsley et al. 2015; Sharma-Wallace 2016). In recent years, there has been renewed interest by 
planners in the advantages of early and ongoing community engagement to educate and understand 
people’s knowledge, values and preferences for effective land-use management that progresses 
beyond simplistic consensus-based outcomes (Healey 1997; Forester 1999; Heberlein 2012; Gallent 
and Ciaffi 2014; Legacy 2017; Natarajan 2017). Yet, such an approach requires community 
understanding of environmental planning systems and effective engagement processes to influence 
spatial and systemic outcomes.  
 
How people perceive of risk is hugely influential over their level of support for particular planning 
outcomes. Issues of transparency and meaningful public participation and engagement are a 
developing research field, with numerous studies focussing on the mitigation of wildfire risk (Martin 
et al. 2009; Frandsen et al. 2011; McGee 2011; Eriksen 2014; Ghasemi et al. 2020). In conjunction 
with public entities, such as biodiversity conservation and emergency response agencies, local 
planning bodies have the complex role of translating government policies into tangible procedural 
and spatial outcomes aimed at minimising the risk factors associated with natural hazards, while 
supporting social values. For that reason, the social and cultural influences over perceptions and 
responses to risk are a vital component of environmental risk research (Pfister 1985; Slovic 1987; 
Pfister 2009; Xue et al. 2014; Reid and Beilin 2015). For example, research on individual resident and 
community-preparedness for wildfires has identified significant links between risk minimisation and 
residents’ place values (Anton and Lawrence 2014; Frick et al. 2018; Weber et al. 2019; Koksal et al. 
2019). The argument runs that as risks of wildfire increase, approaches to manage the forest in 

PAGE 7
6 
 
association with urban expansion will need to adjust by taking into account both the new science 
and stakeholder perceptions of risk.  
 
3. The Swiss deliberative democratic system 
 
Environmental planning relies on a range of different spatial, landscape and natural hazard planning 
instruments. The challenge lies in identifying the tools and approaches to achieve decisions that are 
both reflective of local needs and broader policy outcomes (Healey 2006). Apart from the fact that 
several of the authors are Swiss, Switzerland was chosen as a case-study area because, whilst many 
countries make use of varying levels of deliberation to engage their population in decision-making, 
no other jurisdiction has such entrenched participatory mechanisms (Steiner 2012; Hill 2013; Della 
Porta et al. 2017). Dryzek (2009) defines the deliberative capacity of a political system as the degree 
to which decisions and then outcomes truly reflect the wishes and values of the population. Both 
representative and direct democratic institutions guide the deliberative Swiss democracy (Parkinson 
2006; Pedrini 2014).  
 
The Swiss federal system entails three governance levels: federal, cantonal and municipal. Although 
the legislative framework for spatial planning has been empowered at the federal level since 1969 
(Lendi 2012), in 2012, the three levels of government collaborated to develop and ratify ‘Concept 
Switzerland 2012’, to provide nation-wide strategic planning guidelines (Swiss Federal Council 2012). 
Through the principle of municipal federalism, local authorities are granted wide-ranging powers in 
relation to land use and the issuing of building permits. While the strategic and legislative planning 
frameworks work across all levels of governance, much of the implementation is left to the 26 
Cantons and the over 2000 municipalities to develop detailed structured and land use plans (Grams 
and Nebel 2013). In practical terms, while the responsibility for transport infrastructure, economic 
policy and environmental protection fall to the federal government, governance of land use planning 
and nature conservation falls mostly to the Cantons (Muggli 2013).  Nevertheless, the Swiss federal 
government reserves the right to regulate key areas as defined in the constitution, by approving 
cantonal plans and ensuring key principles are supported, such as the separation of building and 
non-building zones.  
 
In the Swiss model, citizens have direct access to the complex decision-making system through 
referenda and popular initiatives. Partly for that reason, Switzerland has a planning system that 
enjoys a rare level of public support and respect (Lendi 2012; Muggli 2013). The direct democratic 

PAGE 8
7 
 
principles apply to all levels of government, and while the items voted on are often high-level 
legislative frameworks and planning principles, at cantonal and municipal levels citizens can directly 
influence local outcomes. The processes of participation have promoted innovative outcomes, such 
as the establishment of sustainable mountain forestry (Hersperger et al. 2017) and strict controls on 
urban sprawl (Gennaio et al. 2009; Swiss Federal Council 2012; Klaus 2021). In recent times several 
intergenerational issues have been shaped by popular interventions at the cantonal level in Ticino 
(Repubblica e Cantone Ticino 2021). In 2007, following the successful collection of signatures in the 
Canton, the direct intervention of a citizen referendum overturned a parliamentary decision to build 
a freeway leading to Locarno across the Magadino plain. In another example, citizen actions ensured 
that existing cantonal laws to safeguard natural and cultural local heritage were updated to help 
sustain traditional landscapes. 
 
The successful Swiss spatial planning system has emerged from a traditional culture accustomed to 
dealing with environmental risk, which enabled the nation-state to thrive despite the constraints of 
an extreme landscape and hazardous terrain (Bründl et al. 2009; Pfister 2009). Historically, the Swiss 
people have been characterised by a strong sense of cultural attachment to place, and of shared 
responsibility in the governance of the country (Pfister 1985; Kriesi and Trechsel 2008). While 
participatory mechanisms in the governance process aim to provide the Swiss with a deeply 
encultured understanding of risk mitigation, that assumption has rarely been tested by research on 
environmental hazards (Maidl et al. 2021). Implicit to the approach is the assumption that the 
opportunity to be heard by authorities leads to citizens feeling that they are valued as actors in the 
development of new understandings, decisions and institutional capacities.  
 
One of the specific challenges confronting the inclusive Swiss decision-making system is for citizens 
to feel that local spatial outcomes truly reflect the values of the community. If citizens feel that they 
do not have a voice in planning issues, they may feel disaffected and distrustful (Bauer et al. 2014), 
or on the other hand, simply become increasingly reliant on expert technical interventions (Turner 
2001). Recently, the Swiss government has identified a specific lack of understanding amongst the 
general population of the potential effects of increasing environmental risks (Camenzind and Loat 
2014; FOEN 2016).  While opportunities for uniquely high levels of deliberation and engagement 
exist in Switzerland, if the general population is not informed about, is unaware of or holds false 
perceptions of risk, deliberative democracy could lead to undesirable planning outcomes. Therefore, 
both the opportunities presented by citizen participation and the risks of the approach generate 
important examples for policy development in other countries as they also struggle to manage risk 

PAGE 9
8 
 
(eg. Smith 2009). Swiss federal and cantonal authorities are now acknowledging the necessity for 
improved communication and community education on the new types of risk (FOEN 2016). It is 
within such a context that we examine how residents of peri-urban Locarno perceive of 
environmental risks and their influence over planning decisions. 
 
4. Wildfire risk in the study area around Locarno  
 
The research was undertaken in the peri-urban areas of Locarno, within the municipalities of 
Locarno-Bré, Orselina, Muralto, Minusio, Brione sopra Minusio, Minusio and Ronco sopra Ascona 
(Figure 2).  
 
Figure 2: Household Survey areas (2015) within the municipalities of Locarno-Brè, Orselina, Muralto, 
Minusio, Brione sopra Minusio, Minusio and Ronco sopra Ascona  
 
 
 
 
The Locarnese region is located in the southern foothills of the European Alps in the Swiss-Italian 
Canton of Ticino. In broad terms, the local climate is defined as Warm Temperate, with warm, wet 
summers and cool to mild winters, but is also characterised by highly variable temperatures and 
precipitation (Spinedi and Isotta 2004; MeteoSwiss 2012). The area averages 1457 millimetres of 
annual rainfall and has the highest average temperatures for the country. The slopes of the 


PAGE 10
9 
 
Locarnese region are covered in a mixed-forest, dominated at lower altitudes by sweet chestnut 
(Castanea sativa), a tree rich with cultural significance and a forest-type that is traditionally more 
likely to burn during the late-winter dormancy phase (Bajocco et al. 2011). 
 
Palaeoecological studies provide evidence of frequent wildfires in southern Switzerland since pre-
historic times (Tinner et al. 1999). The forests of Canton Ticino were severely depleted in the 
nineteenth century, leading to slopes denuded of trees (Pezzatti et al. 2013). Replanting activities at 
the turn of the twentieth century and forest expansion following the post-WWII abandonment of 
marginal agricultural areas has led to a significative increase in forest area (Bettelini 2007; Loran et 
al. 2017). With more forest there has been an associated increase in wildfire events, but that trend 
was successfully reversed through policy interventions, such as the 1987 Cantonal Executive Decree 
banning private burn offs, and increased investment into fire-fighting infrastructure (Conedera et al. 
2004; Sezione Forestale 2008; Pezzatti et al. 2013). Now however, wildfire risk is projected to 
increase with a changing climate, forest conditions and settlement patterns (Zumbrunnen et al. 
2011; Pezzatti et al. 2016), and the Federal Office for the Environment (FOEN) has called for Cantons 
to have a greater consideration of forest fire mitigation strategies (FOEN 2016, p.39).  
 
Long-range climate forecasts suggest that Canton Ticino will experience an increase in the number of 
hot days and higher median temperatures across all seasons, while summer precipitation is 
projected to decrease and winter precipitation to increase (Spinedi and Isotta, 2004; MeteoSwiss 
2012; Moser and Del Priore, 2013; FOEN 2017). Over the last thirty years, temperatures in Ticino 
have risen at a rate almost double the median global average (MeteoSwiss 2012). Already, over the 
period 1880 to 2005, the length of summer heatwaves over Western Europe, including Switzerland, 
has doubled on average and the frequency of hot days has almost tripled (Brönnimann et al. 2014). 
Although rainfall has always been variable, an increase in the number of dry spells has become 
apparent since the 1970s (Rebetez 1999; Reinhard et al. 2005). This tendency, along with the overall 
warming trend in the Alpine region, is partly why concerns are being raised that environmental 
conditions are becoming more favourable for dangerous wildfires and other hazards (Rebetez 1999; 
Adger 2006; Wastl et al. 2013; Pezzatti et al. 2016; Bottinelli 2017; Matasci et al. 2018). Those 
changes have the potential to adversely affect the protective and recreational functions of the forest 
and increase infrastructure damage (FOEN 2014; IFEC Ingegneria SA et al. 2016).  
 
Concern about the likely impacts of droughts and wildfires in Ticino continue to grow amongst the 
scientific community, especially as they might generate risks with which people are less familiar 

PAGE 11
10 
 
(Reinhard et al. 2005; Rigling and Schaffer 2015; PLANAT 2018). Some of the most extensive wildfires 
experienced in Switzerland occurred during the 2016-2017 winter in Ticino and neighbouring Grisons 
(Pezzatti et al. 2017; Degrussa 2018). Although there was no loss of human life, the significant 
destruction of forests established to protect settlements, exposed communities and infrastructure 
to avalanches, landslides and debris flows in disturbed landscapes (Conedera et al. 2003; Alpine 
Initiative 2018). As risks increase, the conception of wildfires in the region has the potential to 
change from a regular, although manageable issue, to a situation where new, more extreme forms 
of the hazard will become apparent (Henne Bigalke et al. 2018). Reinhard et al. (2005, p.8) conclude 
for Switzerland, ‘Decision makers have to deal with more severe framework conditions than twenty 
or thirty years ago and should implement appropriate measures’. To do so, more specific knowledge 
is required on how authorities can draw from local knowledge to mitigate risk. 
 
5. Method 
 
Both quantitative and qualitative data on residents’ perceptions in the Locarnese area were 
generated through a residential household survey undertaken in October-December 2015. The 
survey was conducted to identify perceptions of environmental values, as well as attitudes towards 
the local planning system’s management of those values and risks. Participants were sampled using 
stratified selection criteria (Matthews and Ross 2014; Etikan et al. 2016), including: primary 
residence; proximity to naturally vegetated areas; and, exposure to geophysical hazards associated 
with steep slopes (Figure 2). Addresses were obtained from the Population and Migration Office of 
Canton Ticino, which listed addresses by a ‘head of household’ criteria, and in most cases, identified 
an adult male as the household representative. To attempt to balance a potential gender bias, 
where multiple residents appeared under the same address, or where two individuals (one male and 
one female) were listed as heads of household for an address entry, a female addressee was 
selected. Households included within the village cores or ‘nuclei’, of the six municipalities were 
excluded from the sample due to lower levels of environmental risk.  
The survey contained Likert-scale, nominal binary (yes, no) and open-ended questions to produce 
both quantitative and qualitative data. Key questions asked about residents’:  
• gender, age, household typology, nationality, place of birth, employment and education, 
home (length of stay, previous place of residence, factors influential in the choice of location 
and on possible relocation, location in relation to forest and green spaces); 
• location (most and least appreciated aspects, attachment levels); 
• perceptions of forest management and biodiversity conservation; 

PAGE 12
11 
 
• perceptions of environmental risk factors and risk management; 
• actions and behaviour to prepare for environmental hazards and protect the environment;  
• perceptions of the efficacy of agencies responsible for fire-fighting; 
• opinions about and knowledge of the planning system; and 
• satisfaction levels with planning decisions and engagement processes. 
 
To optimise coverage in this multi-lingual context, a mixed-mode multi-language survey was applied. 
The paper questionnaire was mailed to residents in the main local language, Italian. However, links 
to online Survey Monkey versions of the questionnaire were offered in both German and English. 
Surveys were posted through the Swiss postal service in October 2015 to 900 households, with 
reminder postcards sent out in late November 2015. A total of 146 valid responses were received 
(paper surveys 124 and online surveys 22), providing a response rate of 16 percent. That response 
rate is in line with similar surveys conducted in Switzerland ranging from 15 percent (Home et al. 
2014; Maidl et al. 2021) to 30-35 percent (Conedera et al. 2015; Frick et al. 2015). While the 
response rate is low and the results here are indicative only, they provide important insights into the 
ways that the Swiss respondents perceive of their environment and planning systems. 
 
To guide the data analysis, responses were organised into three themes linked to the broader 
research objectives: conceptions of place and environment; attitudes to risk; and the attitudes 
towards the planning system. The strength of associations between the variables within the three 
thematic groups were analysed using Pearson Chi-Square (χ²) and Fishers Exact Tests (FET) in IBM 
SPSS (version 22). While a large body of information was generated from the survey, specific data 
are chosen and presented here to develop narratives on perceptions of environment and 
management. In association, qualitative data generated through open-ended questions in the survey 
provide greater depth of insight into the perceptions of respondents, and quotations are used to 
provide in-text support for key arguments. 
 
6. Results 
6.1 Characteristics of survey respondents 
 
A total of 146 valid surveys were received, although not all questions were answered in each case 
(Table 1). Of those respondents who answered, 90 percent indicated that they held Swiss citizenship 
and more than half (54.8%) were born in Canton Ticino, with nearly a third (31.9%) stating another 
Swiss canton as their birthplace and the remainder (13.3%) born abroad. Over one third of

respondents (37%) had lived at their current address for 21 years or longer; 25 percent had lived at 
the address for 11-20 years; and 38 percent had moved into the area in the last 10 years, thus 
revealing a relatively sedentary sample. This permanency could imply a good knowledge of the 
location and associated environmental risks. Yet, it may also infer old age and perhaps a greater 
sense of vulnerability, an assumption supported by the finding that 43 percent of respondents were 
aged 65 years or more. That cohort are also likely to be less-affluent than the broader population 
and dependent on the aged-pension (Swiss Life 2015). Nevertheless, cantonal data for the surveyed 
municipalities reveal an average of 56 percent of all residents aged from 18 to 64 years - very close 
to the 57 percent in that age bracket within the sample (USTAT 2016). Therefore, while the response 
rate is quite low, it is relatively representative in terms of age. Some 65.8 percent of the total 146 
participants were male and 34.2 percent female, whereas data for the six municipalities show a 
slight discrepancy, with a predominance of females (53.1%) over males (46.9%) (USTAT 2016).

Respondents were asked why they chose to live in their current location. The standout motivations 
were peace and quiet (85%), the view (80%); and proximity to nature (80%). Other important factors 
included: relaxed lifestyle (77%); clean air (74%); escape from urban life (70%); and a good 
environment to bring up children (67%). These results highlight the ’Swiss sunroom‘ status of the 
Locarnese region and help to explain the attraction of its mild climate and environment. Some 94 
percent of all respondents agreed with the statement that forests significantly contributed to the 
beauty of regional landscapes.  
Usage of recreational areas (green spaces, forests, parks) was very high among respondents, with 71 
percent using them at least once a week. Only approximately one-third of respondents indicated 
proximity to work and transport/services as being important in their location decisions. While 
people were concerned about the degradation of the environment and urban development, factors 
impacted by climate change such as wildfires did not rate highly amongst respondents in relation to 
other factors (Figure 3).

7. Discussion 
 
A high percentage (79%) of respondents felt they have ‘no voice’ in environmental planning 
outcomes concerning their local municipality/canton. This result was surprising, as the resident 
population of the study area have had numerous opportunities to participate in decision-making 
processes, and they are constantly being supported to learn about contemporary issues (Stanga 
2017). Such a strong negative outcome could indicate a degree of despondency by respondents, who 
are expressing attitudes that might be expected in a population that views the democratic process as 
a mere ‘window-dressing’ exercise rather than a truly deliberative and representative process. 
Questions arise over the effectiveness of a political system in which people get to express their 
opinions and participate to an unparalleled degree, but feel that their participation does not 
influence outcomes (De’Arman 2020). 
 
Survey results reveal a mismatch between respondents’ perceptions of landscape amenity and 
ecological values, and their attitudes towards local risk mitigation approaches. On one hand there is 
a very high value attributed to the forested landscape and a wish for more trees and conservation 
(see also Conedera et al. 2015), but on the other there appears to be considerable support for 
clearing trees to immediately reduce risk. That situation, which is not unique to Ticino (Kalhor et al. 
2018; Weber et al. 2019), suggests diverging priorities that may be impossible to reconcile fully 
within the one space. At the same time, current environmental management approaches could be 
creating a false sense of security in the population even as risks increase, while having the effect of 
‘losing the public’ who clearly desire stricter conservation measures (Buchecker et al. 2003). Such an 
erosion of institutional trust (see Parkins and Mitchell 2005), seems to relate especially to 
immigrants from other Swiss cantons who often do not speak Italian and are more likely to 
misunderstand local risks, and as a result, may be particularly ignorant of the need for targeted 
interventions.  
 
Even though we have analysed only a small sample, the results suggest that many decisions relating 
to planning for residents on the wildland-urban interface around Locarno do not fully incorporate 
local concerns – a result which appears to contrast with outcomes at the national level. Swiss 
planning generally enjoys great respect and ownership amongst the country’s citizens, and partly as 
a result, democratic processes are granted higher political standing than in other countries (Kriesi 
and Trechsel 2008; Lendi 2012). The real strength of Swiss planning derives from its willingness to 
engage, anticipate potential problems and develop appropriate technical solutions in a process 
supported through the Swiss federal governance system (Frey 1994; Muggli 2013; Auer et al. 2014). 

PAGE 22
21 
 
However, the results here would suggest that despite its clear merits, the system also generates 
potential procedural pitfalls for managing increasing natural hazards and urban expansion (Reinhard 
et al. 2005; Price et al. 2015). In this case, respondents may just be using the survey to voice their 
local grievances, but there is also the possibility that a perceived lack of citizen influence over 
environmental planning may originate from the fact that political, economic and technical experts 
increasingly dominate the Swiss policy agenda (Hersperger et al. 2014; Kruse and Pütz 2014). That is 
an issue affecting many democracies (Turner 2001), but in the Swiss case, the relative ease offered 
by popular referenda may be creating a disproportionate sense of citizen entitlement to be heard on 
local planning issues. Perhaps citizens’ expectations are raised too high by the awareness that in 
such a robust democracy they should have a voice in all major decisions that affect them personally 
and collectively.  
 
The highly deliberative Swiss system generates unique opportunities for citizen engagement, but 
simultaneously, institutional risks are created through an emphasis on the importance of 
participation (Stern and Baird 2015; Legacy 2017). The legitimacy of political engagement could 
erode if particular groups, such as many of the younger respondents from the survey in Ticino, do 
not see value in their involvement (see also Forester 1999). Although universal voting is not the aim 
of the Swiss deliberative democratic system, average participation rates in votes vary considerably. 
In two recent nation-wide examples, a referendum in 2018 saw participation reach 54 percent, while 
another in June 2018 drew only 25 percent of voters (FSO 2017; 2018). Participation for cantonal 
voting rarely exceeds 45 percent, with a 2017 vote on changes to the environmental protection law 
achieving 42 percent participation, while an earlier initiative to build a tunnel for a ring road 
attracting 36 percent of eligible voters (Repubblica e Cantone Ticino 2021). Dryzek (2009) argues 
that societies will need to avoid ‘missing’ or ‘overhearing’ the opinions of more vulnerable groups or, 
for that matter ‘losing’ groups that perceive that their involvement is meaningless if they wish to 
maintain effective democratic governance systems. In a society increasingly defined by risk (Beck 
1986), that raises important questions about whether the deliberative planning processes will need 
to evolve to maintain its social licence.  
 
Local planning in peri-urban spaces, such as the sample area, will need to increasingly incorporate 
the complex implications of environmental risk (Gallent and Shaw 2007; Von der Dunk et al. 2011; 
Nilsson et al. 2013). Within a deliberative democracy, the evolving situation must be understood by 
residents so they make an informed vote for their preferred outcomes. However, our results support 
a wider concern within the Swiss government (FOEN 2016) that residents are not fully 

PAGE 23
22 
 
acknowledging that new risks are becoming more important with environmental change and so are 
not demanding changes in planning outcomes. That challenge is compounded by new levels of 
societal complexity. Particular groups within society, and especially those more vulnerable due to 
socio-economic or demographic circumstances, can miss out on opportunities to learn about risk 
(Fung and Wright 2003; Eriksen and Simon 2017). A specific planning focus on the wildland-urban 
interface, areas where socio-ecological risk will concentrate, could be one way to manage the 
unique, complex entanglement of urban growth, natural hazards and environmental values (Paveglio 
et al. 2009; Bardsley et al. 2015; Llausàs et al. 2016; Keller et al. 2020). Developing knowledge of 
residents’ values and priorities for the management of such areas, while also educating about the 
new risk conditions, could be a vital step to informing such a planning approach.  
 
Consultation does not necessarily imply deliberation (Dryzek 2000; Johnston 2014). Beck (2016) 
warns of the need for an evolution in democracy, as he asks how much environmental risk our 
democratic institutions can withstand before their basic tenets start to be undermined. As the public 
is not always keen to participate in democratic processes (Brady and Webb 2013), deliberative 
frameworks must be applied appropriately and be made attractive enough to encourage residents to 
engage by firstly ensuring that their actions will be meaningful, and secondly creating deliberative 
settings that are comfortable and facilitate exchange, communication and learning (Feng et al. 
2018). It is vital that the challenge of emerging risks can be explained to society, so that people 
realise that responses to risk are difficult and expensive, and moreover, that there will always be 
residual risks that cannot be eliminated. The Swiss have the advantage of a traditional cultural 
heritage of risk management (Pfister 2009), where there is already trust in expert opinion, processes 
in collaborative learning, and an emphasis on place-based planning, seen as essential components of 
an effective approach (Champ et al. 2012; Wachinger et al. 2013). A middle path, where planning is 
open to diverse viewpoints and perspectives, but also capable of efficient and decisive action linked 
to scientific knowledge and technical expertise, has the potential to continue to generate a unique 
blend of both the instrumental and participatory planning approaches (Dryzek 1990; Allmendinger 
2009; Inch 2015; Innes and Booher 2016). In doing so, a dialogue of risk reduction amongst the 
population, technical authorities and other government actors could be normalised, and a risk 
culture created and/or strengthened to enable sophisticated planning decisions (Hulme 2008).

8. Conclusion 
 
Respondents from the upper slopes of the peri-urban area around Locarno highlighted a very strong 
attachment to their local forests. Yet, many elements are changing rapidly, including demographic 
and settlement patterns, forests and climate, which all contribute to changing levels of risk. Many 
respondents, and especially those originating from outside of Ticino, are not recognising the new 
levels of environmental risk, and seem to be more satisfied with current planning outcomes as a 
result. New forms of planning for risk would recognise that some decisions will need to mirror the 
perceptions of local residents, while others would continue to rely largely upon the technical 
understandings of risk. Such a comprehensive deliberative process would encourage residents of 
areas with high levels of environmental risk to engage more fully with relevant topics for their 
region. In turn, this has the potential to strengthen community ties. This is what Habermas (1984) 
refers to as the social room for public interaction, which is a crucial aspect in fostering positive risk 
mitigation behaviours across communities. However, local planning processes do not stand in a 
social and institutional vacuum; they must be considered within existing forms of democracy and 
norms of behaviour. One of the big unresolved questions in this field lies in the identification of 
mechanisms capable of translating the theory of deliberative planning into successful public 
participation. It may be that best practice deliberative planning will involve unique local 
combinations of participatory and technical inputs in decision-making at different levels of 
governance. 
 
The Swiss democratic process creates knowledge and ownership of the planning system, thus 
resulting in degrees of approval in government rarely seen elsewhere. The success of that 
normalised process is reflected in the survey results, where 88 percent claim to have at least some 
knowledge of the cantonal planning system. The overwhelming satisfaction with fire-fighting 
authorities articulated in the survey also implies trust in the service that the government is 
providing. Yet, people have very high expectations of the planning system and most do not believe 
that their voices are being heard. As environmental risks become personal through impacts at local 
levels, the societal inability to enable democratic influences over planning outcomes appears to be 
generating challenges for governance. This article highlights the need for experimentation in this 
space to learn how to achieve better, real outcomes for society. In this case, even best-practice 
Swiss democratic processes struggle to guide knowledge and generate decisions that meet complex 
local needs and aspirations. As any reforms are initiated to respond to new levels of environmental 
risk, there will be limitations and failures and no-one has all the answers, so successful engagement 

PAGE 25
24 
 
must be tied to learning processes. Perhaps the real finding is that a new level of uncertainty in 
relation to environmental risk must be made clear to all.
